# Thoughts
- Akerman conveys her thesis through the pacing of this film: Ritualize the slowness and routines of your life. Be present. (*No reading at the dinner table*, Dielman commands her son Sylvain). She doesn't shy away from long shots and repetitive actions -- it's purposeful. She let the bath scene finish from beginning to end! The camera stays fixed - the only movement comes from Jeanne Dielman.
- This film reminds me of some of the short films I've done - *Birthday Bash* and *Antidote*.
- While watching this, there's so many moments where I can hear a film professor in the back of my mind saying *cut here! cut here! you don't need this shot. add some sound. add some music.* Akerman shows Dielman walking back and forth between rooms. Walking through hallways. There's silence. There's minimal dialogue. Akerman doesn't submit to these nagging commands. She takes up our time and space, unabashedly. It's a meditative viewing experience and really makes us reflect on how we, as individuals, live our lives in the moment.
- Akerman has a mastery of control in this film. She knows what she wants to show the viewers and what she doesn't want to show the viewers. Akerman shows Dielman's slow rituals. Akerman doesn't show what Dielman does with Caller \#1. 
- During the letter-reading scene, I couldn't help but think of *News From Home* -- clearly her mother's letters influenced her filmmaking. The letter reads as if it comes from Akerman's own mother.
- Dielman and her son (Sylvain) read *The Enemy* by Charles Baudelaire twice (the full run) like a prayer.
- ~2h51m left of the film, music comes in: Fur Elise while Dielman knits away and reads a paper.
- Day 1 ends ~45min
- This is how some people live their lives. That's pretty much what this film is. Reminding us we're human.
- This is hyperrealism - the way sadness kicks in. The way Dielman realizes she isn't emotionally fulfilled in her life at all. The way she only has her little tasks to complete. It builds up -- slowly at first, then all at once.
- Every frame feels like an Edward Hopper painting.
- I like Akerman's dialogues (maybe it's cuz it sounds a bit better in French, idk) -- it gives me an insight to her mind. I like her thoughts.
- It's that scene where she's alone in the cafe and she just goes :(

- The conversations Jeanne has with Sylvain before going to bed are interesting. Really viewing and discussing masculinity vs femininity and sexuality and love and intimacy.
