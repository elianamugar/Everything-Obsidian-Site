# thoughts
Also known as "Un homme et une femme" in French, this was a film made during the French New Wave (specifically in 1966) by Claude Lelouch. The runtime is was reasonable at around 1h43m, but some parts felt like it was dragging. AFAIK, the film won Best Foreign Film at the Oscars in 1967 and Anouk Aimée won Best Actress that year as well. 

The general plot is a man (Jean-Louis Duroc, played by Jean-Louis Trintignant) falls in love with a woman (Anne Gauthier, played by Anouk Aimée). They met through dropping off their kids to the same school. Jean, a race car driver, happens to be driving into Paris as Anne, a script girl, misses her train into Paris (which is a common trait of hers). Through their time hanging out with each other, Jean learns that Anne's husband (a stuntmant) died during a dangerous stunt and she is still very much in love with him, despite him being dead. Later on, Anne learns that Jean's wife committed suicide while there was a 3-hour operation to revive Jean from a car accident on the race tracks. Lots of love in the air, I suppose!

I first heard of the film through record shopping at Goodwill. The bright pink cover of the vinyl soundtrack caught my eye and I bought it. I haven't listened to since I bought it, but the soundtrack was so spectacular that it made that purchase several years ago much worth it. Now, I can plug it into my record player and enjoy the vibes.

The editing in this was spectacular, in normal French New Wave fashion. It cuts similar to how modern films somewhat do (re: Anora by [[Sean Baker]]), where the cuts seem jarring and abrupt, but it's personally a style that I enjoy when it works well. Here, it does. When the main characters (Anne Gauthier and Jean-Louis Duroc) talk about their tragic pasts regarding their spouses, the scene cuts right to depicting what happened to them, rather than cross-fading into a flashback or talking about the tragic events with intercutting. It was very much an era of *show* more than *tell*.

I loved the cinematography of it. I read somewhere (I think it was in a Letterboxd review) that Lelouch meticulously used/tried different film stocks and used as much of them as he could. The colors in the stock were glorious! He used black and white, sepia, and color stock (I'm not sure on the specifics), and it made the viewing experience much more enjoyable.

Also, in regular French New Wave fashion, there were lots of voiceovers of inner dialogue, which I personally don't seem to mind since it sounds better in French anyway. I think it gets cheesy when it's in American English and it's someone trying to *emulate* the style, rather than use the style purposefully. I will say during Anne and Jean-Louis's dialogue portions, I couldn't look away from the screen. The dialogue felt so natural and realistic, which is something I've kind of missed from modern films. The closest I've seen to it is maybe mumblecore? But there's something so performative about mumblecore that I don't really enjoy... so I don't know. Maybe that's something I have to look into.
