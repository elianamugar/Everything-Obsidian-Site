# Quotes
### 155
“Do you realize that the past, starting from yesterday, has been actually abolished?... Nothing exists except an endless present in which the Party is always right. I *know*, of course, that the past is falsified, but it would never be possible for me to prove it, even when I did the falsification myself.”

Winston is still insane, being a minority of one, who *knows* the past is false but can’t do anything about it. Simply *knowing* that the past is false seems to satisfy Winston, trying to stay away from becoming anything like the rest of the population who’ve ultimately been submissive to the governmental rule. As long as Winston had his own truth, that was enough for him—he just doesn’t want to conform to whatever the government says to believe.

### 167
“But if the object was not to stay alive but to stay human, what difference did it ultimately make? \[The government] could not alter your feelings; for that matter you could not alter them yourself, even if you wanted to.”

Ultimately, the question of what it means to be human is a thought that only an “insane” person would have within the society of Oceania. The thought that Winston concedes was that they weren’t humans because no one could change their feelings. Staying alive and staying human does not coincide anymore within the society of Oceania because all of them are able bodies but not with a mind of their own. The society is “sane” in a sense that they are the masses, but to us readers they are “insane” because of their unconventional way of thought.

### 180-1
It’s announced at Hate Week that Oceania was at war with Eastasia and not with Eurasia, which everyone had previously thought all along.

The government can control the sanity of the people by having them switch, in an instant, what they should believe and what they should not believe. A lot like the Mandela Effect, all the people thought one thing originally, because that was what the government previously said, and now it’s changed and everyone HAS to follow what the government has claimed it to be. It’s an insane situation that the government can fix the sanity of the people.

### 198
“War was a sure safeguard of sanity, and so far as the ruling classes were concerned it was probably the most important of all safeguards.”

War kept in check the sanity of the people because whoever ruled would have control over the people’s sanity into what they believed and what they shouldn’t believe. With war comes fear, and with the constant state of fear, the people have to be subjected to whatever the higher authority tells them too because they’ve become so cowered and “under the protection” of the government that they aren’t motivated to do anything else otherwise. People are just “going along with the flow” of the government, putting no thought into it and certainly not putting any thought about their own sanity because their sanity is not their own.

### 216
“If human equality is to be forever averted—if the High, as we have called them, are to keep their places permanently—then the prevailing mental condition must be controlled insanity.”

Goldstein’s significance in writing this explains *how* Oceania’s government carries out their “controlled insanity” of the people. Although Winston believes he is insane against the “sane” masses, Goldstein writes from the neutral and “normal” perspective of the reader in saying that everything about the situation in Oceania is insane.

### 217
“But after reading \[the book] he knew better than before that he was not mad. Being in a minority, even a minority of one, did not make you mad. There was truth and there was untruth, and if you clung to the truth even against the whole world you were not mad.”

After reading Goldstein’s book, Winston is satisfied that he is still a “minority of one” or, in other words, still a lunatic. He’s referred to himself as a minority of one previously in *1984*, but now it’s coming to light that this is exactly what Winston wants—except for the fact that Winston will not be what he wants due to the situation in Oceania. The purpose of Orwell was to warn the readers about something, and to convey this, *1984* must not have a happy ending. Therefore, Winston will perhaps fall back to the normal tendencies of the rest of the society.

### 218
“He was safe, everything was all right. He fell asleep murmuring ‘Sanity is not statistical,’ with the feeling that this remark contained in it a profound wisdom.”

To be a “minority of one” is technically a statistic. Being one in the crowd of the population is a statistic because one only knows they are “sane” amongst the rest of the “insane.” But such a statistic, being one out of the rest of the people, is “insane.” That one person cannot change the minds of the rest of the people, so this whole situation is statistically insane.

### 220
“...that must be Goldstein’s final message. The future belonged to the proles. And could \[Winston] be sure that when their time came, the world they constructed would not be just as alien to him, Winston Smith, as the World of the Party? Yes, because at the least it would be a world of sanity. Where there is equality there can be sanity.”

Winston hopes for a world of equality, where everyone is their own people and their sanity belongs to themselves. That’s all Winston’s ever wanted, a world of sanity where he’s not alone in his thought and where individual thought is normal. Yet, this dream will not come true in the end.

### 245
“‘\[Winston you] have known it for years, though you have fought against the knowledge. You are mentally deranged.’”

O’Brien tries to convince Winston that he is not sane because he doesn’t follow the way the government wants the people to think. Winston is a lunatic, that is what O’Brien tries to instill in him. O’Brien has to overcome a strong mental factor of Winston that Winston wants to be different, special even, in being the minority of one. Winston will come to realize that there is no use in fighting back, because you can’t fight the Party. It is simply impossible and an insane idea.

### 247
“...it was perfectly possible that O’Brien had really forgotten the photograph…. How could one be sure that it was simply trickery? Perhaps that lunatic dislocation in the mind could really happen: that was the thought that defeated him.”

Winston is starting to get hammered down by O’Brien’s interrogation. Winston’s mind is starting to give into the fact that he is NOT a minority of one. The “lunatic dislocation in the mind” is what Winston’s feared all along—that it was easily possible the Party could implant some idea in his head and he WOULD believe it. Orwell tries to tell us readers that it’s very possible that some higher authority would take advantage of humanity’s vulnerability for their own cause and our situation could end up being like Winston.

### 249
“‘\[Winston you] are here because you have failed in humility, in self-discipline. You would not make the act of submission which is the price of sanity. You preferred to be a lunatic, a minority of one. You believe that reality is something objective, external, existing in its own right. You also believe that the nature of reality is self-evident…. Whatever the Party holds to be truth is truth…. You must humble yourself before you can become sane.’”

To submit to the government is to be sane, that is what O’Brien is saying. As Goldstein said, the ruling class functions on “controlled insanity,” but here, O’Brien is saying that the control of the Party is “sane.” Becoming part of the masses is to be “sane,” but this is exactly what Winston does not want. Yet, Winston must submit because the Party is not going to give up on Winston. They need him to conform to the rest of the people, and to, most importantly, stop thinking.

### 251
“‘It is not easy to become sane.’”

O’Brien concedes to the fact that to be sane, you have to stop thinking. To stop thinking is simply impossible, seen by the fact that Oceania allows the word *doublethink* to exist. But if it’s not easy to become sane, how could such a government actually control people? I assume this was one of the questions that Orwell explores in the bigger picture of humanity. Orwell is saying that individual thought takes precedence over government control, specifically authoritative and totalitarian governments.

### 253
“‘Shall I tell you why we have brought you here? To cure you! To make you sane! Will you understand, Winston, that no one whom we bring to this place ever leaves our hands uncured?’”

This reiterates the idea that the Party’s goal is to control the people for the benefit of a war economy, a constant loop that can’t be broken. To be sane is difficult for the individual, but for the Party, it was not difficult for them to make the people “sane.”

### 256
“There was no idea that he had ever had, or could have, that O’Brien had not long ago known, examined, and rejected. His mind contained Winston’s mind. But in that case how could it be true that O’Brien was mad? It must be he, Winston, who was mad.”

Winston is starting to falter on his logic, saying that he is mad but with a negative connotation. Winston starts to see that maybe he is mad because he doesn’t fit in with the rest of the society, which goes against his ideas of what “sanity” is anymore. He believes he is insane because he can’t fit in with the general population and we see now that he accepts his insanity and lunacy in trying to keep his individuality. In the end, he must concede to O’Brien because Orwell’s ultimate message is that the doom of humanity could happen under such controlling regimes and conditions.

### 259
“‘Your mind appeals to me. It resembles my own mind except that \[you Winston] happen to be insane.’”

O’Brien reminds me of the word *doublethink* because he just explained it here. O’Brien knows that he thinks “more” than the general population, but instead of keeping it to himself, he uses it against others. Knowing what Winston thinks, O’Brien can read his mind and even convince him of other things simply because O’Brien is relatable. If Winston believes he and O’Brien thinks the same things, then why shouldn’t Winston believe O’Brien if O’Brien has gotten everything right about Winston’s thoughts so far? It’s only logical that Winston will submit to the Party’s ideals eventually.

### 261
“‘Do you remember writing in your diary, ‘I understand how; I do not understand why’? It was when you thought about ‘why’ that you doubted your own sanity.’”

By doubting your own sanity, O’Brien alternatively means that Winston started to challenge the ideals of the Party. I see now that “sanity” and “Party’s ideals” are synonyms of each other to ultimately lead to the fact that the Party controls sanity.

### 286
During Winston’s torture scene with the rats, Orwell wrote, “For an instant he was insane, a screaming animal.”

This is when Winston submits to the Party’s ideals and understands that being insane isn’t as great as he thought it would be if he had to be subjected to torture of his greatest fear. Winston has prolonged the mind games and interrogations of O’Brien that really made Winston question his own reality and his own role in the Party’s regime. This is the ultimate end of Winston, in which Orwell seems to be saying that under such oppression, distress, cruelty, and all other things that have happened during and as a result of both World Wars, that such stiflement of the individual can lead to the downfall of decent humanity.
