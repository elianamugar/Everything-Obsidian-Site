#### 10.1.0 Positivism
- [[Positivism]]: a commitment to a unified view of science, the belief that it's possible to adapt the [[Epistemology]] and [[Methodologies]] of the natural sciences to explain the social world (including IR)
	- believes objective knowledge of the world is possible
		- faith and commitment to 18th c. [[Enlightenment]]'s [[rationalism]] tradition
- René [[Descartes]] (1596 – 1650)
	- [[rationalism]], contribution to mathematics and rational bases of modern science
- debate between [[rationalism]] which emphasized logic to be found in [[deduction]] and *empiricists* who made inferences of [[induction]] from what they observed
	- resolved in [[rational-empirical]] synthesis that remains core to [[Positivism]], a "scientific" approach to knowing
- [[David Hume]] (1711 – 1776)
	- objects to causal inferences being drawn to readily
		- causality is itself not directly observable but a construct used by humans to make what they observe around them understandable/predictable
	- causality is no more than an inference humans draw from the conjunction of impressions about the things we observe
		- e.g. when we perceive some factor/event (X) precedes another (Y), our minds may think X caused Y
- [[John Stuart Mill]] (1806 – 1873)
	- consistent with [[David Hume]]
	- influenced by [[Positivism]] of [[France]] philosopher [[August Comte]] (1798 – 1857)
	- developed formal canons of [[induction]], allowing both natural and social scientists to arrive at causal truth claims by applying systematically one or another series of specified tests/methods to observed phenomena
