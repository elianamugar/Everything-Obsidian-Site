#### 5.4.0 System
- *system*
	- not all [[Realism]] portrays relations among states in systemic terms
	- some [[neorealism]]/[[structural realism]] do
	- *system* has currency within each of the images
		- [[Realism]], [[Liberalism]], [[Economic Structuralism]], [[The English School]]
- *system*: the set of interactions among states
	- positivist behavioral methodology
	- dominant approach in 1960s – 1970x
	- efforts to count, track, code interactions among states in hopes of identifying patterns of conflict and cooperation
	- *International Studies Quarterly* – journal
		- publishes research in this tradition
		- emphasizes studies that try to draw meaning from aggregate numbers and data sets that are amenable to probability and statistical analyses, causal modeling, and development of mathematical equations that capture what we observe in relations among states
- [[neorealism]]/[[structural realism]] scholars argument: counting interactions provided limited insights to [[IR]]
	- more useful starting point: various distributions of power/capabilities among states
		- [[unipolar]], [[bipolar distribution of power]], [[multipolar]]
- polarity of system measured by *number of major powers*
	- different polarities will have different effects on [[IR]], including interactions among states
- uses of *system* concept
	- taxonomies/frameworks for organizing knowledge about [[IR]]
		- can speak of the int'l political system, int'l economic system, or int'l social system
	- mental images that may help describe int'l phenomena
		- systems are, in effect, superimposed on the real world by a scholar to make the real world more intelligible/somewhat easier to understand
	- explains and predicts outcomes of [[IR]]
		- process of theory building
			- ascribe to systems properties of *equilibrium* (balance) of their component parts (e.g. among states)
		- critics: find little use in balancing tendencies allegedly found in a system of states
			- treating a system as if it were concrete/tangible entity and ascribing properties to it seems of questionable validity from this POV
			- guilty of methodological error of *reification* (treating abstractions as if they're real and have a life of their own)
			- dealing with abstractions is useful in creating propositions/hypotheses about [[IR]]
				- can be tested empirically to determine if they have factual support
				- enables theorists to describe, explain, predict int'l phenomena
- *systems* concept as an approximation to nature of world politics
	- present within mainstream of contemporary [[Realism]]
	- some [[classical realism]] scholars avoid its use
- systems as an abstract concept
	- will discuss substance to this concept
	- will discuss how the concept of *system* has been used by some [[Realism]] scholars
		- *system* as [[anarchy]] plus the distribution of capabilities
	- scholars intend to explain some aspect of [[IR]] on matters of instability, conflict, [[War]]
	- [[Realism]] assumptions: state and state power are key foci of analysis and investigation
		- also key focus: analytical assumption of [[rationalism]]
#### 5.4.1 Game Theory and Anarchy
- [[Game Theory]]: approach to determining rational choice or optimum strategy in a competitive situation
	- each actor tries to maximize gains/minimize losses under conditions of uncertainty and incomplete information
		- requires actors to rank order preferences, estimate probabilities, predict other actor's actions
	- two person zero-sum game
		- one competitor wins, the other loses
	- two-person non-zero-sum/variable sum game
		- gains and losses aren't necessarily equal
		- possibly both sides may gain
		- also known as **[[positive-sum]] game**
	- n-person games
		- some games, both parties can lose and by different amounts/to a different degree
		- includes more than 2 actors/sides
	- contributed to development of models of [[deterrence theory]] and arms race spirals
	- basis for how collaboration among competitive states can be achieved
		- central problem: rational decision for an individual actor (state) may be to "defect" and go it alone rather than collaborating with another state actor
- [[Realism]] on [[Game Theory]]
	- highly relevant to understanding [[IR]]
		- realist emphasis on conditions of [[anarchy]]
		- realist emphasis on distribution of capabilities/power among states
	- system-relevant structural attributes
		- crucial b/c they act as constraints on decision makers
	- condition of int'l [[anarchy]] viewed by realists as contributing to amount of distrust and conflict among states
	- concern of particular distributions of capabilities involving various [[balance of power]] makes [[War]] between states more or less likely
- [[Realism]]'s "[[anarchy]]"
	- absence of any legitimate authority above states
	- states are sovereign
		- external: claim a right to be independent/autonomous from other states
		- internal/domestic: claim a right to exercise complete authority over their territories
	- states differ in terms of power they possess/can exercise
	- no states can claim the right to dominate another sovereign state
	- [[Realism]] distinguishes between authority and power
		- remember, for realists, anarchy is absence of any hierarchy of legitimate authority in int'l system
		- there *is* hierarchy of *power* in int'l politics, but no hierarchy of *authority*
		- some states more powerful than others, but no recognized authority higher than any state
	- [[anarchy]] is the defining characteristic of the environment within which sovereign states interact
		- violence and war may be evident
		- periods of relative peace and stability also relevant
		- absence of superordinate/central authority over states
			- aka world gov't with authority to enforce rules and to maintain order
			- different from domestic societies
				- authority exists to maintain order and act as arbiter of disputes
					- except in cases of total gov't collapse/civil wars when legitimate authority is unclear
	- [[Realism]]'s argument: absence of a central/overriding authority helps explain why states:
		- come to rely on power
		- seek to maintain/increase their power positions relative to other states
	- condition of anarchy accompanied by distrust among states in this environment
		- each state faces [[self-help system]] situation
			- dangerous to place security of one's own country in hands of another
		- consistent with [[Hobbes]]'s world
			- nothing to keep an "ally" from reneging on a security agreement or any other int'l pact
		- no gov'tal authority to enforce covenants/agreements among states
		- logical to look out for top priority: security of one's own state
			- similar to [[Thucydides]]: [[Athens]] advice to Melia not to place hope in survival in hands of [[Sparta]]
- [[security dilemma]]
	- int'l [[anarchy]] and distrust in this situation
	- states have this dilemma
		- one state arms to protect itself from other states
		- other states become threatened
		- other states resort to arm themselves to protect *their* national security interests
	- dilemma: if a state sincerely arms for only *defensive* purposes, is it rational in a [[self-help system]] to assume the worst in an adversary's intentions?
		- how can one know for certain that a rival is arming strictly for defensive purposes?
			- arms races
	- if a state doesn't have sufficient resources to be completely self-reliant in defense, it may join an alliance to deter aggression by potential adversaries
- [[Game Theory]] of arms races
	- all sides want peace
	- anarchical nature of int'l politics leads states to suspicion of each other
	- states engage in worst-case analyses of another's intentions
		- applicable to understanding [[Ancient World]] competition ([[Sparta]] and [[Athens]]) and contemporary [[IR]]
	- system-level explanation
		- emphasis on anarchic nature of int'l politics as a whole
			- not on internal nature of particular state
				- internal factors e.g. country keeps building more weapons because of demands from its military-industrial complex or nature of national mentality that reflects regional/global ambitions
				- external factors e.g. anarchic structure of system/actions and reactions of other states ignored
- anarchical [[self-help system]]
	- cooperation among states difficult to achieve
	- allegory of stag hunt from [[Rousseau]]
		- 5 individuals in state of nature (world w/o gov't or any other form of social structure) have to decide:
			1. to collaborate in the hunting of a stag necessary to meet hunger needs of all 5
			2. to defect from group to capture a hare
				- serving one's self-interest at expense of group
		- if individual serves common interest (go after stag) can they trust the others to do so?
			- if one cannot trust others, isn't it rational to go for the hare and defect from group before the others do?
			- is it possible to develop the basis for collaboration by all 5?
		- how one understands this allegory has to do with how one sees states interacting in world politics
			- some see state as serving only narrow self-interest
			- pessimists: number, duration, intensity of wars
				- int'l politics as sets of competitive games in which decisions may be zero-sun
			- optimists: great potential for collaboration among states
				- many states live in peace and harmony for a long time
				- competitive setting (positive-/variable-sum games): players can find ways in which all parties can gain (to different degrees)
				- when losses have to be taken: can be distributed to minimize damage to each party
				- payoffs (gains/losses) are asymmetric/uneven, but still the best that can be achieved for all players

|               | Individual interests: pursue the hare        | Group/collective interests: pursue the stag  |
| ------------- | -------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| **Short run** | Serve immediate self-interest                | May provide basis for possible collaboration |
| **Long run**  | No apparent basis for collaborative behavior | Serve long-term common interest              |
- [[structural realism]]'s assumptions
	- states are unitary and rational actors
	- states are largely concerned with *relative* rather than *absolute* gains
		- if state is concerned with individual, *absolute* gains
			- state is indifferent to the gains of others
		- if state is concerned with *relative* gains
			- state is satisfied with simply increasing its power/wealth
			- state is concerned with how much those capabilities have kept pace with/increased/decreased relative to other states
				- issue of how one defines and measures power
- differing assumptions about state's preferences leads to different expectations of prospects for int'l conflict and cooperation
	- [[structural realism]]: relative gains makes int'l cooperation in anarchic world difficult to attain
		- particularly among great powers prone to 
			- improving their relative position
			- holding their own position in int'l competition
		- don't have to rely on [[classical realism]] assumption
	- [[classical realism]]: humanity is inherently aggressive
		- [[Machiavelli]], [[Hobbes]]
	- optimists of int'l cooperation, [[The English School]], [[neoliberal institutionalism]], [[social constructivism]]: states may be satisfied with *absolute* gains
		- b/c of development of int'l norms, collaborative institutions, ability to redefine national interests
#### 5.4.2 Distribution of Capabilities and the Balance of Power
- how [[Realism]] views [[anarchy]]
	- a defining characteristic int'l system **unless one state or some superior int'l authority were constructed to provide a new order to the world through its position of dominance**
	- various distributions of power among states emerge in dynamic, competitive relations amongst among states
	- anarchy + distribution of power defines int'l system at any one time
		- [[unipolar]], [[bipolar distribution of power]], [[multipolar]]
	- [[balance of power]] and alliances among states are the means for sustaining int'l order
- [[Realism]]'s [[security dilemma]] in [[Anarchic]] world
	- where does order come from under such conditions?
	- what keeps states from continually attacking each other?
		- states find it better to ally and pool capabilities/power whenever one state/group of states are gaining a disproportionate amount of power, threatening to dominate the world/part of the world
		- [[Grotius]]/[[classical realism]]/[[constructivism]]/[[The English School]]
			- some degree of order provided by development and acceptance over time of int'l norms and practices (to be codified in int'l law)
		- need to maintain [[balance of power]] to avoid triumph of a dominant power
			- realist concern dating back to [[Thucydides]]
			- [[UK]]'s Foreign Office before [[WWI]]
	- [[bipolar distribution of power]]
		- 2 states of comparable power
	- [[multipolar]]
		- 3 or more states engaging in checks and balances
	- power configurations in the aftermath of [[Europe]]an wars
		- 1648: [[Peace of Westphalia]] following [[Thirty Years War]]
		- 1815: [[Congress of Vienna]] following defeat of [[Napoleon]]
			- successful in establishing basis for maintaining a [[balance of power]] w/o general/major war for about a century
			- [[Kissinger]]: "Their goal was stability, not perfection, and the [[balance of power]] is the classic expression of the lesson of history that no order is safe without physical safeguards against aggression."
				- "a new international order came to be created with a sufficient awareness of the connection between power and morality; between security and legitimacy"
					1. Do balances of power automatically occur, or are they created by diplomats/statesmen?
					2. Which [[balance of power]] – [[bipolar distribution of power]] or [[multipolar]] – is more likely to maintain int'l stability and is [[unipolar]]ity a durable condition?
					3. How much power should states seek in order to feel secure?
					4. How can nonsystemic factors (individual and state-society levels of analysis) be integrated into structural/neorealist accounts to explain the onset of [[War]]?
		- settlements following 20th c. world wars
		- [[post-WWI]] brings 20 years of peace
##### 5.4.2.1 Balance of Power: Voluntarism and Determinism
- [[voluntarism]]
	- freedom of choice
	- ability of humans to influence the course of events
	- context of [[IR]]
		- can states/their decision makers affect their environment in a system of states?
			- [[Kissinger]], [[classical realism]]: emphasizes [[voluntarism]]
				- [[balance of power]] is a foreign policy creation by statesman, not just occurring automatically
				- foreign policy makers aren't prisoners of [[balance of power]]
					- they are creators and charged with maintaining it
					- they're free to exercise their judgment and will as agents for their states with expectation they can have effect on outcomes
			- [[Kenneth Waltz]], opposition: [[balance of power]] is an attribute of the systems of states that will occur whether it's willed or not
				- "the [[balance of power]] is not so much imposed by statesmen on events as it is imposed by events on statesmen" #quote 
				- statesman has less freedom to maneuver and less capability to affect workings of int'l politics than Kissinger would allow
				- assumption: state is rational, unitary actor that'll use its capabilities to accomplish its objectives
					- states inevitably interact and conflict in competitive environment of int'l politics
					- states motivated to improve positions to dominate others
						- countered attempts by other states with same motivation
				- "freedom of choice of any one state is limited by the actions of other" #quote 
					- [[balance of power]], states tend to balance against a rising power vs. joining its bandwagon
				- structure of int'l system ([[anarchy]] + distribution of capabilities)
					- affects calculations and choices of decision makers
				- [[balance of power]] theory can be used to account for arms races, alliances/counter-alliances, coalitions/counter-coalitions, other forms of competitive behavior among states
					- transcends any particular historical era
		- how much is the behavior of states and other units driven by the int'l system vs. how much is socially constructed by human volition? ([[agent-structure]] debate)
- image of [[balance of power]]
	- recurrent phenomenon characteristic of [[IR]]
	- doesn't really matter whether states are [[democratic]] or [[authoritarian]]
		- systemic tendency toward balance is always the same
		- major powers knock lesser powers out of the way
			- these interactions tend toward int'l equilibrium/stability
			- same tendency toward stability repeats itself, only to be upset again
	- Ernst B. [[Haas]] (1924 – 2003)
		- critic of [[determinism]]
		- observation among [[structural realism]] and [[balance of power]] theorists:
			- "They see the components of systems, that is, states as relatively unchangeable and arrange them in an eternal preprogrammed dance. The rules of the dance may be unknown to the actors and are specified by the theorist. The recurrent patterns discovered by him constitute a super-logic which predicts the future state of the system." #quote 
	- [[Kenneth Waltz]]: [[structural realism]], [[Realism]], enamored with [[balance of power]]
	- Arnold [[Wolfers]] (1892 – 1968)
		- [[classical realism]]
		- critic of theorists focused only on states
			- doing so reduces complexity of [[IR]] to the actions and interactions of states
			- problematic formulations b/c they minimize role of non-state actors
- [[bipolar distribution of power]] and [[multipolar]] in history
	- actor combinations involving 2 or more states
	- displayed through 18th and 19th c.
	- before [[WWII]] (1939 – 1945)
	- [[Cold War]]
		- [[America]] vs. [[USSR]]
		- [[bipolar distribution of power]]
	- [[post-Cold War]]
		- dominant power of [[America]]
		- [[unipolar]]
		- problem for [[Realism]]'s [[balance of power]]'s theorists
			- for some, rapidly rising [[China]] foreshadows return to [[bipolar distribution of power]]
			- militaristic [[Russia]] tries to hold its power position
				- turns world [[multipolar]]
		- [[Russia]] remains a great power alongside [[America]] and [[China]]
- [[Kissinger]] vs. [[Kenneth Waltz]]
	- represent alternative ends of spectrum of [[Realism]] in tandem with [[balance of power]] thinking
	- [[Kenneth Waltz]]
		- emphasizes [[balance of power]] as a system tendancy
		- [[structural realism]]/[[neorealism]]
			- departed from [[Realism]] tradition that grants statesman/policy maker greater freedom from constraint –> greater ability to affect int'l events
		- individuals are minor compared to the int'l structure that influences conduct of states
			- e.g. Otto von [[Bismarck]] and [[Franklin D. Roosevelt]]
	- [[Kissinger]]
		- more on [[voluntarism]]
			- wouldn't argue that foreign policy makers are totally free of external constraints
				- function of diplomatic skills
				- [[Kenneth Waltz]] rejects that he's a system determinist (structure of int'l system necessarily determines state behavior)
					- acknowledges possibility of state/unit-level cause negating a structural effect
					- far removed from [[voluntarism]]
					- implication of [[balance of power]] view: individuals and their states have less freedom to affect course of events than others like [[Kissinger]] would assert
- [[Morgenthau]]
	- writings were earlier attempt to combine two perspectives
		- acknowledges [[balance of power]] as tendency within int'l politics
		- prescribes what statesmen should do to maintain the balance
			- "the balance of power and policies aiming at its preservation are not only inevitable but are an essential stabilizing factor in a society of sovereign nations" #quote 
				- [[determinism]]
				- assigns diplomats the task of maintaining [[balance of power]] and to "create the conditions under which it will not be impossible from the outset to establish a world state" #quote 
	- escape from [[balance of power]] and [[voluntarism]] creation of new world order is possible and a worthy pursuit
	- critics: to argue [[balance of power]] is an inevitable system tendency AND to prescribe what should be done to maintain a balance to the system is **contradictory**
	- middle ground between [[Realism]] scholars who tend toward [[voluntarism]] or [[determinism]]
		- recent manifestation of theoretical debate between [[structural realism]] and [[social constructivism]]
			- [[social constructivism]] more on [[voluntarism]], but also understanding *both* ideational and material structures may facilitate/constrain state actions
##### 5.4.2.2 Polarity and System Structure
- is [[bipolar distribution of power]] or [[multipolar]] [[balance of power]] more conducive to stability of int'l system?
	- is war more likely to occur in a [[bipolar distribution of power]] or [[multipolar]] world?
- [[Kenneth Waltz]] for [[bipolar distribution of power]]
	- greater uncertainty leads to policy makers misjudging intentions and actions of a potential foe
	- [[multipolar]] –> higher levels of uncertainty –> less desirable than [[bipolar distribution of power]]
		- [[multipolar]] makes uncertainty –> probability of war greater
- J. David [[Singer]] (1925 – 2009) and Karl [[Deutsch]] (1912 – 1992) for [[multipolar]]
	- [[multipolar]] system more conducive to stability because **uncertainty breeds caution on the part of states**
	- caution means following tried and true policies of the past and avoiding deviations
		- "increase in number of independent actors diminishes the share of attention that any nation can allocate to any other single actor" #quote 
			- reduces probability of [[War]] because a state's attention is allocated to a larger number of actors
- all 3 agree: uncertainty increases about consequences of a particular action taken by a state as the number of int'l actors increases
	- as the number increases, a state's policy makers have to deal with a greater quantity of information
		- more int'l actors means more information generated that has to be taken into account to form foreign policy
	- as int'l system moves from [[bipolar distribution of power]] –> [[multipolar]], amount of overall uncertainty increases in the system
- disagreement on whether an increase of number of actors (and increase in uncertainty) makes [[War]] more or less likely
- consensus on systemic stability?
	- inconclusive at the time, now there's more consensus
	- [[bipolar distribution of power]] more stable
		- [[Cold War]]: [[USSR]] vs. [[America]] was more stable
		- [[post-Cold War]]: [[unipolar]] of [[America]] was less stable
			- invites new balancers
		- emergence of a [[multipolar]] world that complicates relations and decisions by several power – was less stable
- two other dimensions of polarity at systemic level of analysis
	- disparities in capabilities among poles (not simply the number)
	- implications of whether the capability growth rates of states are static or dynamic
##### 5.4.2.3 How Much Power? Defensive and Offensive Realists
- disagreements on implications of [[anarchy]] and how much weight [[anarchy]] should be attributed to it as a contributing factor to power-seeking behavior by states
- [[Kenneth Waltz]], [[defensive realism]]
	- states, at a minimum, merely aim to survive and seek security
	- other objectives can be added to the list
		- capabilities/power will be applied to achieving them
	- requires close attention to [[balance of power]]
	- [[anarchy]] particularly requires states to engage in competitive behavior
		- as opposed to [[Morgenthau]]'s [[classical realism]] emphasis on human nature and drive for power causing security competition
	- int'l system provides incentives for expansion only under certain circumstances
		- under [[anarchy]], [[security dilemma]] causes states to worry about relative power and intentions of other states
			- efforts to increase power may inadvertently generate spirals of hostility
			- pursuit of [[expansionism]] may be generated by this fear
			- mistaken assumption that aggressive behavior is best way to make state more secure
		- states can't escape [[security dilemma]]
		- under [[anarchy]], [[War]] remains possible but outbreak isn't inevitable
- structural factors impacting whether states use **cooperation** or **conflict**
	- [[offense-defense balance]]
		- argument: at any point in time, military power can favor the **offense** or **defense**
			- if **defense** dominates:
				- conquest is difficult
				- major powers have little incentive to use force to gain power
				- incentive is to hold onto what they have
			- if **offense** dominates:
				- states attempt to conquer one another
					- generates majors wars
			- usually in favor of **defense**
				- conquest becomes more difficult
				- states discouraged from pursuing aggressive policies
			- implication: states should carefully consider that moderate strategies may enhance security, but [[expansionism]] will end up being the means to achieve this goal
		- analysts shouldn't overstate the importance of [[anarchy]]
		- argument: security is readily available, especially if states adopt wise strategies
			- assumption: int'l system provides incentives for cautious, restrained behavior for states
			- assumption: reckless [[expansionism]] is a result of domestic factors rather than systemic conditions (like [[anarchy]])
		- defensive realists have [[status quo power]] bias
	- [[offensive realism]]
		- question: how much power do states want?
		- question: what are the implications of [[anarchy]]
		- argument: implications of [[anarchy]] provides strong incentives for expansion of power capabilities relative to other states
			- states strive for maximum power to other states
				- only way to guarantee survival
		- argument: [[status quo power]]s are rarely found in int'l politics
			- structure of int'l system creates strong incentives to gain power relative to one's rivals
			- [[defensive realism]]: can't explain at the systemic level state [[expansionism]] b/c int'l incentives for such behavior are lacking
		- [[Mearsheimer]]: emphasizes [[structural realism]] on offensive/power-maximizing rather than [[defensive realism]]'s [[status quo power]]
			- [[offensive realism]] is a descriptive theory
				- how states behave
			- [[offensive realism]] is also a prescriptive theory
				- states should follow as the best way to survive in a dangerous world
			- critical of [[defensive realism]]'s focus on states being focused on maintaining the existing [[balance of power]] rather than increasing their share of power
			- views states as trying to maximize their power positions
				- state's ultimate goal: be the [[hegemonic power]] in the system
				- "best way for a state to survive in [[anarchy]] is to take advantage of other states and gain power at their expense" #quote 
		- assumption: [[anarchy]] and distribution of capabilities matter most in explaining big issues of int'l politics ([[War]] and peace)
			- little attention to individuals, domestic politics, ideology
			- [[Mearsheimer]]'s argument: doesn't matter whether [[Germany]] in 1905 was led by [[Bismarck]], Kaiser [[Wilhelm I]], or [[Hitler]] or whether [[Germany]] was a [[democracy]] or [[autocracy]]
				- what matters? **how much relative power [[Germany]] had**
			- in cases of domestic factors being a major role, [[offensive realism]] doesn't have much to say
				- cost of simplifying reality and attempting to develop ungenerous theories that tell few things about important matters
		- echoes of [[Morgenthau]] in [[Mearsheimer]]
			- [[Morgenthau]]'s conception of power in int'l politics
			- [[Mearsheimer]]: states want to maximize power as "a means to an end (survival) but also an end in itself"
				- [[structural realism]], agrees with [[Kenneth Waltz]]
					- drive for power is a function of structure of int'l system, **not** human nature as [[Morgenthau]] argued
				- great powers can still understand the limits of their power *while* pursuing power as an end
					- constrained by other states pursuing same ends
			- in the great game of int'l politics, "the trick for the sophisticated power maximizer is to figure out when to raise and when to fold" #quote 
		- [[Mearsheimer]]: actual distribution of military power among great powers is critical in terms of [[War]]
			- if great power has a power advantage over its rivals:
				- more likely the great power will behave aggressively
					- has the capability and incentive to do so
			- if great power is facing powerful opponents:
				- less likely to consider offensive action
				- more likely to focus defending existing [[balance of power]]
			- reflects basic [[Realism]] assumption: states carefully calculate costs and benefits of offensive action, aware of its strengths *and* limitations
		- can cooperation exist among states?
			- great powers *do* cooperate
				- form alliances and act against common enemies
			- *extensive* cooperation is limited by
				- *relative* gains
				- prospect of cheating
			- if each side is pursuing *absolute* gains:
				- little concern with what others are gaining and losing
			- in world of [[balance of power]], states have to be concerned with *relative* gains
				- relative gains of a rival could upset the balance
			- int'l system structured so that cooperation is difficult to achieve and conflict is more likely
			- concerns over cheating in military realm
				- holds out prospect of defeat
				- works against cooperation
##### 5.4.2.4 Nonsystemic Factors
- [[classical realism]] vs. [[structural realism]]
	- [[classical realism]]: blends various units and levels of analysis into their accounts of [[IR]]
	- [[structural realism]]: downplays domestic factors
		- [[Kenneth Waltz]]
			- also highlights importance of state-societal level of analysis
		- [[Mearsheimer]]
- [[defensive realism]] influenced by [[Kenneth Waltz]]
	- tries to overcome [[structural realism]]'s limitations
		- inability to explain cases where major powers act in nonstrategic ways
			- requires delving into individual and state-societal levels of analysis
			- requires examining role of factors like human agency
				- roles humans play in domestic politics
				- roles of humans' cognitive understandings, (mis)perceptions, elite belief systems
- [[Kenneth Waltz]]
	- 1954: dissertation leads to
	- 1959: first book, [[Man, the State, and War (1959) by Kenneth Waltz]]
		- [[classical realism]] tradition
		- doctoral student of [[William T. R. Fox]] (1912 – 1988)
			- Columbia University professor
			- coined the term "[[superpower]]"
	- earlier studies of war
		- [[Quincy Wright]] (1890 – 1970)
			- [[The Causes of War and the Conditions of Peace (1935) by Quincy Wright]]
			- [[A Study of War (1942) by Quincy Wright]]
				- two-volumes
			- advocates strong int'l organization as remedy for [[War]] problem
	- early formulation of "images"/levels of analysis
		- were the causes of [[War]] to found at individual, state-societal, interstate system levels of analysis?
			- some argue human nature is basic cause of war
			- others argue internal organization of states as key to understanding war and peace
			- others focus on impact of structure of interstate system
	- int'l systemic anarchy is permissive cause of war
		- no power/authority can keep state from using force against another
		- efficient causes of particular wars can be found at any/all 3 levels of analysis
		- [[Man, the State, and War (1959) by Kenneth Waltz]] refers to [[Rousseau]]
			- develops "third image" systemic level of analysis
	- influence of [[Rousseau]]'s proposed "[[Perpetual Peace by Abbé de St. Pierre]]" and [[The State of War by Jean-Jacques Rousseau]]

			- distinguishes a *civil state*
				- individuals subject to laws, but still enjoy liberty of nature
			- like [[Hobbes]]: recognition of anarchic structure of int'l system

	- [[Kenneth Waltz]]: states resort to use of force under anarchy
		- states seek to enhance their power to accomplish their objectives

- [[Stephen Walt]]
	- student of [[Kenneth Waltz]]
	- recasts [[balance of power theory]] (states align against most powerful states)
		- offers more historically correct explanations
		- intentions need to be taken into account
			- states balance against states that are powerful and threatening
	- states are attracted to strength
		- "a decline in a state's relative position will lead its allies to opt for neutrality at best or to defect to the other side at worst" #quote 
	- threats matter too, not just power
		- "the greater the threat, the greater the probability that the vulnerable state will seek an alliance" #quote 
	- [[balancing]] vs. [[bandwagoning]]
		- [[balancing]]
			- the dominant tendency in int'l politics
			- allying with others against prevailing threat
			- more common
			- tends to reflect restraint
			- tends to reflect an effort to "minimize the threat one poses to others"
		- [[bandwagoning]]
			- opportunistic option
			- aligning with source of danger (usually a strong state)
			- less common
			- typically occurs in a much more competitive context
	- how revolutionary domestic changes can increase risk of int'l [[War]]
		- intensifies int'l security competition
		- uses theoretical adjustment of [[balance of power theory]] for analysis
		- dangers posed by states matter
			- not just power position
		- imbalances of threat can cause alliances against most threatening state
		- [[balance of threat theory]] provides a stronger explanation than tradition [[balance of power theory]]
- [[Stephen Van Evera]]
	- former student of [[Kenneth Waltz]]
	- adds *ideas, perceptions, misperceptions* to material understandings of power and power distribution
	- beyond [[multipolar]], [[bipolar distribution of power]], and [[unipolar]]

		- accounts for 
			- [[offense-defense balance]]
			- advantage of taking first move
			- size and frequency of power fluctuations
			- available resources
		- perception and misperception matter
			- war more likely when states **believe** conquest is easy
		- other war-causing ideas
			- windows of vulnerability
			- hostility of other states
			- threatening diplomatic tactics
				- [[coercive diplomacy]]
			- when war is cheap or beneficial
- [[Barry Posen]]
	- former student of [[Kenneth Waltz]]
	- tests 2 theories to see which does a better job explaining how ]] takes shape and influences grand strategy decisions ([[France]], [[UK]], [[Germany]]) during [[WWI]] and [[WWII]]
		- [[organization theory]]
		- [[balance of power theory]]

- [[Dale Copeland]]
	- [[The Origins of Major War by Dale Copeland]]
		- effort to understand conditions under which large-scale is likely to occur
		- integrates issues for [[Realism]]:
			- is a [[multipolar]] or [[bipolar distribution of power]] system more likely to encourage war?
			- how important are *relative gains* and *losses* to an explanation of [[War]]?
			- how can insights from two competing camps of [[structural realism]] ([[offensive realism]] and [[defensive realism]]) be reconciled?
			- are rising powers or declining powers more likely to initiate war and under what conditions?
		- bottom line argument: great powers that anticipate deep and inevitable relative decline are more likely to initiate major wars or pursue hardline policies that substantially increase the risk of major war through inadvertent escalation
			- application of [[dynamic differentials theory]] to 10 historical case studies
		- [[dynamic differentials theory]]
			- dependent variable: variation in probability of major war over time within [[bipolar distribution of power]] or [[multipolar]] system
			- key explanatory variable: [[dynamic differential]]
				- "the simultaneous interaction of the differentials or *relative military power* between great powers and the expected trend of those differentials, distinguishing between the effects of power changes in [[bipolar distribution of power]] vs. [[multipolar]] systems"
			- attempts to explain likelihood of major power engaging in preventive war
			- attempts to explain diplomatic and military strategies that might be pursued
				- uses [[rational-actor assumption]] of leaders who are risk-neutral and cost-neutral
		- [[multipolar]] systems
			- declining power must possess significant military superiority over each of the other great powers before initiating war/hardline policies that risk war
				- if lack of military superiority:
					- prospect of facing a [[balancing]] coalition or fighting several bilateral contests would deter state from taking on rest of system
		- [[bipolar distribution of power]]
			- declining power doesn't need to be militarily superior before initiating war/implementing such policies against major rival
				- can even be slightly militarily inferior
			- facing only one adversary
				- chance of costly bilateral wars is minimal
		- likelihood of major war comes from the strategy adopted by declining power
		- if leaders of major power expect trend to be rapid and inevitable decline in power:
			- more likely to engage in preventive war
		- Copeland doesn't fall back on sub-systemic/unit auxiliary theories (threat perception, individual pathologies, ideology, civilian-military relations, [[military doctrine]])
			- [[rational-actor assumption]]
				- theory of [[IR]] and theory of [[foreign relations]]/[[Geopolitics]]
#### 5.4.3 Realists and the Russian Invasion of Ukraine
- major shocks in int'l system
	- 1991: collapse of [[USSR]] (Soviet Union)
	- 2001: terrorists attacked [[America]]
	- 2022: [[Russia]] invades [[Ukraine]]
- [[Realism]]'s perspective validated by world events
	- from fall of [[USSR]], realists cautioned against expanding [[NATO]] eastward (incorporating former [[USSR]] satellite states into organization)
		- would be viewed by [[Russia]] as threat to its security
	- logic of [[anarchy]], [[self-help system]], [[security dilemma]]
		- requires states to be concerned about their relative power
	- [[America]]'s [[unipolar]] moment would eventually end
		- [[balance of power]] will be restored
- at the time, there was little [[Russia]] could do in its weakened position
	- [[Russia]] could evolve into a satisfied member of western community, accepting [[Liberalism]] rules-based order
		- optimism among [[Liberalism]] of [[IR]]
- [[Realism]]'s perspective validated by subsequent events
	- 2004: Russia too weak to keep [[Baltic]] states out of [[NATO]]
	- 2008: [[NATO]] summit in Bucharest, [[Hungary]]
		- statement issued: [[Ukraine]] and [[Georgia (country)]] could eventually join [[NATO]]
		- assumption: [[Ukraine]] would become member of [[EU]]
		- [[Russia]] states expansion of [[NATO]] to [[Russia]]-[[Ukraine]] border is unacceptable
		- [[Putin]] declares such event would be "direct threat" to [[Russia]]'s [[security]]
	- 4 months later: [[Russia]] invades [[Georgia (country)]], former [[USSR]] republic
	- 2014: [[Russia]] takes over [[Ukraine]]'s [[Crimea]]
	- 2022: [[Russia]]'s full-scale invasion of [[Ukraine]]
- [[Realism]] condemns [[Russia]]'s actions, not justifying them
	- moral condemnation doesn't prevent such actions
	- [[Russia]] acted in defense of perceived nat'l security interests
		- not deterred by their assessment of Ukrainian power in 2014, nor 2022
- [[Realism]]'s expectations
	- [[America]] and allies dramatically revised their assessment of [[Russia]] as a threat
		- engaged in [[balancing]] behavior
			- responded with severe sanctions and massive shipment of arms and other aid to [[Ukraine]]
