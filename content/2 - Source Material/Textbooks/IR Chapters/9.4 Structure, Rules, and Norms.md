#### 9.4.0 Structure, Rules, and Norms
- [[constructivism]]'s definition of *structure*: social relations and shared meanings, differing on the component elements and their relative importance
	- elements: clusters of rules, norms, principled beliefs, shared knowledge, practices, material elements
	- material resources: acquire meaning for human action only through structure of shared knowledge (embedded)
- int'l politics are [[Anarchic]], but structure is defined in cultural rather than [[Realism]]'s material terms
- [[security dilemma]] can exist among states, but viewed as an ideational social structure composed of intersubjective understandings in which states are prone to make worst-case assumptions about one another's intentions
- beyond [[self-help system]], states may seek allies or coalition partners
	- [[Cold War]]: a social structure of intersubjectively shared knowledge and meanings that governed relations among [[America]], [[USSR]], and their allies
		- 1980s: the structure of shared knowledge or meanings begins to change
			- tentative steps towards reassurance made by [[USSR]], reciprocated by [[America]]
	- [[post-Cold War]]: structure of shared knowledge shifted from emphasizing egoistic identities and [[self-help system]] –> [[zero-sum]] (one side gains, the other loses) –> [[positive-sum]] (mutual gains)
- [[constructivism]] and [[Realism]]: world can be a nasty and violent place
	- [[constructivism]]: emphasizes importance of subjective and intersubjective understandings
		- takes issue with [[Realism]]'s explanation
- [[anarchy]] is a social construction and actors give it meaning
	- IR can take place in a [[Hobbes]] world of constant strife
	- [[constructivism]] attempts to explain how and why particular practices become fixed in some cases and fluid and subject to change/decay in others
		- detailed examination of the historical context within which structures arise
		- rules and meanings of expected behavior that reflect mutual, intersubjective understandings provide order to int'l politics, stabilize actors' expectations, and help to manage relations among actors
			- e.g. generally accepted diplomatic practices
			- when such ideational structures persist and become institutionalized/routinized over time, they gain causal and [[normative]] force
				- leads to patterns of behavior that can be empirically analyzed
				- can facilitate or serve as obstacles to courses of action that states choose to follow
				- structures *can* change over time, but not guaranteed change
					- e.g. changing structures affecting states involves altering an existing set of mutually reinforcing expectations
				- this conception of structure is applied by [[constructivism]] in several ways at different levels of analysis from global to regional or issue-specific int'l regimes
				- these ideational structures can impact the behavior of state and non-state actors as much as materially based power or class structures (found in [[Realism]] and [[Economic Structuralism]])
#### 9.4.1 Rules
- [[constructivism]]'s distinction between *constitutive* and *regulative* rules
	- contrasting approach to IR from [[neorealism]] and [[neoliberalism]]
		- *regulative* rules
			- e.g. driving cars existed before establishing socially accepted rule in certain countries (right-hand vs. left-hand side of the road)
				- rules were instituted due to increased traffic and possibility of accidents
				- *regulative* rules: formulated to regulate an existing activity (e.g. driving cars)
					- other regulative rules added to include issuing licenses, setting speed limits, yielding at intersections
		- *constitutive* rules
			- e.g. chess
				- rules create the possibility of playing chess in the first place
					- not a matter of people pushing pieces around the board and bumping into each other that created the need for regulative rules
					- "The rules are constitutive of chess in the sense that playing chess is constituted in part by acting according to the rules" #quote 
				- *regulative* rules are designed to have a causal effect (e.g. getting people to drive on right/left-side of the road)
					- "In contrast constitutive rules define the set practices that make up any particularly consciously organized social activity – that is to say, they specify *what counts* as that activity" #quote 
	- [[constructivism]] point: [[Epistemology]] (ways of knowing present in [[neorealism]]) lacks this concept of *constitutive* rules
		- world of antecedent/pre-existing actors and their relations in an [[Anarchic]] world
			- role of theory: explain their conduct/behavior within a system not of their making
		- [[constructivism]]: [[neorealism]] tends to focus on things as they are/have come to be, and fails to explain the origins of states/system of states that are so prominent in their analyses
- [[John Ruggie]] (prominent constructivist)
	- argument: the concept of the *modern state* was only possible when a new rule for differentiating the components of the emerging [[Europe]]an system replaced the [[medieval]] system of overlapping jurisdictions and authorities claimed by popes, kings, feudal lords, trading assocs.
	- examines material *and* ideational factors that produced the concept of exclusive territoriality that serves as the constitutive rule defining the *modern state system*
- *constitutive* rules are logically prior to what's the focus of most [[Realism]] and [[Liberalism]] theorists who tend to take as a given the existence of the state and non-state actors engaged in int'l politics
#### 9.4.2 Norms
- *norms*: generally accepted values that define standards of appropriate behavior for agents (actors) with a given identity
	- norms operating as rules (define)
		- has a constitutive effect that specify what actions will cause relevant others to recognize a particular identity
	- norms operating as standards (prescribe)
		- has a regulative effect that specify the proper enactment of an already defined identity
	- norms can do both
- how are norms developed?
	- origin: can be int'l or domestic
	- when norms take form of principled beliefs, they can lead in certain circumstances to states redefining their interests and sense of self (identity) and influence int'l outcomes
		- e.g. support for [[decolonization]], end of [[apartheid]], human rights, emergence of taboos on certain weapons, prohibiting bribery of gov't officials to secure contracts, etc.
	- impact of principled beliefs can be facilitated by transnational networks of non-state actors
	- current research re: norms
		- norms governing the conduct of interstate [[War]] and humanitarian intervention 
		- examining the situations under which norms are violated
			- repeated violations of int'l norms by states can erode/eventually invalidate norms, occasional violations don't
