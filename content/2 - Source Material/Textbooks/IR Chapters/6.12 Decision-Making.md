#### 6.12.0 Decision-Making
- [[Liberalism]]'s most important insight on [[IR]]: centrality of state-society relations
	- evident in [[democratic peace theory]] literature
	- the state is composed of diverse societal actors, instead of a rational, unified actor
		- ideas, group interests, institutions, individuals shape state preferences (not given)
	- catalog of independent variables to help account for state behavior in any particular issue area
		- combined with reality of impact of [[interdependence]] and [[Globalization]]
- 1960s: [[Foreign Policy Decision Making by Richard Snyder, H.W. Bruck, and Burton Sapin]]
	- [[Richard Snyder]], H. W. [[Bruck]], Burton [[Sapin]] (SBS)
		- looks inside the state and society
		- disaggregates [[neorealism]] rational, unified actor
		- reflects and parallels perspectives of [[interest group liberalism]] ([[6.2 Intellectual Precursors and Influences#6.2.1 Interest Group Liberalism]])
		- bureaucracies, interest groups, psychological and social-psychological factors that influence decision makers
	- [[Valerie Hudson]] re-examines work by SBS
		- traces its impact over decades and on more recent work that would follow in this genre
- SBS aware of [[agent-structure]] issue
	- state-as-actor has meaning only when we look within the state to the decision makers themselves and examine how they're influenced by domestic factors
		- how they relate to their decision-making counterparts in other states
	- material and ideational factors intersect at the decision-making level
		- not abstract level of state
	- real people make decisions
	- situational **and** biographical factors influence foreign policy choices
	- **agency** is important in this classic understanding
- work in [[Psychology]] and [[social psychology]] 
	- contributed substantially to knowledge of how and why decision makers act
	- cognitive orientations and ontologies decision makers have directly influence their perceptions and meanings they infer from what they observe
		- under what conditions are decision makers blind from realities that contradict their prior expectations?
		- are our perceptions affected by **cognitive dissonance** since we tend to not see what we don't expect to see?
			- if so, this can lead to intelligence failures and adversely affect decision-making in crisis/non-crisis conditions
- **perceptions** play an important role in times of crisis
	- [[Perception and Misperception in International Politics by Robert Jervis]]
		- Robert [[Jervis]]: individuals relate images they have to the facts/information they have before them
			- Richard Ned [[Lebow]] tested similar ideas in [[Between Peace and War by Richard Ned Lebow]]
	- content analysis of documents used to esplore pattern of decisions, mindsets, assumptions that led to outbreak of e.g. [[WWI]]
		- work on crisis decision-making builds upon this foundation
			- identifies how stress during crises compounded by short time to receive and assess a large quantity of information can reduce span of attention, results in cognitive rigidity, causes dysfunctions that adversely affect decision-making tasks
- **group dynamics** can result in dysfunctions with adverse affects on decision-making
	- [[Victims of Groupthink by Irving Janis]]
		- [[Irving Janis]]: consensus building and team play within a group can lead to group screening out information at odds with consensus view
			- naysayers that challenge "common wisdom" will be ostracized/pay some high price for taking a contrary position
- challenge to [[rational-actor assumption]] approach to explaining foreign policy choices
	- [[Essence of Decision by Graham Allison]]
		- [[Graham Allison]]'s book expands on earlier conceptual models of [[Cuban Missile Crisis]]
			- October 1972: [[Cuban Missile Crisis]] almost led to war between [[America]] and [[USSR]]
		- **Model I**: [[Realism]]'s [[rational-actor assumption]]
			- [[Graham Allison]] questions this conventional assumption in which states specify their objectives consistent with national interests, identify alternatives, and choose options most likely to achieve these purposes
		- **Model II**: asks whether organizational process affect decisions, by contrast ([[Graham Allison]])
			- routines, standard operating procedures orgs employ, ethos/perspectives that define their organizational essence and ways of doing gov't business
			- [[Graham Allison]] acknowledges insight owed to [[Max Weber]]'s perspective on [[bureaucratic rationality]]
				- routinizing recurrent functions in efforts to achieve efficiencies
		- **Model III**: bureaucratic policies added to the mix ([[Graham Allison]])
			- coalition and counter-coalition formation
			- pulling-and-hauling of every day struggles within and among bureaucracies
- 1980s: decline in decision-making literature
	- cognitive factors remained part of discourse
		- attention to role of ideas in foreign policy
			- Robert O. [[Keohane]] and Judith [[Goldstein]]'s works
	- [[Goldstein]]: to understand [[America]]'s trade policies over the years, reliance on int'l structure or domestic economic interests are insufficient
		- need to take into account actors' causal beliefs as to which economic policies can best achieve preferred interests
	- Ernst [[Haas]]: importance of cognitive factors and ideas ([[When Knowledge is Power by Ernst Haas]])
		- role of ideas is critical to [[constructivism]]'s theories on [[IR]] and foreign policy ([[9. Constructivist Understandings]])
- recent revival interest in perception literature applied to [[IR]]
	- [[Overconfidence and War by Dominic Johnson]]
		- [[Dominic Johnson]] revisits question: why are states susceptible to exaggerated ideas of their ability to control events and foresee future outcomes?
			- especially when power differences would suggest it's foolish to go to war
		- examines power of "positive illusion" and overconfidence
			- case studies: [[WWI]], 1939: [[Munich Crisis]], [[Vietnam War]], [[Iraq War]]/[[Second Gulf War]]
			- includes theoretical and empirical work: [[Psychology]], [[evolutionary biology]], int'l conflict
	- [[Failing to Win – Perceptions of Victory and Defeat in International Politics by Dominic Tierney and Dominic Johnson]]
		- decision-making literature mainly focused on decision to go to [[War]] or factors influencing decisions during a crisis
		- [[Dominic Tierney]] and [[Dominic Johnson]]: interested in evaluations of success or failure once shooting has stopped/crisis is resolved
			- their question: what are the psychological, political, and cultural factors that predispose observers (leaders, public, media) to perceive outcomes of int'l disputes as victories or defeats?
			- argument: there's a gap between perceptions (observers' personal interpretations) and reality
				- sometimes perceptions and reality of events on battlefield match
					- e.g. April 1945: [[Fall of Berlin]] to [[USSR]]
				- other times, they don't or different observers judge similar events differently
		- conceptual framework to explain dependent variable: people's perceptions of victory and defeat
			1. examine conventional wisdom hypothesis: people's judgments of victory and defeat simply reflect material outcome
				- referred to as **score-keeping**
				- 5 possible definitions of **victory** that can serve as empirically verifiable metrics
			2. attempt to explain why observed gains and losses made by each side often fail to explain people's perceptions of victory in wars/crises
				- argument: score-keeping approach fails to answer this question adequately
				- explanatory concept: "match-fixing"
					- relies on literature of informational and psychological biases: cognitive processes, affective processes, learning theory, cultural influences
- recent work on individual leaders during crises
	- corrective to focus on system-wide variables
	- [[Peace at What Price by Sarah Croco]]
		- Sarah [[Croco]] argues that leader culpability is a critical explanation for decision to prolong wars
			- even wars that are obvious quagmires (no easy exit) and seem destined to fail
				- e.g. [[America]] in [[Vietnam War]], [[Quagmire Theory]]
		- explicates relationship among leaders, domestic politics, [[War]], and why some leaders are held accountable for wars they initiate but not others
- 1990s: Daniel [[Kahneman]] and Amos [[Tversky]]'s [[prospect theory]]
	- alternative to [[expected utility theory]] ([[rationalism]]) to provide insights into decision-making under conditions of risk and uncertainty
		- [[expected utility theory]]: focus on how individuals evaluate and choose between available options
	- [[Kahneman]] and [[Tversky]] focus on why individuals consistently deviated from predictions made by [[rationalism]]
		- most individuals were **risk-averse** when it came to securing gains, but **risk-acceptant** when it came to avoiding losses
			- **loss aversion** was a prime motivating factor
				- most people value items they already possess more than items they want to acquire
- primary question: how to explain obvious anomalous choices by leaders
	- [[prospect theory]] concepts integrated with experimental literature on **group-risk taking** in foreign policy decision-making
		- [[coercive diplomacy]], [[bargaining]], [[deterrence theory]]
- studying ideas, perceptions, and orgs to improve understanding of foreign policy/nat'l security decision-making
	1. how much emphasis should be placed on domestic level of analysis as opposed to the int'l level when attempting to explain int'l outcomes and patterns?
	2. how can theorists link the two levels to provide a complete accounting of phenomena under consideration?
- challenge for [[IR]] scholars: developing theories that account simultaneously for interaction of domestic and int'l factors
