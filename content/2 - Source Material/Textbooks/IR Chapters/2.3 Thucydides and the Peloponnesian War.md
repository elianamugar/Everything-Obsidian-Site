#### 2.3.0 Thucydides and the Peloponnesian War
- *History of the Peloponnesian War* - account of the first 20 years of the 5th c. struggle (431 - 404 BCE) between two alliances dominated by Athens and Sparta
- Thucydides - Athenian upper-middle class, naval officer stripped of command and banished from Athens in the 7th year of the war
	- failed to arrive in time to prevent the fall to the Spartans of northern Macedonia (Amphipolis)
	- writing picked up where Herodotus left off, traveling through Greece (similar to Marco Polo)
	- different from Herodotus: no role for gods, oracles, omens
	- uses actual events to illustrate underlying patterns of world politics
		- history of war: simple recounting of campaign strategies and battle tactics to illuminate trends –> moral issues entwined with analysis of war
	- **difficulties of the morality of exercising power**
#### 2.3.1 Historical Context
- Hellenic world of 5th c. BCE similar to Italian Renaissance city-state system (rather than modern states) – small populations, limited control of territory beyond city walls, proximity to other city-states
- political forms of city-states: 
	- monarchies –> [[despotisms]]
		- rule by powerful individuals
	- enlightened aristocracies
	- exploitative oligarchies
		- rule by the few
	- democracies
		- rule by the many
			- "many" limited to males deemed worth of being a "citizen"
- Athens, [[Pericles]] - democratic
	- integrated, organic relation between state and society (polis)
	- slavery an accepted institution
- some city-states were naturally more powerful than others, dominating weaker city-states
	- perhaps extracting tribute in return for military protection
- diplomatic practices were rudimentary
	- delegations traveling to other city-states to present demands, resolve disputes, negotiate trade agreements
- Sparta - aristocracy
	- participation of the upper classes
	- accountability on individuals chosen to preside over the city-state
	- strong warrior caste
	- majority population, underclass (helots) excluded from participation in gov't and politics
	- power expansion
		- primary goal: assure that neighboring states wouldn't be in a position to stir up trouble among the helots
			- concerned with domestic security
				- limited extent of Spartan ambitions beyond Peloponnesian peninsula
				- leadership didn't want to have military forces off in foreign lands should a helot revolt break out
	- city-states allied with Sparta were allowed to conduct their own affairs
		- assured of Spartan military protection
		- pledged to support Sparta in time of need
	- minor role in repelling Persian invasion of northern Greece (490 BCE)
		- Greek victory - battle of Marathon
	- agreed to accept command of combined Greek forces to repel second Persian invasion of Xerxes (480 BCE)
- Sparta + allies unable to hold back Persians - battle of Thermopylae
- Athens - naval + democracy wanted naval confrontation
	- Athenian victory - battle of Salamis
- Spartan army + allies routed the Persians, Xerxes forces retreated - battle of Plataea
- creation of the **[[Delian League]]**, one foreign threat: Persia
	- ~200 members
	- Sparta returns to traditional concerns, limited sphere of influence on the Peloponnese
	- Athens wants to keep Persians deterred from launching another invasion of Greece
	- league comprised of city-states most vulnerable to Persian pressure (west coast of modern-day Turkey, islands in the Aegean)
	- Athens expands navy and military, reconstructs defensive walls
- once the foreign threat was neutralized, problems in the city-states appear
	- resentment and fear of Athenian domination
- democratic city-states were faithful to Athens, aristocratic city-states were faithful to Sparta
- Delian League looked more like part of an Athenian empire than an alliance of independent city-states
- deterioration of relations between Athens and Sparta led to war (457 BCE)
	- Athens dominated central Greece + sea power
	  Sparta dominated Peloponnesian peninsual + land power
- Sparta and Athens truce (451 BCE)
- Athens uses League resources to extend power in campaigns vs. Corinth
	- campaigns had little to do with League's avowed purpose of repelling the Persians
- Pericles of Athens invites Greek city-states to attend a conference to improve relations
	- rejected by city-states in Peloponnese (Spartan area), viewed in suspicion by city-states subject to Athenian rule
- Athens quells Sparta-backed unrest in Boeotia (central Greece)
	- threatened to renew direct conflict between Athens and Sparta
- Athens and Sparta negotiate another peace treaty (30 years long)
	- Athens receives Spartan recognition of Athenian rule over remaining allies
	- Athens allows city-states to leave the Delian League
	- treaty recognizes spheres of influence and two principal city-states being roughly balanced in terms of power
		- structural realists: [[bipolar distribution of power]]
#### 2.3.2 *History of the Peloponnesian War:* The Work Itself
- accessible account of war
- plague in Athens almost leads to complete social disintegration
- civil war in Corcyra (modern-day Corfu)
	- brother v. brother, father v. son
- destruction of Athenian military expedition to Sicily
- importance to IR:
	- provides numerous analytical insights on world politics
		- useful in understanding present-day IR dynamics
	- cautionary tales for statesmen engaged in the conduct of foreign policy (learning from history)
		- Thucydides reveals larger truths about the human condition and highlights situations expected to recur throughout the ages
	- prime example of realist perspective
#### 2.3.3 Analytical Insights from Thucydides
- What caused the outbreak of the Peloponnesian War?
	- draw larger lessons about the general phenomenon of war that would stand the test of time
	- difference between the underlying and the immediate causes of a given war
- Thucydides
	- underlying cause involved factors that developed over a long time
	- immediate causes included specific events or more recent developments that made war more likely for the timing of its outbreak
##### 2.3.3.1 Underlying Cause of War
- What was the underlying cause of the Peloponnesian War?
	- Thucydides: "What made war inevitable was the growth of Athenian power and the fear this caused in Sparta."
	- Sparta was afraid of its relative decline compared to Athens
	- the changing distribution or [[balance of power]] in the Greek system of city-states generated suspicion and distrust
		- fortification of Athens made Peloponnesians uneasy
- present-day theorists hypothesize a critical underlying cause of war is the link between the outbreak of war and a changing distribution of power ([[Anarchic]])
	- anarchic – lacking any central authority with power over separate states
- **Graham Allison:** asks if it is inevitable that war will occur between China and the United States, given fear in the latter about the rising power in the former ("Thucydides's trap")
	- consistent with present-day [[power transition theory]] – when a rival beings to gain on the dominant or hegemonic state, the probability of war increases
- Would war have been broken out if the situation had been the reverse, if it were the Athenians who feared the growth of Spartan power?
	- PTT suggests it is likely
	- fear is the basic reason a city-state takes a particular action in the hope of safeguarding its security and independence
		- Why did Athenians claim they initial increased their power?
			- Because their chief motive was fear of Persia.
		- Why did the Thessalians and Magnetes prepare for war?
			- Because they were afraid that the army of Sitalces, allied with Athens, might descend upon them.
		- Why did the Athenians send a fleet of ships to Mytilene?
			- Because they were afraid Mytilene would form an alliance with a neighboring state.
		- Why did the Athenians tell the Melians they must side with Athens and cannot be neutral?
			- Because the Athenians feared not only that they would look weak in the eyes of other city-states, but that Sparta might force the Melians to ally with them.
- If anarchy (absence of authority above city-states) and the shift in the [[balance of power]] are critical factors in explaining the outbreak of the Peloponnesian War, what about other factors?
	- Does it matter whether city-states were more [[authoritarian]] or [[democratic]]?
	- What about human nature or other factors related to individuals or the groups they form?
- Thucydides doesn't consider those factors as underlying causes for the war
	- doesn't emphasize the internal makeup of city-states
- Thucydides's pessimism of human nature
	- on Corcyraean civil war: "love of power, operating through greed and through personal ambition, was the cause of all these evils"
##### 2.3.3.2 Immediate Causes of War
- case study: civil war in Corcyra
	- Corcyra was a colony of Corinth, but Corcyra prospered and became increasingly independent
	- Corcyra vs. Corinth over colony of Epidamnus
		- Corcyraean victory
		- Corinth plans major military operation
	- Corcyra –> Athens for alliance
	- Corinth revenge vs. Athens for breaking truce between Spartan and Athenian leagues
- case study: Potidaea, Athens vs. Corinth
	- Potidaea was a colony of Corinth, but also tribute-paying allies of Athens
	- Athenian fear of Corinth revolt via Potidaea
		- Athens demands Potidaean fortifications be torn down, hostages sent to Athens
	- Corinth + Potidaea –> Sparta for alliance
		- Sparta pledges to help if they're attacked
		- Potidaea + other city-states revolt against Athens
- Sparta votes Athens broke peace treaty –> war
	- Sparta + allies spent time preparing for upcoming conflict and made charges against Athens
		- "so that there should be a good pretext for making war"
	- Athens does the same
- Spartan fear of the further growth of Athenian power was the underlying cause of conflict
- argument on underlying cause of WWI
	- rise to power of Germany and the fear it caused UK, France, Russia
	- power decline of Austro-Hungarian Empire
	- power increase of Russia losing to Japan (1905)
	- most important immediate cause of war: assassination of Austro-Hungarian Archduke Franz Ferdinand (Sarajevo, June 1914)
- Thucydides: both underlying and immediate causes must be taken into account in explaining why the war occurred when it did
- [[Man, the State, and War (1959) by Kenneth Waltz]]
	- underlying cause of war: anarchy
		- nothing to stop states from going to war *is* the cause of war
		- more "efficient" causes are responsible for the outbreak of particular wars
- Thucydides – system-level explanation
	- identifying the increase in Athenian power relative to Sparta + other city-states as underlying cause of war
	- deterministic – statesmen have little control over int'l events in the face of basic trends as changes in the [[balance of power]]
- Thucydides' narrative undercuts the deterministic "war was inevitable due to the rise of Athenian power"
	- 454 BCE – Athenian military expedition to Egypt destroyed at cost of ~250 ships, ~50k men
	- 452 BCE – revolts within Athens
	- 446 BCE – Athenian defeat at Coronea
	- 449 BCE – Athens seeks peace with Persia
	- 446 BCE – Athens concludes Thirty Years' Peace agreement with Sparta
		- treaty cost Athens most of its continental empire
		- treaty required Athens to withdraw from Megarid, strategic land route between Attica (southeastern Greece) and Peloponnese peninsula
	- 431 BCE – war breaks out, 25 years after Athenian power peaked
- extended debates among various participants concerning what policies a state should follow
	- different decisions were possible, different outcomes could have occurred
- warning future decision makers about reaching the strategic miscalculations committed by the participants he observed by providing cautionary tales
	- if shifts in [[balance of power]] make war inevitable, what would be the purpose of providing cautionary tales to policy makers?
	- one of Thucydides goals
#### 2.3.4 Cautionary Tales: Lessons Drawn by Thucydides from the Peloponnesian War
##### 2.3.4.1 Be Wary of Wars in Distant Lands
- Athens vs. Sparta ends in stalemate
	- stalemate can end if Sicilian states (Sparta allies) are defeated
	- Athens was "ignorant of the size of the island and the number of its inhabitants"
- Athens declares war on Syracuse (principal Sicilian city-state)
- "be wary when deciding to engage in wars in distant lands"
- Athenian desire to break military stalemate similar to other points in history:
	- June 1941, WWII – German desire to break stalemate by invading USSR
	- American involvement in Vietnam
	- 1979 – Soviet invasion of Afghanistan
	- Douglas MacArthur warns U.S should be wary of engaging in land war in Asia
	- after Al-Qaeda attacks on September 11, 2001 – US troops to Afghanistan
	- March 2003 – US invasion of Iraq ([[Iraq War]]/[[Second Gulf War]])
- **Nicias** – Athenian to head the Sicilian expedition
	- favored nonintervention – Athens shouldn't trust its "allies" in Sicily who had their own reason for seeing Athens "drawn into a war that doesn't concern us"
		- Athens shouldn't divide its forces, running risks, "grasping at a new empire before we have secured the one we have already", shouldn't underestimate difficulties with governing rebellious foreigners
	- Athens should spend resources on "home and ourselves" instead of those "begging for assistance and shows interests it is to tell lies"
		- "leave all the dangers to others"
- Sometimes best foreign policy is to do nothing
	- putting reputation to the test against a distant power could have disastrous consequences if anything goes wrong
	- be wary of those who spew patriotic platitudes, but wish to go to war for their selfish reasons
		- runs the risk of "endangering the state in order to live a brilliant life"

- **Alcibiades** – Athenian commander
	- pro-expedition, looked down on Sicilians and viewed them as weak and submissive
	- not intervening risks losing the entire existing empire
		- state and society become a virtual living organism impelled to expand or wither and die (think agar.io)
- classic case of wishful thinking: Athenians agreed with Alcibiades instead of Nicias, committing Athens to a two-front war
- expedition takes place, little went as planned
	- "pro-Athens" city-states that were supposed to aid Athens remained neutral
- **Lamachus**
	- fleet should go straight to Syracuse and begin war because "it is at the beginning that every army inspires most fear; but if time is allowed to pass before it shows itself, men's spirits revive"
	- if Athenians attacked with overwhelming force, Sicilians might ally with Athens instead of Syracuse
- Athens delays military ops, Syracuse gains confidence
	- Athenian morale deteriorates
	- Nicias requests Athens to surrender or send another force
- Athens sends reinforcements
- Corinth and Sparta escalate commitment to war effort in Sicily
	- Athens in total mobilization, increase in draft, taxes raised
- Athens defeated
	- Nicias put to death, Alcibiades defected to Sparta
	- army tried to escape overland, cut to pieces
	- POWs diseased, hungry, thirsty
##### 2.3.4.2 Dangers of a Punitive Peace
- How should the victor deal with the vanquished (the powerful with the weak)?
- earlier case studies before Athens defeat
	- Athens vs. Sparta in Pylos (45 miles from Sparta)
		- Athens fortified, Sparta viewed as threat
		- Spartan force went across Pylos
		- Athens fleet removed troops on land, fought Spartan navy, cut off Spartan troops
		- Spartan reps sent to Athens to suggest a treaty to end the war
		- two insightful observations:
			1. why is it difficult to predict the course of int'l events?
				- Sparta: no matter how carefully one calculates, the course of war is impossible to predict
			2. if an opponent is forced "to carry out the terms of an unequal treaty," a thirst for revenge will result
				- the victor should make "peace on more moderate terms than his enemy expected," reducing the desire for revenge instead of placing the vanquished "under an obligation to pay back good for good"
- case study: handling of a defeated Germany after WWI and WWII
	- punitive Versailles Treaty (1919)
		- fueled spirit of revenge in Germany, contributed to rise of Hitler and Nazis who used the public discontent
	- post-WWII – US< UK, French treatment of West Germany
		- provision of economic aid stabilized the country
		- undercut political extremism
		- encouraged development of democracy
- Thucydides: most effective leaders are those who recognize long-term interests of the state are better served by not pursuing short-term advantages to the bitter end
##### 2.3.4.3 War Fever and a Fickle Public
- catalysts to conflict
	- strong public support –> dismay and search for scapegoats
		- older generations don't like war, affected by seeing previous wars
		- fosters overconfidence and belief that the war will be short
- classic case of war fever, [[jingoism]]
- initial public enthusiasm for war is a common phenomenon in int'l politics
	- case study: WWI
		- even European social democratic parties + trade unions that previously were pacifist were caught up in war fever
			- assumption: if the vast working classes of all countries refused to fight, war would be impossible since there's no troops to fight
			- reality: young men hurried to join the military
				- motivation: patriotism, missing out on adventure, youthful enthusiasm
- Thucydides: war fever is an important domestic contributor to an aggressive foreign policy
	- this behavior isn't restricted to any single type of political system
#### 2.3.5 Thucydides and IR Theory
- who would be interested in Thucydides
	- realist: if one is interested in war and conflict
	- economic structuralist: if one is interested in political and class conflict within states
	- if one is interested in the role of justice, norms, values in IR
	- social constructivist: if one is interested in critical use of dialogues that frame security issues and influence policies through words
##### 2.3.5.1 State as Principal Actor
- city-states: Athens and Sparta + allies
- non-state actors: mercenaries, Oracle at Delphi (insignificant)
- shift in relative power between two most powerful states that accounted for outbreak of Peloponnesian War
##### 2.3.5.2 State as Unitary Actor
- viewing the state as a unified or unitary actor?
	- would democratic Athens, whose decisions depended among the people, count as a unified actor?
- Thucydides: democratic Athens was a unitary city-state, allowing one to speak of an "Athenian policy"
- fractious domestic politics (civil wars) had impact on Greek city-state system
- domestic + external politics are linked
##### 2.3.5.3 Rational Reconstruction
- Thucydides: people are rational beings
	- assumption: through recounting arguments and debates, a state can reach the decision in its best interests
- Thucydides: political leaders must place themselves in the position of rival statesmen to anticipate what they might do 
	- empathetic analysis: one pursues in one's own interest
- Given a certain situation with various constraints and opportunities, what would I do?
	- rational reconstructionist: one should be wary of taking intelligence reports at face value and shouldn't use them "as a basis for calculating probabilities but consider what a clever and widely experienced people the Athenians would be likely to do"
##### 2.3.5.4 Limitations of the Rationality Assumption: Outcomes
- practical utility of rationality assumption when one wishes to divine the intentions of a rival
- foreign policy decision-making processes generally attempt to consider pros and cons of various suggested actions
- Does such rational calculation guarantee a good outcome? No
##### 2.3.5.5 Limitations of the Rationality Assumption: Perception
- Thucydides intended *The Peloponnesian War* to be an illustration of the limits of rationality
	- cognitive limitations of rational decision-making
- a state enhancing its security can breed suspicion and result in an unanticipated outcome
	- other states might follow similar logic, increasing tension and suspicion
- **John Herz:** ***[[security dilemma]]*** – steps taken to enhance a state's security may result in countermeasures taken by opponents, the net effect reducing or undermining the security of the state that initiated the action
	- arms races: what one state deploys militarily is countered by another state(s) with new/more offensive/defensive weapons systems
- 3 faulty perceptions of *History of the Peloponnesian War*:
	1. Decision makers perceive the enemy to be more centralized and coordinated in its decision decision-making process than it actually is.
	2. Those in authority experience "cognitive closure" as evidenced by a belief that few, if any, alternatives are open to them.
	3. Leaders engage in "wishful thinking."
- social scientists claim all 3 contribute to flawed assessments of rivals, undercuts rational decision-making process, increases possibility of war during crisis
##### 2.3.5.5 Perception of Centralization
- rational reconstruction: placing oneself in the mind of an opponent to discern what's the most likely course of action the rival will take
- trade-off: perceiving the opponent's decision-making more centralized and coordinated than it actually is
	- divisions and bureaucratic politics may be downplayed
	- rivals viewed as clever, resourceful, and in complete control of its decision-making process
- coincidences, accidents, unintended consequences viewed with suspicion or dismissed
- corollary: placing oneself in the mind of an ally –> benefit of the doubt, downplaying volition, emphasizing environmental factors beyond ally's control to explain behavior
##### 2.3.5.6 Cognitive Closure
- Thucydides: pessimistic about humans' ability to control their fates
	- in his debate recordings, many leaders made the basic assumption that there were few (if any) choices open to them
- situations seemed characterized more by determinism than one in which human agency can be exercised effectively
- premature **[[cognitive closure]]**: failure to search seriously for alternative policies
	- decision makers exhibit *resignation* ([[fatalism]])
- dangerous phenomenon in crises of stress and time pressure
	- case studies leading to WWI:
		- armies of USSR, France, Germany, Austria-Hungary, UK mobilized
			- military leaders told politicians it was impossible to stop the process
			- events took on their own logic
- **[[inherent bad faith model]]:** no matter what a state may do, its actions are interpreted in the worst possible light because images, once developed, are highly resistant to change
	- "kinder" actions are viewed by the decision-maker as a ruse to get them to drop their guard
	- evidence that may undermine the hostile image is rejected
- corollary: tendency of decision makers to assume their own benign intentions are self-evident to an adversary
	- when rivals fail to respond favorably to a "mutually" beneficial initiative, assumption: bad faith reinforced
##### 2.3.5.7 Wishful Thinking
- wishful thinking and self-delusion undercuts attempts at achieving a rational decision-making process
- leaders tend to reject unsettling information
- wishful thinking happens to those in fear AND confident powers
	- due to inappropriate historical analogies
		- 1941: Japan thought a limited war with the US was possible b/c Japan previously defeated USSR in a limited war
		- Hitler assumed France and UK wouldn't fight for Poland since they didn't fight for Austria or Czechoslovakia
- wishful thinking based on arrogance
	- Alcibiades' defense of the Sicilian expedition
	- dismisses Nicias's concerns over enemies closer to home
- Thucydides recognized limitations of rational discourse and the fallibility of human beings
	- still believed people were essentially rational and able to perceive alternative courses of action
	- extreme emotional stress, cognitive blindness to realities, faulty reasoning undercuts rational decision-making process
##### 2.3.5.8 Security as the Most Important Interest
- prime interest of all states = security
- security includes: military, productive economy/society with a strong will to resist foreign intrusion
- states can enhance security:
	1. internal efforts to increase its capabilities
	2. external means by joining alliance
- by pooling resources, the security of each state will be enhanced at a lower cost than if each state attempts to provide its own security on a unilateral basis
##### 2.3.5.9 Alliances
- How are alliances formed and what's their relation to the onset of war?
- Thucydides: states may join alliances due to compulsion, interest, moral principal, cultural/racial affinity
	- critical factor: security interest of city-states
- hypotheses of allies maximizing national security interests:
	1. [[balancing]]
		- states join an alliance to oppose other states that are perceived to be a threat
		- the greater the threat, the larger/stronger the alliance
		- case study: Corcyra seeking protection from Athens against Corinth
		- stronger states/empires might pursue [[balancing]] to prevent the rise of a major power
	2. [[bandwagoning]]
		- faced with an external threat, states will accommodate and ally with the threatening state to avoid being attack/share in victory
		- states might pursue counterproductive policies to prevent alliance defections and loss of neutrals to a rival's alliance
		- case study: Athens' preoccupation with defections from Delian League
			- Athens worried how defections would be viewed by allies and enemies
				- if one ally defects, the others might as well
		- case study: [[USSR]] and [[Warsaw Pact]] during [[Cold War]]
			- alliance directed against an external threat AND organizational expression of an imperial power
- fear of rival makes *[[balancing]]* the policy norm, but concern with [[bandwagoning]] tendencies by some city-states preoccupied leaders of the Athenian and Spartan alliances
- *compulsion* instead of *common interest* held an alliance together
	- *compulsion* was the primary means to maintain an alliance, not feelings of loyalty to allies or fear of Persia
- Delian League
	- fragile alliance and constant specter of defection
		- Mytilene had its own regional ambitions and revolted from Athens when Athens was suffering from plague and war exhaustion
- fear of defection
	- concern for leaders of alliances based more on *compulsion* than *common interest*
		- leader's obsession with defection may result in counterproductive policies that encourage allies to defect
		- case study: Athens' control of Potidaea
			- Athens tells Potidaea to pull down fortifications, provide hostages, banish Corinthian magistrates
			- Potidaeans ask Sparta for aid AND sent delegation to Athens
			- Potidaea uses the Spartan support to revolt, Athens wars against Potidaea
	- concern for states leading alliances based more on *coercion* than *common interest*
		- Athens believes any perception of Athens being weak or indecisive results in alliance defections
- neutrals can affect alliances adversely when they gain leverage from threatening to ally with one's adversaries
	- Thucydides: neutrals are susceptible to [[bandwagoning]] when it's clear who's the victor
- leaders of alliances have to be wary of manipulation by smaller allies who have a different security agenda
	- wars begin in small places
- states call upon their alliance leaders for support in regional disputes
- leaders of alliances faced with two trade-offs with allies:
	1. policy of capitulation (surrendering)
	2. confrontation
- if leader backs an ally, the ally may want to pursue its own regional ambitions
- leader's worry about state's reputation and coercing allies into an alliance could encourage alliance defections
- Thucydides: leaders need to balance justice, honor, and state interests
- relation between justice and power
	- Do states have rights or is power ultimately the decisive factor?
	- Do the strong do what they will and the weak what they must?
- case study: Belgium in WWI and WWII
	- Belgium tried to be neutral, but suffered invasion by Germany
	- Int'l law on Belgium's side – as a neutral, Belgium had a right to stay out of the conflict
- Thucydides: security should be the primary interest of leaders
- Thucydides: morality and power should not be divorced from one another
- **E. H. Carr:** argued for a [[balance of power]] and moral considerations in IR
#### 2.3.6 Reflections on Thucydides
- *History of the Peloponnesian War* provides analytical insights, cautionary tales concerning war and peace, contributed to later development of realist theories and IR theory
- Thucydides: pessimistic view of human nature
	- war brings out the worst in people
	- though one can be pessimistic about human nature, circumstances also matter
- Thucydides: options other than war or confrontation ere discussed and debated by leaders and citizens of Athens and Sparta
	- other policies could've been pursued
- Thucydides: allowance for free will on the part of leaders
	- leaders can make a great deal of difference
- Thucydides: extremely difficult to foresee how various factors come together to produce a certain outcome
	- whatever the outcome, it's the result of actions taken by humans
- Thucydides: morality and power should not be divorced from one another
	- should hold during times of war, if for no other reason than a state may later be in a disadvantageous position and wish to be treated with compassion
