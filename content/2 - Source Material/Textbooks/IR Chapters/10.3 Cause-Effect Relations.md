#### 10.3.0 Cause-Effect Relations
- 5 patterns of cause-effect relations in positivist theorizing
	1. There can be a single causal/independent variable (X) that can be used to explain/predict an effect on another variable (Y) that depends causally upon/reacts to variations in (X)
		- X –> Y
			- e.g. when our hypotheses holds that int'l tensions (X) are causally related to the outbreak of war (Y)
	2. Sometimes an intervening variable (Z) comes between the independent variable (X) and dependent variable (Y), moderating/altering the effect that (X) otherwise would've had on (Y)
		- X –> Z –> Y
			- e.g. when our hypothesis holds that int'l tensions (X) that can lead to war are exacerbated when policy elites (Z) with a militant orientation come to power or, conversely, reduced when peace-prone policy elites oriented toward conflict resolution take office, the former are more like to go to war (Y) than the latter.
				- the intervening variable (war- or peace-oriented policy elites come to power) matters causally
	3. In other cases, the independent variable (X) is itself the result of/caused by some third variable (Z) in what effectively is a "developmental sequence"
		- Z –> X –> Y
			- e.g. when our hypothesis holds that whether war or peace-oriented elites (Z) come to power has a causal effect on the level of int'l tensions causally related to the outbreak of war
				- war-oriented policy elites tending to increase tensions leading to war
				- peace-oriented elites tending to reduce tensions or create a climate more propitious to peace
	4. Sometimes we see dual/double causes as when (X) and (Z) are both causally related to (Y), still the dependent variable
		- X –> Y <– Z
			- e.g. when our hypothesis holds that int'l tensions (X) and orientations of policy elites in power (Z) separately are causally related to war (Y)
				- int'l tensions can cause war or policy elites on their own choose to go to war quite apart from whether the climate of relations (X) is one of high or reduced tensions
				- an extension of double cause is the case of *multiple* causation involving 3 or more independent variables in this case as causally related to the outbreak of war
	5. Looking to the outcome side, dual/double effect occurs when (X) is causal to both (Y) and (Z)
		- Y <– X –> Z
			- e.g. when our hypothesis holds that the climate of relations or level of int'l tensions (X) may produce a greater/lesser likelihood of going to war (Y) and, at the same time, influence whether war-like/peace-oriented policy elites (Z) come to power
				- as with multiple causation, we can also extend this reasoning to cases of multiple effects involving 3 or more dependent variables
				- dual/multiple effect is also the "spurious correlation" case in which the apparent association/correlation between (Y) and (Z) is due only to the fact both are affected causally by the same variable (X)
					- variations in (Y), likelihood of the outbreak of war, and (Z), orientations of policy elites toward war or peace, are each due to variations in (X), int'l tensions
- [[logical positivism]]
	- 1930s: [[Epistemology]] [[Empiricism]] adopted by scholars in the "Vienna circle"
		- extreme [[rationalism]], scientific form
	- pursuit of a pure science to separate fact from value and achieve precision of mathematics
	- members of the Vienna circle
		- Moritz [[Schlick]] (1882 – 1936), Otto [[Neurath]] (1882 – 1945), Rudolf [[Carnap]] (1891 – 1970)
		- influenced by earlier works of [[Wittgenstein]] (1889 – 1951)
			- [[Tractus Logic-Philosophicus (1921) by Ludwig Wittgenstein]]
				- relates the thought and ideas we have to the words we use, focusing on necessary logic and precision of language applied to observations about the world
		- sought logical precision and clarity in scientific language
- [[Karl Popper]] (1902 – 1994)
	- mid-1930s: addressed empirical tests of hypotheses drawn from theories
	- it's impossible to "prove" empirically that a certain hypothesis/universal proposition is true
		- would require infinite number of tests
	- to be scientific, claims/propositions have to be stated in falsifiable form
		- if a proposition if false, it must be possible empirically to show that it's false
		- therefore, one can accept a falsifiable proposition as possibly true until one actually shows it to be false (scientifically or by experiment)
- [[Post-WWII]]: watered-down application of positivist premises to social sciences
	- incorporation of statistical methodologies and use of mathematical equations to specify causal relations among variables
		- critical reaction to [[Modernism]] [[Epistemology]]
	- Carl [[Hempel]] (1905 – 1997)
		- *deductive-nomological* schema for scientific explanation
			- formalized deductive approach in formulation of universal and probabilistic lawlike statements
			- *covering-law* approach to theory
				- still the preferred choice of many scholars working within positivist framework
	- Thomas S. [[Kuhn]] (1922 – 1996)
		- identification of "scientific revolutions" focused on natural sciences
		- *paradigms*/frameworks of understanding influence the way we observe and make sense of the world around us
			- Kuhn's [[relativism]], direct challenge to positivist school
				- critics: knowledge is understood to be empirically grounded and not so arbitrary as to be based on such pre-existing/newly discovered frameworks of understanding
		- doesn't reject empirically based claims
			- argument: when theories and component concepts associated with a particular paradigm are challenged empirically or theoretically, holders of this paradigm may be forced through some modification to accommodate the new finding or insight or give way to a new paradigm
				- e.g. Ptolemy's idea of Earth as center of the universe (grounded in religion) –> Copernicus's revolution of the heliocentric model (grounded in empiricism)
		- debate in social sciences: whether these fields are developed sufficiently to justify the identification of *paradigms*
			- important point in [[Kuhn]]'s thesis: knowledge, even in the natural sciences, is grounded in human understanding in the form of paradigms that influence observation and construction of concepts and theories
				- such interpretive understanding or agreed meaning would seem to apply to the social sciences that deal with human behavior in all its forms
		- work receives criticism from positivists, but he doesn't abandon scientific premises
			- his argument comes from within the scientific/positivist community
			- no rejection of science, but his work on paradigms has influenced interpretive understanding
	- [[Einstein]] (1879 – 1955)
		- [[Theory of Relativity]]
			- challenges [[Newton]]'s (1642 – 1727) understanding of gravity and laws of motion
			- reduces Newtonian mechanics paradigm to Earth-based observable laws
				- not as directly applicable to macro-universal domain of astrophysics or micro-domain concerning motion of subatomic particles in quantum mechanics
	- Stephen [[Toulmin]] (1922 – 2009)
		- argument: the lenses one wears affect science and its progress in the continuing question to make the world around us more intelligible
			- we need to scrutinize closely and critically the principal images that currently inform much IR theory
		- his critique within the positivist, natural science discourse
			- against any claim to the idea that scientific work can ever be value-free
			- we must take into account the subjective dimensions that influence our inquiries
				- helps keep us from errors to be found in the logic of the arguments we make or the evidence we use to buttress our truth claims
	- Imre [[Lakatos]] (1922 – 1974)
		- pursuit of science in the spawning of multiple research programs and ensuing competition among them (positive)
			- not as a series of paradigmatic revolutions
		- progress in IR is facilitated by development of research programs
			- changes in research programs occur only in the fullness of time
			- one falsification isn't enough
			- bases for such a research program change doesn't become established overnight, but rather as a part of a progressive process over time
