#### 13.5.0 Armed Intervention and State Sovereignty
- the issue of armed intervention
	- where traditional concerns with the laws of war blend with concerns over human rights
	- 1928: Pact of Paris ([[Kellogg-Briand Pact]])
		- an unsuccessful attempt to eliminate use of force in IR
		- supposed to help outlaw "recourse to war for the solution of int'l controversies"
			- world pace through law in a system of [[collective security]] under the [[League of Nations]]
				- alliances/coalitions that rely on armed defense/military power rather than int'l law enforcement against aggressor states
	- [[League of Nations]]
		- tried to substitute law-abiding behavior for individual and [[collective defense]] relations based on [[power]], [[balance of power]], and military might
			- law-abiding states under [[collective security]] arrangements enforce int'l law against law-breaking states
		- powerless to counter aggressive actions
			- e.g. [[France]] intervention in [[Germany]]
			- e.g. 1923: [[Italy]] capture of Mediterranean island Corfu
			- e.g. 1931: outbreak of [[China]]-[[Japan]] war
			- e.g. 1932 – 1935: [[Bolivia]]-[[Paraguay]] [[Chaco war]]
			- e.g. 1935: [[Italy]] invasion of [[Ethiopia]]
			- e.g. 1938: [[Germany]]'s annexation of [[Austria]] and [[Czech Republic]]
			- e.g. 1939: outbreak of [[WWII]]
	- [[United Nations]] Charter (1945)
		- specifies conditions under which force may legally be used
			1. unilaterally in self-defense
			2. multilaterally when authorized by the UN Security Council "to maintain or restore int'l peace and security"
			3. in multilateral, often regional, collective defense action as, e.g. [[NATO]]
	- armed interventions still occur frequently enough
		- sometimes justified by participants serving humanitarian purposes or a measure to maintain/restore int'l peace and [[security]]
			- a broad grant of legal authority for UN-sponsored actions
		- normally prohibited under int'l law in a world of sovereign states
	- states that have suffered violation of their legal rights may choose arbitration, mediation, or judicial remedy offered by [[ICJ]] or appropriate regional/national court
		- critical weakness: these tribunals don't have enforcement powers
		- practically, states resort to [[self-help system]] to include the use of force
		- in [[anarchy]] world that lacks a central gov't or other governing authority with power to enforce int'l law, sovereign states don't always comply with such legal authorizations and restrictions
		- states sometimes choose to violate/ignore their obligations under int'l law
			- political leaders and diplomats can interpret/manipulate legal principles to justify what they've already done/plan to do in any event
#### 13.5.1 Intervention and Civil Wars
- applying int'l law is more complicated when conflict is internal to a particular state and society (civil war)
	- internal wars are the most likely threat to int'l peace and security
	- difficult to contain civil wars within borders of the affected state
		- can spill beyond their borders and become interstate wars
- even when motives are legit, interventions in domestic affairs of sovereign states conflict with a long-established principle of int'l law that prohibits them
	- e.g. [[American Civil War]]
		- debate in [[UK]] as to whether Britain should support the South
		- southern states claim [[sovereignty]] as the [[Confederacy]] and sought outside assistance in their struggle against [[America]], from which they claimed to be separate
		- [[Lincoln]] administration denies South's claim
			- argument: southern states had no right to secede from the Union in the first place
			- it wasn't a war between sovereign states, but rather a civil war between loyal US armed forces and loyal rebellious states
			- stressed to UK that intervention is illegal and UK decided to not intervene
- determining the difference between an interstate war and civil war is difficult
	- e.g. 1960s [[Vietnam War]]
		- American armed intervention justified by US as coming to defense of South Vietnam against aggression from North Vietnam
			- if this was correct, going to aid a victim of aggression was legitimate under int'l law
			- if the situation in Vietnam was understood as a civil war (single state torn between two rival gov'ts and an insurgent movement tied to one of the parties), outside intervention in an internal matter wouldn't have been legitimate under int'l law
		- forces of the war
			- North Vietnam, South Vietnam, US, [[Korea]], [[Australia]], guerrilla warfare supported by North Vietnam
				- capitalized North Vietnam's ties with people in the countryside
					- used anti-government and ideological appeals, knowledge of terrain, protective cover of jungle
				- regular and guerrillas conducted successful campaign against South Vietnamese gov't and its allies
				- guerrilla warfare: terrorism, ambushes, rocket attacks, firefights with regular forces
		- 1975: guerrillas that were part of an anti-gov't insurgent movement + North Vietnamese regulars eventually succeeded in winning the war against US + South Vietnamese gov't
	- e.g. [[Yugoslavia]]
		- early 1990s: Serbs opposed the secession of "breakaway republics" and their recognition by outside states as independent, sovereign states
		- Serb POV: the ensuing war among competing parties was a civil war precluding any legal right to intervention by outside parties
		- [[Croatia]], [[Slovenia]], [[Bosnia]]-Herzegovina recognized as separate, independent, sovereign states by UN members
			- seen by other observers as engaging in a war among states against [[Serbia]]
		- as an interstate war, outside intervention/other activities by UN, NATO, Organization of Security and Cooperation in Europe, other legitimate authorities acting in compliance with the UN Charter was presented as legitimate
#### 13.5.2 Criteria for Humanitarian Intervention
- humanitarian intervention can legally conflict with the principle of non-intervention in the domestic affairs of a state
	- absence of an invitation from the legit gov't of a state
	- a basis under customary int'l law may be emerging to give legal legitimacy to intervention intended to stop genocide/other human rights abuses
		- UN Charter doesn't give Security Council authority to use force for humanitarian purposes
		- armed intervention under UN auspices in internal affairs of a state is legitimate in this strict interpretation
			- only if the problem cannot likely be contained to the state in which it's occurring, posing a threat to int'l peace and security
- e.g. 1999: [[Kosovo]]
	- a province of [[Yugoslavia]]
	- Serbs state that whatever actions they took in the province were an internal matter and outside intervention was a violation of Yugoslavian sovereignty
	- Serbian policy of systematic ethnic cleansing led to [[NATO]] military action on the grounds of humanitarian intervention and claim that Serbian actions were a threat to regional peace and security
- humanitarian motives can be presented as a pretext used by political leaders and diplomats to justify armed interventions done exclusively for national interest reasons
	- propagandists like to present humanitarian purposes as a cover for armed interventions conducted for national interest purposes, making behavior seem less self-serving
		- leveled against Russia following 2022 [[Russia-Ukraine War]]
- [[post-Cold War]] didn't end armed intervention
	- e.g. [[Iraq War]] in relation to their takeover of [[Kuwait]]
	- e.g. civil strife in [[Somalia]] and [[Haiti]]
	- e.g. [[genocide]] in [[Balkan wars]] and central [[Africa]]
- post-9/11
	- regimes were overturned by interventions under NATO auspices by US, UK, coalition of other states
		- 2001: [[Afghanistan]]
		- 2003: [[Iraq]]
	- Iraq invasion justified on the presumption that they possessed WMDs
- policy makers decisions on whether to intervene with armed force as a response
	- aggression, prevent/stop genocide, restore order, maintain peace
	- e.g. [[Syria]] and [[Yemen]]
		- bombing and other abuses of populations
		- condemnation against perpetrators, but not a massive armed intervention
		- political factors ([[Russia]] involvement in Syria, [[Saudi Arabia]] in Yemen)
			- kept US and other countries from intervening militarily
			- Russian and American veto power kept the Security Council from taking significant action
				- Russians protective of [[Assad]] regime in Syria
				- US reluctant to take action against Saudi Arabia, which it was supporting militarily
- economic, military capabilities, domestic political support/opposition are part of decision-making calculus
	- political support for/opposition to armed intervention is expressed in terms of criteria:
		1. [[sovereignty]]
			- under int'l law, states are normally prohibited from intervention in domestic affairs of other sovereign states unless requested by the legitimate gov't of the state subject to such intervention
			- use of force (i.e. armed intervention) is allowed under UN Charter for collective security
				- when the Security Council authorizes using force in response to a contingency endangering int'l peace and security
			- self-defense/collective defense by alliances/coalitions of states is justified in responding to aggression against a sovereign state
		2. national interest
			- armed intervention is an option weighed against considerations of national interest and related nat'l security objectives
				- some argue armed intervention should be pursued only if there's a vital national interest to be served ([[Realism]])
					- no escaping practical difficulty in precise definition of what the nat'l interest might be in a particular case
					- nat'l interest subject to multiple interpretations
						- its ambiguity remains part of the decision-making calculus
			- when states act in self-defense or come to aid fo other states to repel aggression
				- claims legal legitimacy for acting in nat'l interest
			- not all interventions taken to advance nat'l interest meet this self-defense criterion and amount to acts of aggression
		3. human rights
			- currently a consensus forming that humans have rights that may supersede rights claimed by sovereign states
				- [[Universal Declaration of Human Rights]]
					- rests on increasing understanding and acceptance of respect for life, human dignity, and justice/fairness as universal ethical/moral principles that have global application to individuals, groups, other categories/classes of humans
			- unilateral and multilateral voluntary assistance for relief in natural disasters
				- manifestations of human rights principles in action
			- enormous human and material cost suffered by victims of mass destruction and atrocities –> growth in int'l law
				1. define certain civil/political, social, economic rights
				2. prohibit certain acts defined as war crimes, genocide, other crimes against peace and humanity
			- human rights violations when they endanger int'l peace and security
				- clearer legal ground for humanitarian, armed intervention under UN Security Council auspeices
		4. expected net effect on the human condition
			- armed intervention has real costs (people, property, armed forces)
			- difficult to precisely quantify the net effect (benefits minus costs) on the human condition even after an armed intervention has occurred
				- e.g. human psychological damage
			- this criterion in minds of policy makers contemplating whether armed intervention will improve or worse the human condition
			- expected net effect on human condition can influence how an armed intervention is implemented
			- policy makers may select options expected to minimize/reduce adverse consequences to both armed forces and the people subject to their actions
		5. degree of [[multilateralism]]
			- unilateral armed intervention has come increasingly into disfavor
			- policy makers more prone to look for multilateral support and cooperation in conducting armed interventions
				- e.g. UN Security Council mandates provide political and legal ground for proceeding
			- proceeding multilaterally as a collective defense response is still viewed by most policy makers as politically preferable to unilateral action
				- explains why [[Bush]] administration sought as UN Security Council resolution in 2002 requiring Iraq to readmit weapons inspectors
				- [[Colin Powell]] (Bush's Secretary of State) talks to UN Security Council on alleged Iraqi WMDs
- criteria competes with each other
	- choices concerning how much weight to give to one over the other
	- analytical framework that specifies factors that are part of decisions o engage in armed intervention
	- states usually intervene to serve their interests, but don't always do so
		- may be genuine for humanitarian reasons or contributes to restoration of int'l peace and security
			- consistent with their broad interests
			- e.g. 1999: [[NATO]]'s intervention of [[Kosovo]] 
		- states may weigh costs and benefits of armed intervention/how well they serve the human condition
- use of force for humanitarian purposes may cause more bloodshed than if no intervention took place
	- in other cases the reverse is true
		- armed intervention at relatively low cost may succeed in providing greater security and meeting human needs
		- difficulty: expected net costs or benefits to humans aren't always easy to estimate accurately
