#### 6.8.0 Neoliberal Institutionalism
- [[neoliberal institutionalism]]
	- [[regime theory (neoliberal institutionalism)]]
		- 1980s: developed by [[Keohane]]
		- believed "[[regime theory]]" too limiting of a term to describe his approach to conditions under which int'l cooperation can be achieved
		- developed broader concept of "institutions"
			- institutions: "persistent and connect sets of rules (formal and informal) that prescribe behavioral roles, constrain activity, and shape expectations"
- 3 forms of int'l institutions
	1. **formal intergovernmental** or **cross-national, nongovernmental organizations**
		- purposive entities
		- bureaucratic orgs with explicit rules and missions
			- e.g. [[United Nations]] - explicit rules
			- e.g. [[Red Cross]] (ICRC) - explicit missions
	2. **international regimes**
		- institutionalized rules explicitly agreed upon by gov'ts that deal with a particular set of issues
			- e.g. 1944: int'l monetary regime
				- adapted to changing circumstances since then
			- e.g. 1970s: the [[Law of the Sea]] regime
			- e.g. [[Cold War]]: various arms control agreements between [[America]] and [[USSR]]
	3. **conventions**
		- informal institutions (or customary norms and practices) with implicit rules and understandings
			- allows actors to understand one another and coordinate their behavior
			- facilitates coordinations
			- affects actors' incentives not to defect in those situations where at least in the short term it might be in their interest to do so
			- e.g. "reciprocity"
				- political leaders expect reciprocal treatment in int'l dealings, both positive and negative, and anticipate costs of one kind or another if they violate the convention
			- e.g. diplomatic immunity
				- 1960s: codified in formal agreements
- [[Keohane]]'s [[neoliberal institutionalism]] formulation isn't restricted to formal orgs and regimes
	- a counter within [[Liberalism]] to what some perceive as intellectual [[hegemony]] of [[structural realism]] or [[neorealism]] writings in [[IR]]
	- "variations in the institutionalization of world politics exert significant impacts on the behavior of governments"
		- "patterns of cooperation and discord can be understood only in the context of the institutions that help define the meaning and importance of state action"
- [[Power and Interdependence – World Politics in Transition (1977) by Robert O. Keohane and Joseph Nye Jr.]]
	- their first attempt at a theory of institutions
		- int'l organization model of regime change
- 1980s: subsequent work done against backdrop of rising challengers to the primacy of [[America]] in world politics
- [[After Hegemony (1984) by Robert O. Keohane]]
	- adopts [[Realism]] premises
		- desire to explain behavioral regularities in decentralized int'l system
		- epistemologically sensitive to the fact that while theories can and must be tested, it's naive to believe that reality can be objectively known
		- state power must be taken seriously
			- assumption that leaders of states calculate costs and benefits of contemplated courses of action
	- shares with [[neorealism]]
		- interest in applicability of [[IR]] of economic theories of market behavior
	- addresses puzzle of why even self-interested, rational egoists (individuals seeking to maximize gains) would pursue multilateral, cooperative behavior
- [[Keohane]] argues [[neoliberal institutionalism]] as a distinct school of thought
	- [[neorealism]] and [[neoliberal institutionalism]] agree that [[IR]]/world politics lack a stable hierarchy due to its [[Anarchic]]/decentralized character
	- [[neoliberalism]] are more emphatic that there's no necessary logical link between condition of [[anarchy]] and [[War]]
		- if a connection does exist between [[War]] and lack of harmony among states, it's conditional on the nature of prevailing expectations among actors to include those held by institutions
- [[Realism]]/[[neorealism]] claim that in condition of [[anarchy]], *relative gains* are more important than *absolute gains*
	- states concerned with preventing others from achieving advances in their relative capabilities
	- important question to [[Realism]]: who gained more gains?
		- even though 2 states may make material gains (e.g. enhancing military capabilities)
	- is the power gap widening?
	- e.g. [[America]]-[[USSR]] relations during [[Cold War]]
	- e.g. security implications of rising [[China]] and [[Russia]]
- [[neoliberalism]] counter
	- [[Realism]] understanding doesn't accurately describe [[America]]n policy toward [[Europe]] or [[Japan]]
		- where [[America]] actively promoted economic recovery and development
	- [[Europe]]
		- [[America]] pleaded for greater European defense spending for its [[NATO]] allies
			- even after [[Russia]]'s invasion of [[Ukraine]] in 2022
	- [[neorealism]] doesn't explain peaceful relations among members of [[EU]]
		- despite economic integration favoring some states more than others, members across the board for decades were willing to accept an **asymmetric distribution of *absolute gains***
			- some clearly gaining more than others
	- [[neoliberalism]] concedes that *absolute gains* argument may be more applicable in conditions where substantial mutual gains can be achieved AND when gov'ts don't expect others to threaten to use force against them
- conditionality for [[neoliberalism]]
	- function of prevailing rules and expectations
	- states use int'l institutions
		- performs important tasks that enhance cooperation for self-interested reasons
			- e.g. transaction costs (making, monitoring, enforcing rules) are reduced when institutions provide information to all parties and facilitate the making of credible commitments
		- what are the guarantors of compliance with the commitments made by states?
			- reputation
			- reciprocity
				- threats of retaliation
				- promises of reciprocal cooperation
- [[Keohane]]'s interest in trade, monetary, energy issues deals with material self-interest in which **reciprocity** plays a key role
	- argues in same framework
		- environmental issues
			- includes normative dimension and role of principled ideas
- [[Keohane]]'s work with Judith [[Goldstein]]
	- importance of ideas as a significant **independent variable** in explanations of foreign policy
	- worldviews, principled beliefs, causal beliefs
		- ideas that become embedded in institutions
		- impacts making of policy by acting as cognitive road maps
	- ideas define universe of possibilities for action
	- [[Keohane]]: "interests are incomprehensible without an awareness of the beliefs that lie behind them"
		- ideas shape agendas and directly affect outcomes
	- when ideas become institutionalized, they assume a life of their own as socially embedded norms
	- ideas linked to interests do influence the making of foreign policy choices
- [[neoliberalism]] (like [[Keohane]]) claim institutions and regimes matter because they enable states to do things they otherwise couldn't do
	- rising levels of [[interdependence]] and interconnectedness in world politics
		- hypothesis: states will likely rely more heavily on regimes for their own selfish reasons
- [[Realism]] views regimes as constraints on state behavior
- [[neoliberalism]] views regimes more positively as enabling states to achieve mutually beneficial outcomes
- [[Rousseau]]'s stag hunt ([[5.4 System]])
	- possibilities of upgrading common interest, despite underlying condition of [[anarchy]]
	- literature on int'l orgs, regimes, institutions in [[Liberalism]] offers insight into how states may accommodate differences and upgrade interests they share
	- consistent w/stag hunt, actors may agree to collaborate in certain circumstances in hunting the stag rather than serve only narrowly defined, short-term self-interest
	- *absolute gains* for all are possible
		- policy makers learn benefits of cooperation in areas of interest to them
