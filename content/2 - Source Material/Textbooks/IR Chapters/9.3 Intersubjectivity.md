#### 9.3.0 Intersubjectivity
- central to [[constructivism]]: understanding that int'l politics is guided by intersubjectively shared and *institutionalized* norms, rules, ideas, beliefs, and values held by actors (or agents)
	- *intersubjective*: shared by other people the ideational component of IR not simply being the total of the beliefs of individuals
		- defined by their person-to-person, self-other exchanges
	- *institutionalized*: collective ideas are established in the social world as structures or institutions, practices, and identities
	- shared norms and rules set expectations about how the world works and what constitutes legitimate behavior
- [[constructivism]]: normative or ideational factors or structures are more important than material structures (population size, weapons systems, manufacturing output, geographic factors – subject to interpretation)
- [[neorealism]], [[Economic Structuralism]], [[Liberalism]]
	- material constitutes the base line
	- ideas are derivative
	- [[neorealism]]: material objects (power defined as material capabilities) have a decisive impact on outcomes unmediated by the ideas people have concerning these objects
		- ideational factors are discounted
			- seen as variables dependent on their preferences of powerful states rather than having an impact independent of the int'l distribution of power among states
		- [[Kenneth Waltz]], [[Mearsheimer]]: distribution of material capabilities/power is key to understanding the conduct of states in IR
	- [[classical realism]]: incorporates int'l norms or rules in their analyses
		- also [[The English School]]
	- [[neoliberalism]]: focus on institutions
		- [[Keohane]], [[Goldstein]]
		- understands importance of ideas
		- material interests remain an important part of explaining outcomes driven by actors who make cost-benefit calculations (within or outside institutional settings)
	- [[constructivism]]: commitment to materially based understandings doesn't grant ideas the important standing they warrant as an independent variable or factor that shapes IR patterns and outcomes
- [[constructivism]]'s emphasis on intersubjectively shared and institutionalized norms and rules
	1. social construction of the concept of [[sovereignty]]
		- consists of a set of rules or standards of behavior guiding states interacting with one another
		- sovereign states come to claim under int'l law a right to complete jurisdiction over their territories
			- development of the int'l norm prescribing non-interference in internal affairs of other states
				- violated many times over the centuries
					- one country invading another
					- one country intervening another's domestic politics
				- claim to sovereign still remains
			- internal/domestic dimension
		- states claim a right to be independent or autonomous in the conduct of their foreign relations
			- external/int'l dimension
		- [[sovereignty]] *is* an intersubjectively shared and socially constructed institution/normative structure among states
		- [[Peace of Augsburg]] (1555) and [[Peace of Westphalia]] (1648)
			- emergence of [[sovereignty]] among [[Germany]] and other states as a convenient norm
				- became globalized in internal and external dimensions
			- placed authority in ruling princes, dukes, kings
			- [[sovereignty]] expressed through rules of behavior (diplomatic practices)
				- mutual understandings about providing order in the int'l system, stabilizing actors' expectations, managing power relations
			- development of norm of territorial integrity
				- reinforced norm of [[sovereignty]] by acknowledging the socially constructed sanctity of state boundaries			
		- violations of [[sovereignty]]
			- doesn't extinguish the norm
			- legitimacy is highlighted by victims of its violation
			- e.g. [[Estonia]], [[Latvia]], [[Lithuania]]
				- 1940: incorporated by force within [[USSR]]
				- idea that they were still sovereign states was kept alive
				- contributed to [[Baltic]] states re-emerging [[post-Cold War]]
			- e.g. 2022: [[Russia-Ukraine War]]
				- global consensus: Ukraine's sovereignty violated
		- intersubjective consensus on sovereign rights of states
			- partially weakened by other consensus
				- growing int'l support for human rights
				- a collective responsibility to do something about human rights violations
				- idea of universal *human* rights
					- encouraged and justified military intervention for humanitarian purposes
						- e.g. states violate human rights of minority groups or their citizens
						- e.g. prohibitions against genocide and inhumane treatment/torture of captive humans
				- widely accepted, intersubjective, socially constructed norms that erode claims to state sovereigny
					- if state decides that acts of genocide or other inhumane acts demand int'l diplomatic or armed intervention in domestic affairs of states, such actions run counter to rights claimed by sovereign states to exercise complete internal jurisdiction over their territories
		- states who have no particular strategic or economic value to the great powers
			- [[Realism]]: difficult to explain efforts by great powers to curb human rights violations when no material interests are at stake
			- [[constructivism]]: [[normative]] context shifted since [[Post-WWII]]
				- human rights have been accepted gradually as a widely held norm
			- concern for human rights has had broad pattern of effect in shaping many states' conceptions of interest
			- commitment to human rights as an idea matters
	2. [[slavery]] and slave trade were once accepted practices in IR (re: human rights)
		- [[America]] and [[Europe]]: slavery was an economic imperative
			- late-18th – 19th c.: slavery was an institution of critical importance to the economic interests of the slave-trading states (and owners) 
			- intersubjective consensus gradually breaks down
		- late-19th c.: domestic and int'l accepted norms against [[exploitation]] of humans became part of the fabric of rules prohibiting violations of human rights
		- various forms of human bondage still exist
			- [[social constructivism]]: global and national norms make such practices morally illegitimate and illegal
		- intersubjective norms and laws/rules associated with them can (and do) change, however slowly
	3. norms concerning legitimacy of [[War]] and warfare
		- early 20th c.: [[Europe]] engaged in [[WWI]] and [[WWII]] in defense of their respective national interests
			- [[Western Europe]]: creates zone of peace eventually
				- [[Germany]] and [[France]] used to be bitter rivals, resorting to use of force against each other in major wars
			- how can [[Realism]] explain these developments?
			- [[constructivism]]: intersubjective consensus emerges among elites, citizens, gov'ts, NGOs that force is no longer the appropriate way to resolve differences
				- peace as a value and normative commitment to maintaining it multilaterally has an impact on how states define their interests
- [[constructivism]]'s recognition that intersubjective understandings can vary across regions and over time
	- e.g. [[Western Europe]]
	- attempts to understand how shifts in collective meanings occur through detailed empirical research and rich description
		- how have actors viewed existing normative structures and rules of behavior?
		- how have alternative understandings been devised and propagated through state bureaucracies and int'l orgs, transnational NGOs, and advocacy networks?
	- e.g. [[post-Cold War]] decline of [[Communism]]
		- left intact the current globalized market system and associated [[Liberalism]] financial institutions, norms, rules by which states and non-state actors operate
	- [[The Great Recession]] – 2008 global financial crisis
		- alerted [[constructivism]] to possible weakening of this intersubjective, [[Capitalism]] consensus, opening door for states and non-state actors to begin modifying, adapting, constructing alternative to status quo
	- ideas about IR in global society don't exist in nature, but rather of human origin
		- [[social constructivism]]: socially constructed ideas
