#### 5.1.0 Major Actors and Assumptions
- what is [[Realism]]
	- an image and approach to [[IR]] based on 4 principal assumptions:
		1. states are the principal, most important actors in an anarchical world lacking central legitimate governance
			- states are key unit of analysis
			- study of IR is study of relations among states, specifically major powers as they shape world politics and engage in [[War]], armed interventions, economic actions, etc.
			- realists who use concept of *system* usually refer to an *int'l system* of states
			- what about non-state actors?
				- [[United Nations]] (int'l org) may want to be considered an independent actor, but realists don't view them as such
				- realists view int'l orgs as doing no more than their member states direct
				- MCNs (multinational corporations), terrorist groups, other transnational and nongov'tal orgs are frequently acknowledged by realists, but position of these non-state actors are insignificant
			- realists don't care about individuals
		2. state is viewed as a unitary actor
			- realists: state is encapsulated by a metaphorical hard shell/opaque, black box
				- we need not look much inside the shell/black box that figuratively contains domestic actions and interactions among diverse state and non-state actors
			- state is an integrated unit facing the outside world
			- political differences within the state are ultimately resolved authoritatively
				- the gov't of the state speaks with one voice for the state as a whole
			- state has one policy at any given time on any particular issue
			- gov'ts are merely agents of states
		3. state is a *rational* (purposive) actor
			- rational foreign policy decision-making process
				- statement of objectives
				- consideration of all feasible alternative in existing capabilities available to state
				- relative likelihood of attaining the objectives by alternatives under consideration
				- benefits/costs associated with each alternative
			- using rational process, gov'tal decision makers select alternative that maximizes *utility* or at least achieves an acceptable outcome
				- utility: maximizing benefit or minimizing cost associated with attaining the objectives sought
			- result in rank ordering of policy preferences among viable alternatives
			- realists aware of difficulties in viewing state as rational actor
				- decision makers without all factual information or knowledge of cause and effect required to make value-maximizing decisions
				- human bias
			- state as a rational actor is important in applying [[Game Theory]] and other rational-choice methods
				- [[deterrence theory]], arms control, [[balance of power]], use of force, economic advantage, etc.
		4. within hierarchy of issues facing the state, nat'l/int'l security is on top of the list
			- military and related political issues dominate world politics
			- realists focus on actual/potential conflict between state actors and use of force
				- examine how int'l stability is attained/maintained, how it breaks down, the utility of force as a means to resolve disputes, prevention of any violation of its territorial integrity
			- economic matters important in how they contribute to power or relative power of the state
			- with state's objectives, goals, purposes in regards to security, seeks to use *power*
				- power: material terms as capabilities relative to other states
			- realists' key concept: [[balance of power]]
				- [[structural realism]]/neorealist emphasizes security implications of the distribution of power of the int'l system of states
					- unipolar (one great power)
					- bipolar (two great powers)
					- multipolar (three or more great powers)
			- states use power to serve their interests and achieve their objectives
			- struggle for/use of power among states is the core of IR
				- [[Morgenthau]]: "International politics, like all politics, is a struggle for power. Whatever the ultimate aims of international politics, power is always the immediate aim or means to an end." #quote 
- on assumptions 2 and 3
	- image of unified, rational state is an *assumption*, not a description of actual world
		- used by realists to build theories, not describe reality
	- assumptions should be viewed in terms of how fruitful they are in generating insights an valid generalizations about int'l politics
	- assumptions are neither true or false, but helpful for theorists derive testable propositions/hypotheses about IR
	- once hypotheses are developed, they're tested in real world
	- image of unified, rational state is the starting point for realist analysis, not a concluding statement
- [[Realism]]'s connection to [[Game Theory]]
	- [[Game Theory]] is a realist example of use of such simplifying assumptions as an aid to develop hypotheses and theories about causes of various int'l political phenomena
	- works on [[deterrence theory]] and [[coercive diplomacy]] (compellence) use rational, unitary actor assumptions
	- rationality assumption is similarly central to *expected utility* models of int'l politics
	- rational choice is also part of theorizing associated with [[Liberalism]] image
- [[Realism]] as an image of politics
	- focuses on power and power politics among states
	- neorealists: [[Kenneth Waltz]], John J. [[Mearsheimer]], Christopher [[Layne]]
		- emphasize overall distribution of power among states
		- highly skeptical of extent to which int'l norms and int'l institutions can better competition among states
	- (neo)classical realists: [[Morgenthau]], [[E. H. Carr]], Arnold [[Wolfers]]
		- more inclusive approach
	- serious consideration of how factors at domestic/societal level of analysis influences iR
		- possible factors: impact of leaders, whether a state is revisionist or status-quo oriented, role of *norms* and institutions
	- adherence to basic realist assumptions can still result in different interpretations and theoretical applications based on these assumptions
