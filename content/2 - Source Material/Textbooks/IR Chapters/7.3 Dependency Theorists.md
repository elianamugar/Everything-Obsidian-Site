#### 7.3.1 ECLA and UNCTAD Arguments
- 1960s – 1970s: [[Latin American]] scholarship in [[Economic Structuralism]]
	- [[dependency]] theorists
		- 1960s: these writers associated with Economic Commission on Latin America ([[ECLA]]) and United Nations Conference on Trade and Development ([[UNCTAD]])
		- concerned with explaining why [[Latin American]] and [[Third World]] regions weren't developing as anticipated
			- [[North America]] models predicted economic takeoff for LDCs
			- what went wrong?
			- what explained economic stagnation, balance of payments difficulties, and deteriorating terms of trade?
			- why was [[North America]]-[[Western Europe]]'s experience not being repeated?
		- response: mainstream *[[modernization]]* literature
			- explores difficulties of LDCs in moving from "traditional" to "modern" societies 
				- tradition-modernity dichotomy used as a tool of analysis by social scientists since 19th c.
			- ethos and organization of traditional society are both a cause and an expression of underdevelopment
			- cultural values of traditional society are postulated as a hindrance to [[modernization]]
			- LDCs are wedded to their pasts, reflecting lack of entrepreneurial spirit found in [[Europe]] during rise of [[Capitalism]] in 16th c.
		- criticism of cultural values of traditional societies holding back LDCs' development
			- view of development and underdevelopment as outcomes of internal processes
			- criticism 1: [[modernization]] writers assume tradition-modernity dichotomy is universally applicable
				- is [[Latin American]] experience similar to [[Europe]]'s experience?
				- are there significant historical differences between [[Africa]] and [[Europe]]/[[America]]'s experiences?
					- yes, substantially - [[Colonialism]]
			- criticism 2: [[modernization]] literature neglects state's or society's external environment
				- neglects int'l political and economic factors
				- focuses internally/within particular states or societies
					- ignores state's/society's place in world-capitalist order
				- is there any society, even in [[Europe]]'s historical experience, that's immune to outside influences?
					- response by [[dependency]] theorists: highly unlikely
						- emphasizes [[Latin American]]'s colonial heritage and historical legacy of [[exploitation]] also experienced in [[Africa]]
- [[ECLA]] and [[UNCTAD]] economists's focus
	- initially narrow
		- examined unequal terms of trade between LDCs ([[Third World]] countries) that exported raw materials and [[First World]] countries that exported finished manufactured goods
		- questioned supposed benefits of [[free trade]]
	- [[ECLA]] once favored diversification of exports
		- advised LDCs should produce goods instead of import them
		- result: unsuccessful, increased influence of foreign MNCs brought in to facilitate domestic production
	- did all countries fail to experience economic growth?
		- no, some economies did grow
		- growth tended to occur in an LDC only if the [[First World]] countries needed a particular raw material/agricultural product
		- since many LDCs are dependent on only a few of these commodities for foreign exchange earnings –> drastic decline in demand for one commodity would have a calamitous impact on an LDC economy
			- e.g. can be caused by a global [[recession]] like in 2008
		- or, a bumper crop in many LDCs heavily dependent on one particular export (e.g. coffee, sugar) would cause prices to fall
	- volatility of prices for minerals and agricultural products and downward tendency of those prices
		- contrasts with more stable and gradually increasing prices for manufactured items produced by industrial countries
- terms of trade stacked against [[Third World]] countries that export farm products/natural resources
#### 7.3.2 Radical Critiques
- writers in [[ECLA]] and [[UNCTAD]]
	- Raúl [[Prebisch]] (1901 – 1986)
		- [[Argentina]]
	- critical of more conservative views of development
	- restricted their analyses to economic dimensions
	- cast their arguments in terms of [[nationalism]] and need for state-guided [[Capitalism]]
- other writers emphasize political and social factors within context of [[capitalist world-system]] that bind [[Latin American]] to [[North America]]
	- argument: development isn't autonomous
		- if development occurs, it's reflexive (subject to ups and downs of world's advanced economies/[[First World]] countries)
	- choices for [[Latin American]] countries are restricted as a result of [[Capitalism]] and supporting political, social, cultural relations
		- result: structure of domination
	- web of [[dependency]] reinforces unequal exchange between North and South
	- opportunities for LDCs are few
		- reason: LDCs are allocated a subordinate role in [[capitalist world-system]]
- [[one-way advantage]]
	- various states and societies produce those things of which they're relatively the most efficient producers or sell those items in which they have a comparative advantage
	- economic [[exploitation]] of LDCs by [[First World]] is an integral part of the [[capitalist world-system]]
		- required to keep it functioning
	- result: [[dependency]]
		- a "situation in which a certain number of countries have their economy conditioned by the development and expansion of another... placing the dependent countries in a backward position exploited by the dominant countries" #quote 
- the [[modernization]] experience of a particular society shouldn't be seen in isolation
	- "but as part of the development of an internationalist capitalist system ([[capitalist world-system]]), whose dynamic has a determining influence on the local processes" #quote 
	- result: underdevelopment isn't "a moment in evolution of a society which has been economically, politically, and cultural autonomous and isolated"
		- [[Latin American]] and [[Third World]] countries attempt to develop under historical conditions different from [[First World]] countries
- some [[Economic Structuralism]] scholars use [[Marxism]] terminology and [[Gramsci]]'s insights to explain [[dependency]]
	- [[Transnationalism]] class coalitions links elites in [[First World]] countries with their counterparts in the [[Third World]]
		- more important than relations between states
	- this version of class analysis emphasizes how [[Transnationalism]]'s ties within global [[Bourgeois]]/capitalist class work to the disadvantage of workers and peasants in the *periphery*
- MNCs and int'l banks are viewed in a different perspective than other [[IR]] images
	- [[Liberalism]] or [[The English School]]: MNCs and int'l banks are simply other/benign actors in world politics/global society
	- [[Realism]]: MNCs and int'l banks are secondary importance because of emphasis on state-as-actor
	- [[Economic Structuralism]]: MNCs and int'l banks are central players in establishing and maintaining [[dependency]] relations
	- [[Economic Structuralism]] of [[Marxism]]: MNCs and int'l banks are agents par excellence of the int'l [[bourgeoisie]]
		- MNCs and int'l banks represent two of the critical means by which the states in [[Global South]] are maintained in their subordinate position within the [[capitalist world-system]]
#### 7.3.3 Domestic Forces
- [[dependency]] theorists deal with external factors (foreign states, MNCs, int'l banks, multilateral lending institutions, foreign control of technology, int'l [[bourgeoisie]])
- also internal constraints on development (patterns of land tenure, social structures, class alliances, role of the state)
	- reinforces instruments of foreign domination
	- argument: inability to break out of [[dependency]] situation is strengthened by citizens of [[Latin American]] country who accrue selfish benefits at the expense of the country as a whole
		- *comprador* class or national [[bourgeoisie]] aids in [[exploitation]] of its society
			- allied with foreign capitalists
			- self-serving policies encourage expansion of social and economic inequality
				- takes form of an ever-widening rural-urban gap
					- limited development can occur in few urban centers
					- countryside stagnates though
						- viewed only as a provider of cheap labor and raw materials
			- these exploiters have more in common with elites of the [[First World]] countries than with their fellow citizens of the [[Third World]]
	- a sweeping argument
		- importance of internal dimensions varies depending on particular country under examination
			- e.g. class coalitions will differ and may relate to external actors in a variety of ways
		- "We conceive the relationship between external and internal forces as forming a complex whole whose structural links aren't based on mere external forms of [[exploitation]] and *coercion*, but are rooted in coincidence of interests between local dominant classes and international ones." #quote 
			- "coincidence of interests" might involve portions of the working class
- interplay of external and internal factors, nature of development or underdevelopment of a society will vary
	- changes in int'l economy affects LDCs in different ways
	- [[dependency]] theorists argue that development benefits some at the expense of others, increases social inequalities, leads to greater foreign control over [[Third World]] economies
		- doesn't claim that economic stagnation in LDCs is always and inevitably the norm
- 1990s: [[dependency]] literature as a concept virtually disappears
	- a function of empirical and theoretical criticism
	- many [[Latin American]] gov'ts were pursuing [[free-market]] economic policies with some degree of initial success
	- [[Fernando Henrique Cardoso]]
		- scholar of [[dependency]] theory
		- becomes president of [[Brazil]] for 8 years
		- sets aside sociological work on [[dependency]]
		- pursues conservative fiscal policies he saw central to country's economic development
	- [[dependency]] insights and arguments were subsumed under the broader concept of the [[capitalist world-system]] literature and even broader [[postcolonialism]] research programs
