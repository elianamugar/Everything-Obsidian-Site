#### 7.1.0 Major Actors and Assumptions
- [[Realism]]'s question: how can stability or order be maintained in an [[anarchy]] world?
- [[Liberalism]] and [[neoliberal institutionalism]]'s question: how can int'l collaboration and peaceful change be promoted in a world that's increasingly [[interdependence]] politically, militarily, socially, and economically?
- [[Economic Structuralism]]'s question: why have so many countries in [[Latin American]], [[Africa]], and [[Asia]] (the [[Global South]]) been unable to develop or have suffered from economic booms, busts, and financial instability?
	- part of a larger effort to develop a theory of *world-capitalist* development
	- [[Globalization]] isn't a new phenomenon, can be traced back several centuries
	- growing literature on [[postcolonialism]]
		- period since formal end of [[Colonialism]] [[Post-WWII]] AND the historical [[imperialism]] and [[Colonialism]] experiences that have so many implications for present day
	- some [[Economic structuralists]] avoid [[Marxism]] modes of analysis
- [[post-Cold War]]: fewer theoretical and empirical challenges against [[Economic Structuralism]] approaches to [[IR]]
	- ignored in mainstream literature
		- partially due to fall of [[USSR]] and other [[Lenin]]ist regimes in [[Eastern Europe]]
			- dampened scholarly interest in [[Economic Structuralism]], especially in [[America]]
				- ironic since the [[capitalist world-system]] perspective was persistent, anticipating continual unfolding of the logic of [[Capitalism]] and its spread worldwide
	- [[Economic Structuralism]] as an image of [[IR]] (especially [[capitalist world-system]] approach) remains highly relevant
	- during global economic hardship, there's a revival in interest in [[Economic Structuralism]]
- [[Economic Structuralism]] has always been independent of the rise and fall of regimes occurring in [[Capitalism]]'s mode of production
	- still-surviving [[Communism]] regimes ([[China]], [[North Korea]], [[Vietnam]], [[Cuba]]) are understood by their different adaptations to domination within a [[capitalist world-system]]
- recent literature and debate regarding economic, political, social crises in developing world cast into [[postcolonialism]] theorizing
	- accommodates [[Economic Structuralism]] approaches
	- had little impact on debates of mainstream [[IR]]
- [[Economic Structuralism]]'s 4 key assumptions:
	1. necessary to understand the global context within which states and other entities interact
		- to explain behavior at all levels of analysis (individual; group, *class*, bureaucratic, institutional units; state and society; between/among states or societies), we must understand the overall structure of the global system within which the behavior takes place
		- starting point of analysis should be int'l/[[capitalist world-system]]
			- behavior of individual actors is explained by a system that provides opportunities and constraints
		- difference between [[Economic Structuralism]] and [[structural realism]]
			- [[structural realism]]: focus on structure as the distribution of power among states
			- [[Economic Structuralism]]: focus on global economic structures
				- North vs. South
				- First vs. [[Third World]]s
				- core vs. *periphery*
				- capital-owning [[Bourgeois]] vs. [[proletariat]] (working/peasant classes)
	2. importance of historical analysis in comprehending the int'l system
		- historical focus –> [[postcolonialism]] studies
		- by tracing historical evolution of the system, it's possible to understand its current structure
		- key historical factor and defining characteristic of the system: [[Capitalism]]
			- benefits some, but at the expense of others
			- [[Socialism]] states must operate within a [[capitalist world-system]] that significantly constrains their options
			- East-West division during [[Cold War]] wasn't as important as the North-South divide and competition in advanced [[Capitalism]] states ([[Germany]], [[Japan]], [[America]], [[China]])
	3. particular mechanisms of domination exist that keep states comprising the [[Global South]] from developing, contributing to worldwide **uneven development**
		- requires examination of dependency relations between "northern" industrialized, capital-rich states and capital-poorer neighbors in southern hemisphere
			- capital-rich: [[Europe]], [[North America]], [[Japan]], [[Austria]], [[New Zealand]]
			- capital-poor: [[Africa]], [[Latin American]], [[Asia]], [[Pacific]] islands
				- "south" is more figurative than geographic
		- [[First World]]/northern capital-rich countries are referred to as the **core** of global [[Capitalism]]
		- [[Third World]]/southern capital-poor countries are referred to as the **periphery** of the [[capitalist world-system]]
			- "[[Third World]]" stems from 1960s [[France]] [[Socialism]] discourse to capture aspirations for a better life where most people life
				- traced back to term "[[Third Estate]]", referring to downtrodden classes of pre-[[revolution]] 18th c. [[France]]
				- other 2 estates were upper rungs of [[France]]'s society
					- nobility, aristocracy, clergy
			- calling poor countries Third World wasn't intended to insult, as if they were third rate
				- ultimately the people will triumph – the hopeful implication
		- **semiperiphery** is reserved for states between **core** and **periphery**
		- important to economic-structural analyses
			- capital position that describes the level of economic development/productive capacity a country has achieved regardless of its geographic location
		- changes in global economy [[post-Cold War]] had an impact on [[Economic Structuralism]] analyses
			- adaptation of concepts and terms used during [[Cold War]]
			- "[[Second World]]" term becomes obsolete as "East vs. West"
				- fall of [[USSR]] and [[Lenin]] regimes with command economies in [[Eastern Europe]]
				- embrace of [[Capitalism]] by [[China]] and [[Vietnam]]
				- in the past, referred to [[Socialism]] and [[Communism]] countries
					- separate from those linked within the dependency mechanism of [[Third World]] and [[First World]] countries
	4. economic factors are absolutely critical in explaining the evolution and functioning of the int'l/[[capitalist world-system]] and relegation of [[Global South]] states to a subordinate position
		- impact on political, social, cultural, ethnic, gender issues
			- included in [[postcolonialism]] literature
- [[Economic Structuralism]] approach shares commonalities with other images
	- [[structural realism]]/[[neorealism]]: greater emphasis on importance of systems level, or world as a whole, in affecting actors' behavior
		- difference: ontology as to how they characterize systems-level components
			- [[Economic Structuralism]]: focus on economic structure within [[Capitalism]] mode of production (classes/blocs, core/periphery, North/South)
			- [[neorealism]]: structure is found in distribution of aggregate power among states ([[unipolar]], [[bipolar distribution of power]], [[multipolar]])
		- difference: [[Economic Structuralism]] more likely to emphasize connection between int'l/[[capitalist world-system]] and domestic politics
			- state and society are never viewed as being encapsulated
			- e.g. class structure transcends boundaries of states and component societies
	- [[Liberalism]]: 3 commonalities, criticisms to [[Realism]]
		1. stress an approach to [[IR]] grounded in political economy
			- no distinction between *high* and *low politics*
				- relative importance of political-military vs. economic factors
			- [[Economic Structuralism]]: various manifestations of political and military power reflect the driving fore of underlying economic actors
				- politics depends on economics
				- politics isn't autonomous
		2. more attuned to events, processes, institutions, actors operating within AND between states
			- unitary, rational actor ([[Realism]]) is broken down into its parts
			- ranging in levels of analysis
			- focus on greater variety of actors
			- [[Economic Structuralism]]: emphasis on context within which actors operate (more than [[Liberalism]])
				- context: [[Capitalism]] nature of the int'l system
			- agency matters
				- more [[voluntarism]] in [[Liberalism]]
				- more [[determinism]] in [[Economic Structuralism]]
		3. those writing in [[Transnationalism]] tradition emphasize socioeconomic/welfare issues
			- [[Liberalism]]: normative commitment to peaceful change
				- [[IR]] doesn't have to be viewed as a [[zero-sum]] game with winners and losers
					- can be a [[positive-sum]] game in which the restructuring of interstate relations can be achieved through [[bargaining]] and compromise
						- allows all parties to gain
			- [[Economic Structuralism]]: not as optimistic about possibility of peaceful change
				- hierarchical and economic nature of world politics dictates it's unlikely that [[First World]] will make any meaningful concessions to [[Third World]] in [[capitalist world-system]]
				- change (peaceful or revolutionary) is problematic until [[capitalist world-system]] reaches a point of systemic crisis
	- [[The English School]]: little in common
		- [[The English School]]: more a middle path between [[Realism]] and [[Liberalism]] and [[Idealism]]
- [[Economic Structuralism]]'s difficult question: why can't the "developing world" develop?
	- how and why did [[Capitalism]] develop in [[Europe]]?
	- how did [[Capitalism]] expand outward to other countries?
	- as an int'l phenomenon, how has [[Capitalism]] changed over the centuries?
	- what are the specific mechanisms of dependency that allow for the maintenance of exploitative relations?
	- what are the relations between elites of [[First World]] countries and the elites of [[Third World]] countries?
	- is it possible for least developed countries (LDCs) to break out of a dependent situation?
