#### 13.2.1 The Levels of Analysis
- *levels of analysis*
	- individual, community, state and society, regions/cultural areas, world as a whole
	- do we have duties beyond borders?
	- in an era of [[Globalization]], how might we live in a world threatened by weapons of mass destruction, water and food shortages, pandemics, global climate change, and other challenges within/outside borders of states?
- national/community position on norms
	- obligations to fellow citizens take priority over strangers living in other nations, states, cultures
		- developing and justifying normative universal criteria is questionable from this POV
			- int'l law provides bases for states to respect one another's autonomy
			- [[pluralism]] of values among societies/groups
- a third perspective: universal/cosmopolitan normative theorizing
	- dates back to [[Stoicism]] and [[jus naturale]] thinking, [[Kant]]ian moral imperatives, [[Utilitarianism]] principles, [[social contract theory]] bases for moral choice
#### 13.2.2 Moral Relativism
- [[universalism]]/cosmopolitan perspectives are in direct conflict with [[moral relativism]]
	- moral relativism: no universal standard exists by which to assess an ethical proposition's truth
		- if we believe in a strict moral relativism (values and rights can have no independent standing of their own), then there's no such thing as morality or [[Ethics]]
- significant problem with moral relativism
	- gives no universal basis for condemning atrocities and human tragedies (e.g. [[Holocaust]], other acts of [[genocide]])
	- any rational human being, regardless of cultural origin, should understand the immorality of such atrocities
- religious basis for universal human rights
	- [[Religion]] typically doesn't limit themselves to their followers, but also make universally applicable moral claims
	- practicality of religion
		- rejection of religion by some and absence of theological consensus among followers of various religious groups prevents the use of particular religions as the solitary bases for common worldwide acceptance of human rights and other moral claims
	- many writers try to identify secular/non-religious bases for universalist positions
#### 13.2.3 Secular Bases for Moral or Ethical Choice
##### 13.2.3.1 Stoics
- [[Stoicism]] argues that we are all part of a larger community of humankind, regardless of different political communities and cultures
- all humans are able to reason
	- possible to determine the laws of nature that apply to all, regardless of the community in which one lives
- influential in [[Roman Republic]] and imperial [[Rome]]
	- [[Cicero]]: "true law is right reason in agreement with nature; it is of universal application, unchanging and everlasting" #quote 
		- "Justice is one; it binds all society, and is based on one law" #quote 
		- "Justice does not exist at all if it does not exist in Nature" #quote 
- this [[universalism]] was the basis for the idea of law common to the nations of the [[Roman Empire]], [[jus gentium]] (law of the peoples)
	- [[Augustine]], [[Thomas Aquinas]], religious writers of the [[Middle Ages]]
		- values transcend a single community/state
	- [[Grotius]], etc.
		- secular basis for int'l law
			- general principles consistent with reasoned understanding of [[jus naturale]]
			- customary int'l practice
			- writings of jurists (decisions rendered by judges/justices)
			- commitments in treaties/conventions voluntarily undertaken by states
- [[jus naturale]] thinking played an important role in [[Hobbes]] and [[Locke]]'s [[social contract theory]]
##### 13.2.3.2 Kant
- [[Kant]] (1724 – 1804)
	- use our rational faculty to look inward
- the moral element
	- vision of the universal transcends space and time and can be discovered within oneself
	- inherent sense of right and wrong stems from one's [[Ontology]]
		- worldview a person has/how one internally sees or understands the essence of things
- Kantian approach to ethics
	- *deontological*
		- *deont*, Greek for *being necessary*
		- one can know through reason what's right and one is duty bound to follow that imperative
- writings on reason with direct application to discovering universal maxims/ethical imperatives that we're duty bound to follow in private and public
	- fundamental principle: to act always so that you respect every human as a rational, thinking agent capable of choice
	- individual has free will to choose the correct moral course ([[voluntarism]])
	- individual behavior isn't predetermined
	- individual is obligated to follow the moral law that's discoverable through proper exercise of reason
- Kant on free will
	- one should exercise free will and act according to [[categorical imperative]] (independent of contingencies)
		- one acts "according to the maxim which you can at the same time will to be a universal law"
		- one should "treat humanity, in your own person, and in the person of everyone else as an end as well as a means, never merely as a means" #quote 
			- we are to treat other humans as ends worth in themselves, not just as means
	- the correct prescription for a moral conduct is knowable by the individual and amounts to those precepts that have universally binding character
- [[universalism]] in Kantian ethics
	- basis for his thinking on IR
	- addresses [[War]] and peace in [[international society]]
		- looks toward an improvement in state behaviors among liberal [[Republic]]s that would make them less prone to use force against other states
			- [[democratic peace theory]]
	- Kantian ideal is a future cosmopolitan int'l society of individuals, states, other actors following ethical principles and aiming toward perfection
		- right reason is to be used to discern obligations stemming from universal law that transcends the laws made by individual states
		- path towards "perpetual peace", world free of war
		- federation of peaceful states could (but not necessarily) become established as a response to the [[security]] needs of states
##### 13.2.3.3 Utilitarians
- Jeremy [[Bentham]] (1748 – 1832), [[John Stuart Mill]] (1806 – 1873)
	- focus on attaining the greatest good for the greatest number ([[Utilitarianism]])
	- we should be concerned with assessing outcomes/consequences of our actions
	- a society is rightly ordered if "its major institutions are arranged so as to achieve the greatest net balance of satisfaction" #quote 
		- abstract principle applied to a wide range of human circumstances
			- defense of liberty, other human rights as representing the greatest good for the greatest number
- main application of utilitarian principles
	- within domestic societies
- utilitarian and Kantian criteria provide philosophical basis for int'l law
	- application of these criteria transcends the boundaries of any given states or society
- practicality
	- obstacles trying to apply Kantian or utilitarian ethics as basis for constructing some radically new and just world order
		- present division of the world into separate, sovereign states with different perspectives on global issues
##### 13.2.3.4 Social-Contract Theorists
- question of the scope of justice
	- an issue dating back to beginning of [[Philosophy]]
	- should conceptions of justice have boundaries, limiting it to particular societies/cultures?
	- is justice by nature universal and cosmopolitan?
- normative IR theory interested in social contract theorists
	- begins with domestic question of justice
	- provided insight into expansion of such normative concerns to int'l politics
- social contract approach as a guide to right behavior
	- assumption: individuals may voluntarily agree to bind themselves to some set of principles
	- challenge: explaining how this might come about
	- [[Rousseau]]'s stag hunt analogy
		- hunters in a state of nature seen as maximizing individual, short-term self-interest by going for the hare
			- with no sovereign authority to compel collaboration or force the honoring of contracts, no other outcome can be expected
	- [[Hobbes]]
		- escape state of nature can only be achieved by people elevating a sovereign (monarch or legislature) to provide the order and security that comes from governance
		- finds a way out of figurative state of nature for domestic politics
		- no such social contract remedy to resolve problems of cooperation among states existing in anarchic int'l system
		- [[The English School]] and [[constructivism]]
			- different assumptions about state of nature result in different prospects for int'l collaboration
	- [[Locke]]
		- humans have certain [[jus naturale]] to life, liberty, and property
			- surrendered only as part of a social contract
		- apart from cultural context, humans have rights as part of their nature
			- secular ground for making universalistic moral claims
		- human rights are part of human nature
		- citizenry/people who empower gov'ts must strictly limit the authority of gov'ts to abridge them
		- gov'ts are created to guarantee certain civil rights
			- rights that individuals have as members of the societies to which they belong
				- applicable to the domestic and int'l levels of analysis
	- John [[Rawls]] (1921 – 2002)
		- what would be considered fair if individuals were in a state of nature and none knew in advance what one's place in society, class position, wealth, or social status would be?
			- common "veil of ignorance" about outcomes
			- what principles of distributive justice would these hypothetically free agents choose?
		- "all social values – liberty and opportunity, income and wealth, and the bases of self-respect – are to be distributed equally unless an unequal distribution of any, or all, of these values is to everyone's advantage" #quote 
			- socioeconomic inequality is admissible only if it benefits everyone in society *and* if there's an equal opportunity for everyone to acquire those positions associated with unequal rewards
		- [[A Theory of Justice by John Rawls]]
			- whether one extends such propositions as the basis for global justice for individuals or states isn't clear
			- formulates a "conception of justice for the basic structure of society"
			- "the conditions for the law of nations may require different principles arrived at in a somewhat different way"
		- explores ways and means of extending [[jus gentium]] (law of peoples) that transcends borders of states and their respective societies
			- constructing an int'l society calls for application of universal norms
			- behind a veil of ignorance "a well ordered non-liberal society"/[[authoritarian]] regime in our times "will accept the same law of peoples that well ordered liberal societies accept"
				- similar to intended universal applicability of Roman [[jus gentium]]
				- finds the fabric of this law in [[Liberalism]] understandings of human rights, freedom, independence, non-intervention
- social contract theory applied to states as if they were individual persons
	- basis for a [[Positivism]] (legal) interpretation of int'l law
		- int'l law and obligation to follow other int'l rules/norms stem from affirmative actions taken by states
		- voluntary contract made by states in form of treaty, convention, customary practice that creates the obligation
- norms, morality, ethics not new to study of IR
	- addressed in terms of "right reason" to discern obligations stemming from some aspect of natural law (jus naturale) or provide greatest good for the greatest number (utilitarianism)
	- may conform more closely to positivist approach to constructing int'l law
		- e.g. treaties are binding and such obligations ought to be kept
	- Kantian perspective
		- contrasts with views of [[Machiavelli]] and [[Hobbes]]
	- concerns for moral choices present in [[classical realism]]
		- [[power]] and [[balance of power]] are more important considerations in this tradition
		- incorrect to view power and values as if they were mutually exclusive approaches to int'l politics
		- [[E. H. Carr]] ([[Realism]], [[The English School]]): "The utopian who dreams that it is possible to eliminate self-assertion from politics and to base a political system on morality alone is just as wide of the mark as the realist who believes that altruism is an illusion and that all political action is self-seeking." #quote 
			- int'l politics involves a blend of values and power, [[utopian]]ism and [[Realism]]
				- found in virtually all intellectual precursors discussed
				- disagreement on the relative importance of values and power, and which values should be pursued
