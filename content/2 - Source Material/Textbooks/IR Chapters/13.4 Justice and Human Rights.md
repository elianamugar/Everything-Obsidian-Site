#### 13.4.0 Justice and Human Rights
- the quest for universal understanding of socioeconomic, political, legal rights belonging to individuals, groups, classes, societies, humanity
	- evolutionary and still-ongoing process
	- challenge: unequal, adverse treatment humans suffer based on race/ethnicity, national origin, social/economic class, age, gender, sexual identity/orientation
- violations of labor, safety, health standards, illegal trafficking of persons for prostitution/slave labor, torture, genocide
	- human [[exploitation]]
#### 13.4.1 The Enlightenment
- concept of rights in Western thought rooted in ancient Greek, Roman, and religious writings
- stronger philosophical ground for specifying human aspirations for liberty, equality, communitarian concerns, and human obligations in society found in the [[Enlightenment]] and [[social contract theory]]
	- [[Liberalism]] spirit of the Enlightenment developed further in work by [[Kant]] and [[Utilitarianism]]
- [[social contract theory]] writers
	- [[Hobbes]], [[Locke]], [[Rousseau]]
	- differ on relative importance each lace on liberty, equality, community, and order
	- common theme: humans are the ultimate source of legitimate political authority in society
		- democratic understanding explicit in [[Locke]] and [[Rousseau]]
		- [[Hobbes]]: [[monarchy]] rested legitimately not on [[Divine Right of Kings]], but on the people who vested the sovereign with authority and power to maintain societal order in interest of their own [[security]]
- documentary statements of aspirations for rights taking political/legal form
	- 13th c. [[UK]] [[Magna Carta]]
	- late-18th c. [[America]] [[Bill of Rights]]
	- late-18th c. [[France]]'s [[Declaration of the Rights of Man]]
	- content analysis of these documents
		- greater focus on individual political and legal rights in the [[UK]] and [[America]] documents
		- [[France]] declaration contrasts, extending itself to socioeconomic realm
			- applications to individuals and larger aggregations of communal and societal levels
	- people may have rights as a class, group, or society as a whole
- the present-day global society
	- differences in understandings across societies and cultures remain
	- culture matters in how we interpret/understand rights in different social contexts
		- e.g. differences in relative importance of liberty, equality, and order
		- e.g. whether rights/obligations are applied at the individual level of analysis or larger human aggregations
	- [[America]] tends to focus on human rights as individual political and legal rights and liberties
	- other states and societies accept these civil rights and liberties as part of a larger package
		- puts a higher premium on socioeconomic rights and communitarian understandings
- advocacy of human rights
	- grounded predominantly in Western political thought
	- philosophical bases for human rights in non-Western traditions
		- William Theodor [[de Bary]] (1919 – 2017)
			- translated and interpreted classic works from [[China]] and [[Japan]]
			- [[Confucian]]'s communitarian understandings relevant to human rights
		- [[Islam]] writings also have philosophical and legal bases for human rights
			- [[Qur'an]] and [[Sunnah]], sayings and actions of the [[Prophet Muhammed]]
#### 13.4.2 Current Application
- human rights advocates adopt a [[universalism]] rather than [[relativism]] view
	- searching for universal, underlying values that inform our understandings of human rights in practice
	- respect for life, human dignity, justice/fairness broadly are understood and accepted
		- disagreement on how these values are to be applied in particular human rights contexts
		- basis for discourse aimed in the interest of justice and fairness at resolving differences in how these values are applied across societies
- continuing discourse that develops consensus across cultures and societies on the realization of these values in common practice
	- 1948: [[Universal Declaration of Human Rights]] adopted by [[United Nations]] Gen. Assembly
		- emphasizes political and legal, individual preferences of its sponsors
		- 6 of 30 articles (20%) addresses socioeconomic and cultural rights in individual, rather than collective terms
			- foundation for later expansion in UN treaties/agreements on civil and political rights and economic, social, and cultural rights, elimination of discrimination based on race and against women, and creation of children's bill of rights
#### 13.4.3 Humanitarian Treatment and the Sovereign State
- claims for human rights and demands for humanitarian treatment grounded in treaty commitments
	- collides with prerogatives of a sovereign state
	- states claim a right under int'l law to exclusive jurisdiction over all persons within their territory
	- human rights advocates see moral and legal bases for action when decisions/policies of gov'ts violate human rights
		- includes armed intervention e.g. [[genocide]]
		- when states have legally bounded themselves in treaties specifying commitment to these same rights
	- when nat'l [[security]] considerations conflict with these obligations, compliance by states can't be taken for granted
- difficulties beyond when armed intervention in domestic affairs of a sovereign state legally can be legitimate
	- e.g. jurisdiction of the [[ICJ]] (International Court of Justice)
		- legal remedy/alternative to using force is limited to ruling on disputes/cases states voluntarily bring to court for judicial decision
		- even when the court has rendered a decision, compliance still depends on the will of states to carry out their obligations specified in these rulings
	- existence of [[ICC]] (International Criminal Court)
		- established to hold individuals accountable for genocide, crimes against humanity, [[War]] crimes, crime of aggression
		- important cases, but remains limited
		- its jurisdiction severely constrained by the fact that not all states have yet authorized this tribunal
		- [[America]] as most prominent non-subscriber, part of its drafting, to the Rome Statute (agreed in 1998, entering into force in 2002)
	- e.g. the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide
		- 1948, entering into force in 1951
		- [[genocide]]: "any of the following acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical \[*sic*], racial or religious group": "(a) killing members of the group; (b) causing serious bodily or mental harm to members of the group; (c) deliberately inflicting on the group conditions of life calculated to bring about its physical destruction in whole or in part; (d) imposing measures intended to prevent births within the group; \[and] (e) forcibly transferring children of the group to another group."
		- doesn't provide armed intervention as an explicit remedy to stop genocide
			- when such severe human rights violations threaten int'l peace and security, there's legal basis for humanitarian intervention under UN auspices
				- Articles 34, 41, 42
		- some see emerging in customary int'l law the occurrence of genocide as a legitimate basis in itself for armed intervention
			- contrast (gov'ts explicit in genocidal actions): politically oppose what they see as an expansion of the UN agenda
				- claim: such efforts are meddling in internal affairs of sovereign states, which amounts to a violation of int'l law
- efforts to spare non-combatants and other defenseless persons
	- civilian populations are not the proper object of warfare
	- captured enemy soldiers become defenseless persons who may be taken prisoner, but not executed because they're prisoners
		- POWs have rights under [[Geneva Conventions]]
			- guarantees of humanitarian treatment have been made part of int'l law
		- controversy of [[America]]'s [[Guantanamo Bay]], [[Cuba]]
			- established following the overthrow of the [[Taliban]] regime in [[Afghanistan]], Fall 2001
				- holding suspected terrorists
			- US gov't relied on laws dating back to [[American Civil War]] and [[WWII]]
				- declared these individuals weren't POWs, but rather "enemy combatants" held as "detainees"
				- allegedly, they couldn't invoke the int'l legal rights associated with POWs
		- [[Hamdan v. Rumsfeld (2006)]]
			- ruled that Common Article 3 of the [[Geneva Conventions]] provides safeguards to those held in prisons does apply
				- they are to be treated "humanely" with the following prohibitions
					- "(a) violence to life and person, in particular murder of all kinds, mutilation, cruel treatment and torture;"
					- "(b) taking of hostages;"
					- "(c) outrages upon personal dignity, in particular, humiliating and degrading treatment; and"
					- "(d) the passing of sentences and the carrying out of executions without previous judgment pronounced by regularly constituted court affording all the judicial guarantees which are recognized as indispensable by civilized peoples"
					- those who are "wounded and sick shall be collected and cared for"
		- practical concern for finding information deemed vital to nat'l security
			- frequently conflicts with moral and legal obligations to afford humane treatment in the interrogations of those taken captive
			- Convention against Torture and Other Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or Punishment
				- 1984, entering into force in 1987
				- torture: "any act by which severe pain or suffering, whether physical or mental, is intentionally inflicted on a person for such purposes as obtaining from him or a third person information or a confession, punishing him for an act he or a third person has committed or is suspected of having committed, or intimidating or coercing him or a third person, or for any reason based on discrimination of any kind, when such pain or suffering is inflicted by or at the instigation of or with the consent or acquiescence of a public official or other person acting in an official capacity. It does not include pain or suffering arising only from, inherent in or incidental to lawful sanctions."
			- those who argue torture's legitimacy or stretch the operational definitions of what constitutes torture say the *end* (nat'l security) *justifies the means*
			- by no means is the claim that moral purposes justify immoral means a legitimate basis for actions taken
