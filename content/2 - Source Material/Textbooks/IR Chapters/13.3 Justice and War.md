#### 13.3.0 Justice and War
- morality of war itself ([[jus ad bellum]])
- ethical/moral constraints within any given war ([[jus in bello]])
- basis of justice and war in Western thought, stemming from ancient Greeks
	- [[Augustine]] drew from [[Cicero]], who drew from [[Plato]]
		- contrary to absolute pacifism of early [[Christianity]]
		- addresses war as something that was to be avoided but was sometimes necessary
	- [[just war theory]] grows with [[Thomas Aquinas]], [[Francisco Suarez]], [[Francisco de Vitoria]], etc. in [[medieval]] period
- developing just war theory through exercise of right reason and right conduct ([[jus ad bellum]])
	- philosophical assumptions underlying normative theory on armed conflict
	- preoccupation with national [[security]] issues
		- contemporary thinking on just war within [[Realism]] ([[classical realism]]), [[The English School]] ([[Grotius]] understandings)
			- not all realists accept [[Machiavelli]]'s characterization of war as something useful for acquiring or maintaining rule
				- if postponed, might work only to the advantage of the enemy
			- highlighted by [[Carl von Clausewitz]], war is decidedly not a legitimate end in itself, but a means used to achieve political purposes
			- war shouldn't be waged without legitimate purpose
				- should be subordinate to the political objective or serve some national interest
				- a limited but moral statement
	- defense against provoked aggression is generally a legitimate political objective justifying war
	- war is a last resort to be undertaken only if there's some chance of success
		- death and destruction by war are to be minimized, consistent with achieving legitimate military purposes
			- no positive moral content in war unless legitimate political and military purposes are served
		- [[Carl von Clausewitz]]'s conventional military logic
			- the purpose of any war is to destroy/substantially weaken an enemy's war-making capability
			- military necessity doesn't justify the use of means disproportionate to the ends sought or the use of weapons that are indiscriminate or that cause needless human suffering
			- lives of noncombatants are to be spared to the maximum extent possible
- just war theory raises questions of right conduct in war once armed conflict breaks out ([[jus in bello]])
	- real limits set to confine the death and destruction of warfare to what's militarily necessary
		- reducing war's barbarity
	- principles that specify the bases for moral legitimacy to going to war and conduct of force in war
		- [[13.3 Justice and War#13.3.0.0 Just War Principles]]
		- their application depends on right intention
			- can't always be assumed when it comes to conduct of states still sovereign in an [[anarchy]] world lacking in viable enforcement authority
- Grotian schools of thought ([[The English School]], [[Liberalism]], [[classical realism]])
	- identify rules/laws that constrain states, statesmen, soldiers in exercise of their war powers
	- treaties/conventions based on earlier just war thinking come into force
		- beginning with [[Hague Conventions]] in late 19th – early 20th c.
		- 20th c. [[Geneva Conventions]] and other agreements provide basis for contemporary law of war
	- defining aggression and dealing with insurgencies in which the legitimacy of the parties in question remains on the agenda of moral and legal challenges
- just war thinking rooted in pre-Christian, non-Judaic Greco-Roman world of [[Cicero]]
	- writing in [[Plato]]nic tradition
	- a secular thesis that didn't rely on religious premises
	- Christian writers ([[Augustine]] and [[Thomas Aquinas]]) took up and developed the argument further, becoming part of church doctrine
	- these ideas made their way into [[Islam]]'s thought, dealing with practical challenges related to use of force
		- e.g. [[jihad]] has been misinterpreted and misused by some to justify mass murder perpetrated upon the innocent
			- same way the just war doctrine has been manipulated over the centuries by those who seek to legitimate untoward decisions and actions taken by those in authority
				- the fault is not with the doctrines that seek to constrain the use of force, but rather the perpetrators who intentional misconstrue the meanings of moral criteria to justify their conduct
			- jihad is an acknowledgment of a moral struggle that may call for a use of force 
				- "a Muslim's striving to fulfill his Islamic responsibility, both in outward actions and in inward correction of his own mistakes"
				- "working or fighting in the cause of Allah"
				- open to multiple interpretations
					- "Jihad does not necessarily involve waging war (offensive or defensive)"
	- Islamic understandings vary from country/region in relation to culture and historical experience
		- benign Arabic translation of *jihad* – "to strive for some objective"
		- "combative jihad" at the center of controversy: the declaration of war against belligerent and aggressive non-Muslim powers/against fellow Muslim transgressors
	- conditions justifying combative jihad
		- "aggressive designs against Islam;... concerted efforts to eject Muslims from their legally acquired property; and, that military campaigns are being launched to eradicate them."
	- the Imam/religious leader has authority to declare combative jihad that the ruler carries out
		- with the just war doctrine, combative jihad requires just cause and legitimate authority
		- peaceful means ought to be employed, if possible, and the aim remains to reestablish peace

| ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1. Just cause                                                    | 1. Military necessity                                                         |
| 2. Legitimate authority                                          | 2. Spare noncombatants and other defenseless persons                          |

#### 13.3.1 Applying Just War Theory in the 21st Century
- can just war theory be used to legitimate [[deterrence theory]] doctrines arguing that to maintain peace one must threaten devastation even on a global scale?
- focus on limits in just war theory and int'l law can be impractical in age dominated by weapons of mass destruction (WMDs)
- effort in just war theory to put practical limits on use of force and reduce/eliminate the barbarity of warfare
- just war theory's failures
	- did not prevent the obliteration bombing of cities/other population centers in [[WWII]]
	- raids seen as undermining societal morale in enemy countries, weakening enemy's will to resist
		- postwar evaluation of strategic bombing and air power challenged this rationale
		- survivors's rage increased will to resist rather than submit
			- obliteration of bombing proved to be counterproductive/dysfunctional (also militarily)
		- in hindsight, obliteration bombing of population centers has been discredited militarily and morally [[Post-WWII]]
			- no moral justification under just war doctrine for mass death and destruction
				- these military actions didn't serve legitimate military purposes
				- additional conditions need to be met to satisfy [[jus in bello]] obligations
- principle of military necessity
	- can be construed broadly in the interest of national [[security]] to allow almost any conduct in war
	- expectation for political authorities/military commanders to approach use of force with consistency to human cost-reduction purpose of just war theory
		- narrow construction of military necessity prescribed by just war theory
		- destroying enemy's war-making capability focuses on destructive efforts on an adversary's armed forces and *only* those parts of the society's infrastructure that directly contributes to its war-making effort
			- not a call to destroy an entire society, its population, or anything else of material/cultural value
	- principle of military necessity needs to be narrowly interpreted to limit destructiveness of war to what's absolutely necessary for military purposes
- distinction between counter-force and counter-value targets
	- counter-force targets: military HQs, troop/tank formations, combat aircraft, ships, maintenance facilities, other military installations
		- destruction of military installations that would directly weaken enemy's war-making capabilities
	- counter-value targets: factories, rail junctions, airports, power plants in or near cities that contribute to an enemy's war-making capability/overall war effort
		- bombing counter-value targets usually produces more civilian, non-combatant casualties than counter-force targeting
- the means used to accomplish military purposes need to be proportional to the goal
	- compatible with [[jus in bello]]
- how to reduce unnecessary death/other damage
	- invoking dual-/double-effect principle
		- deals with moral problem of killing non-combatants and producing collateral damage in warfare
		- any action may have two or more effects/consequences
			- if the intent is to destroy a legitimate target that contributes to an enemy's war-making capability/overall war effort
				- then every reasonable effort must be made to avoid unnecessary casualties/other destruction
			- "good effect": destroying legitimate military target
			- "bad effect": unintended human and material consequences
	- following double-effect logic and proportionality assumption
		- proportionality assumption: the target is worth destroying in light of its military value when weighed against the expected consequences
		- just war theory *seems* to support the idea that killing non-combatants/destroying civilian property may be morally justifiable when both effects occur simultaneously/the good effect precedes the bad
			- e.g. targeting an arms factory at night when most workers were expected to be at home
				- it's accepted that a few workers may still be killed when the factory is destroyed – the misfortune of war
				- bad things happen in war, which is why just war theory puts emphasis on avoiding war in the first place
	- if warriors intend the bad effect or if it precedes the good, such conduct doesn't satisfy the double-effect principle and is understood to be morally wrong
		- e.g. bombing workers at their homes next to the arms factory (bad effect) will likely reduce/eliminate the production capacity of the factory (good effect, militarily)
			- problem: good effect depends upon achieving the bad effect first
			- just war theorists argue that good ends cannot justify evil means
				- the ends don't justify the means, no matter how good one's objectives/purposes are
			- it would be morally wrong to bomb the village
			- if factory production must be halted, the factory itself should be targeted preferably at a time when as many workers can be spared
#### 13.3.2 Morality and Weaponry
- any weapon can be used immorally, but some cannot be used morally even if one intended to do so
	- immoral weapons
		- those that are indiscriminate/cause needless suffering
			- e.g. rifle is not immoral itself
				- when used properly, it can be used with discrimination, sparing non-combatants
				- if used improperly to murder non-combatants, it's the action and not the immoral weapon
			- e.g. conventional bombs delivered accurately by planes/missiles
				- can be used morally or immorally depending on target selected and how it's to be destroyed
				- more accurate, the better is true militarily and morally
				- the destruction of a legitimate military target is more likely
				- collateral/unnecessary death and destruction, if not eliminated, can be minimized if accurate weapons employed
	- inaccurate weapons
		- includes chemically/biological agents (gas, germ warfare)
		- eliminates the distinction between combatant and non-combatant
		- not useful militarily
			- winds disperse chemical agents indiscriminately
			- diseases can be spread to both sides of battlefield and across societies
		- immoral and illegal
			- treaties prohibit use of chemical and biological weapons
		- int'l consensus that led to these chemical and biological conventions rests on this moral argument
			- weapons are indiscriminate and fail another moral test: causes needless suffering
			- rifle bullets/anti-personnel weapons designed to prolong/increase agony also fail the moral test
			- killing in war is supposed to be as humane as possible
			- most categories of weapons that are intended to enhance rather than reduce human suffering are also illegal
		- nuclear weapons
			- e.g. [[America]] bombing of [[Japan]]'s city Hiroshima and Nagasaki
				- justified at the time on [[Utilitarianism]] grounds that the bombings would shorten the war and reduce casualties on both sides
				- loss of life at Hiroshima and Nagasaki precluded greater loss of life from Allied invasion of Japanese home islands
				- some questioned morality of bombing even for this purpose
					- if bombs were to be used at all, they should've been directed toward strictly military targets, not population centers interspersed with military targets
				- decision makers responded that Japanese leaders should take the blame since they decided to locate military-related plants where they did
			- nuclear weapons yield heat, blast, and radiation effects that can't be used with discrimination
				- these weapons fail on grounds of human suffering
				- some argue that lower-yield, tactical nuclear weapons can be used *with* discrimination and not cause unnecessary suffering
					- critics are skeptical of this claim
					- counter: using any nuclear weapons at all "opens Pandora's box", legitimating this category of weaponry and increasing the likelihood that even larger nuclear weapons will be employed by one or another of the parties
			- have not yet been declared illegal, however ill-advised or immoral their use might be
