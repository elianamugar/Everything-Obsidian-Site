#### 6.7.0 International Regimes
- [[complex interdependence]] didn't displace *state* as principal focus of study
	- [[interdependence]] involves reciprocal effects among countries/other actors in different countries
	- states can use their power resources to manipulate interdependent relations
	- states would experience difficulties in linking issues together
- int'l orgs
	- institutions composed of state members
	- actors that can set agendas, encourage coalition formation, act as arenas for political actions (even by small, relatively weaker states)
	- nongov'tal orgs capable of establishing own ties with these intergovernmental orgs
	- viewed from *dependent variable* (decisions/actions to be explained) –> int'l orgs + their agents (leaders and staffs) viewed as *independent variables*
		- substantial influence on states
- 1980s: [[Liberalism]] focuses on [[liberal institutionalism]]
	- research program focuses on role of *international regimes*
		- rules agreed to by states concerning their conduct in specific issue areas
		- associated w/int'l and nongov'tal orgs linked to these regimes
- regime literature
	- focus on ways and means of constructing/maintaining/managing interdependent relations found in multilateral, institutionalized arrangements
	- term *regime* borrowed from domestic politics
		- refers typically to existing gov'tal order ([[democratic]], [[authoritarian]], etc.)
	- *regime* in int'l context
		- absence of superordinate central authority
		- rules voluntarily established by states to provide some degree of order in [[IR]]
	- strong [[Grotius]] strain in [[Liberalism]]
		- managing [[interdependence]] through construction of regimes
	- int'l regimes aren't the same as int'l orgs
		- regimes don't require a mailing address or possess the capacity to act
		- in [[IR]], regimes are analytical constructs defined by observers
		- action remains with states, int'l, nongov'tal orgs
	- an org associated with a particular regime may concern itself with other regimes covering diverse issue areas in global context
		- [[United Nations]]'s "system" of int'l orgs
		- e.g. International Telecommunications Union that regulates global distribution of frequencies
	- concerned with:
		- how and why are int'l regimes formed?
		- what accounts for rule-based cooperation?
		- how do regimes affect state behavior and collective outcomes in particular issue areas?
		- how and why do regimes evolve or dissolve?
- [[power-based realism]]
	- theories emphasize role of [[anarchy]] and impact of relative distribution of capabilities
	- [[regime theory (realism)]] 
		- [[hegemonic stability]] ([[5.7 Realists and International Cooperation]])
			- best-known realist regime theory
			- regimes are established and maintained when a state holds majority of power resources
				- e.g. [[America]] [[Post-WWII]]
			- once this [[hegemonic power]] declines and power is spread more equally among states, if regimes don't adapt themselves to changed circumstances, they can be expected to decline
		- Stephen [[Krasner]]
			- regimes help states avoid uncoordinated action
			- regimes can be a source of power for weaker states
- [[knowledge-based cognitive regime theory]]
	- critical of [[Realism]] ([[hegemonic stability]]) and [[neoliberal institutionalism]]
	- cognition theorists (some [[constructivism]])
		- state interests are not given, but rather created
		- examines role of normative and causative beliefs of decision makers in explaining preferences and interest formation
		- focus less on overt behavior and more on intersubjective understandings
			- learning matters when a change in beliefs/understandings influence subsequent behavior
		- attempt to demonstrate states can redefine their interests without any shift in overall systemic distribution of power and use regimes and institutions to "lock in" to their advantage the learning that happened
- [[epistemic communities]]
	- how knowledge might come to be shared by decision makers
	- influence of these transnational communities
		- networks of professionals with recognized expertise and competence in a particular domain and an authoritative claim to policy-relevant knowledge within that domain
	- can influence creation and maintenance of int'l regimes
	- [[Peter Haas]], Emanuel [[Adler]], others pathbreaking work on [[epistemic communities]]
		- substantial impact on [[Liberalism]] understandings of how ideas are carried by transnational groups of specialists impact policy processes
		- composed of politically connected elites
			- found earlier in Ernst [[Haas]]'s work on [[integration]]
			- global impact on how policies are made in diverse institutional contexts
	- [[Peter Haas]] observes "members of [[epistemic communities]] not only hold in common a set of principled and causal beliefs, but also have shared notions of validity and a shared policy enterprise"
		- specialists bring socially constructed interpretations of facts/knowledge and causality to questions at issue
	- studying roles played and influence on policy by [[epistemic communities]]
- int'l regimes embedded in broader normative structures of [[international civil society]]
	- states aren't free to ignore institutional commitments without paying a price
	- focus on self-interest alone won't explain regime maintenance
	- regimes have more than regulative function that requires states to behave in accordance with certain norms and rules
	- regimes create a common social world that interprets the meaning of int'l behavior
	- regimes have ***constitutive*** dimension
		- they're socially constructed
- focus on social construction of world politics and identities
	- actors in int'l politics make decisions based on what the world appears to be and how they conceive their roles in it
		- derived from systemic, intersubjective shared understandings and expectations
	- regimes
		- rule-governed cooperation can, over time, lead actors to change their beliefs about who they are and how they relate to the rest of the world
	- cooperative and collaborative behaviors can become habit
