#### 5.2.0 Intellectual Precursors and Influences
- root of current realist thought
	- culmination of thinking about IR over the millennia
	- identify more notable intellectual precursors who had a significant impact on writings of contemporary realists
		- more greater details in [[International Relations Theory (7th ed.) – Kauppi, Viotti#Part 1 - The Intellectual Roots of International Relations Theory]] in intellectual roots of IR theory
	- discusses several 20th–21st c. writers in [[Realism]] and how realism informed their approach to theory building
- [[Thucydides]] (472 – 400 BCE)
	- first writer in [[Realism]] tradition, founding father of [[IR]]
	- his [[History of the Peloponnesian War by Thucydides]] chronicles 21/28 years of war between [[Athens]] and [[Sparta]] in 5th c. BCE
		- beyond simply chronicling events, he aimed to discuss the nature of [[War]] and why it continually recurs
		- past was the guide for the future
		- less interested in immediate causes of [[Peloponnesian War]], more focused on underlying forces at work
		- study of the struggle for military and political power
- [[Machiavelli]] (1469 – 1527)
	- draws from study of [[Ancient World]] ([[Roman]]) writings
	- 15th – 16th c. Italy divided into city-states was similar to Hellenic world of [[Thucydides]]
	- worked a civil servant and diplomat to Republic of Florence 
		- 1512: Republic of Florence falls
	- tortured by [[Medici]] family (assumed power of Florence), thought to be pro-[[Republic]] counterrevolutionary opposed to them
	- reflected on chaos and political instability among Italian city-states
	- like [[Thucydides]], writes of importance of personality in politics, power, the [[balance of power]], the formation of alliances, and causes of [[War]]/conflict between different city-states
		- primary focus: nat'l security
			- survival of state is paramount
		- prince can lose his state by not coping effectively with internal and external threats to his rule
			- [[Germany]]'s term [[Realpolitik]] (central to [[Realism]] thought), refers to power and power politics among states
	- [[The Prince by Niccolo Machiavelli]]
		- practice manual on how to gain, maintain, expand, and lose power
- [[Hobbes]] (1588 – 1679)
	- political philosophy developed during first 50 years of [[English Civil War]]
	- tutored [[Charles I]], who was later executed in 1649 during [[English Civil War]]
	- [[Leviathan by Thomas Hobbes]]
		- either a monarch or an assembly (parliament) could be tasked by the people to assure their security as the primary responsibility of gov't
		- marked the end of [[Divine Right of Kings]]
		- primary focus: domestic politics
		- goal: make the strongest case possible for the necessity of a powerful, centralized political authority
			- before creation of society, humans lived in a "state of nature", a condition of war of "every one against every one"
			- in this "state of war", there is "a continual fear and danger of violent death; and the life of man, solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short"
	- Like [[Machiavelli]] and [[Thucydides]], pessimistic view of human nature
	- his "state of war"
		- result of a thought experiment, imaging what the world would be like without gov'tal authority or any other social structure
		- interested in showing how people can escape from this hypothetical situation of the "state of nature"/"state of war" by agreeing to place all power in the hands of a sovereign, supreme leader ([[Leviathan]]) that would end [[anarchy]] of state of nature
			- uses power to maintain order, essential to daily life
	- his impact on [[Realism]] of [[IR]]
		- image of states as if they were individuals in a mythical state of nature
		- assessment of why conflict and violence between individuals/states are to be expected
			- in absence of sovereign/central, superordinate authority, the [[Anarchic]] world described by [[Hobbes]] is dismal
- [[Grotius]] (1583 – 1645)
	- [[Netherlands]] contemporary of [[Hobbes]]
	- part of [[classical realism]], related to [[Liberalism]], [[neoliberalism]], and [[The English School]] images
	- father of int'l law
	- introduces consideration of norms/rules that states follow in their enlightened self-interest 
		- perspective found in [[classical realism]] and [[neoclassical realism]]
- [[Carl von Clausewitz]] (1780 – 1831)
	- [[Prussia]]n officer, served in [[Napoleonic period]]'s [[War]]s
	- military element of state's power is extremely important, but subordinate always to political
	- consistent with [[Machiavelli]] on [[War]]
		- [[Carl von Clausewitz]]: war is "a continuation of political activity by other means"
	- [[War]]/use of force, implemented by resourceful commander in field
		- policy makers may choose rationally to accomplish their state objectives
- [[Sun Tzu]] (544 – 496 BCE)
	- from [[China]] during [[Warring States period]]
	- addresses ways and means of warfare
	- experiences of militaries of states and empires in 19th c. [[Europe]] draw upon ancient Chinese militaries
		- leads [[Carl von Clausewitz]] and [[Sun Tzu]] to draw similar conclusions
	- like [[Carl von Clausewitz]], puts premium on mental capacity of commanders
		- cleverness and adaptability to changing circumstances
		- reliance on intelligence
		- knowing when to fight and when not to do so
		- how to think strategically
			- maximizing advantage regardless of whether or not one's side has superior numbers
	- best approach to achieving objectives might not involve combat
	- superiority in command is achieving objectives without fighting at all
	- understood high costs of warfare in lives and treasure
		- rationally informs preference to avoid use of force when objectives can be achieved by other means
#### 5.2.1 Geopolitical Writers
- [[Geopolitics]]: a state's/empire's geographic location/territory it controls/seeks to control has significant political implications
	- a branch of [[Realism]] that fell into disfavor during [[WWII]] and [[Post-WWII]]
	- variations of geopolitical understandings return from time to time in minds of policy makers
- 2003: overthrowing of Saddam [[Hussein]] in [[Iraq]] ([[Iraq War]]/[[Second Gulf War]])
	- seen by geopolitical advocates as a strategic move to assure [[America]]n dominance of the region, despite how poorly this policy unfolded
	- [[Iraq]] was at the top of [[Persia]]n/[[Arab]] Gulf oil-export lanes
		- flanked by [[Iran]] and [[Pakistan]] to the east
		- flanked by [[Saudi Arabia]] and [[Syria]] to the south and west
- February 2022: [[Russia]]'s invasion of neighboring [[Ukraine]]
	- geopolitically justified by Vladimir [[Putin]]
		- claim: [[Ukraine]] was increasing turning to the West
- Alford Thayer [[Mahan]] (1840 – 1914)
	- [[America]]n naval strategist
	- observes how command of seas was central to [[British]] [[imperialism]] power
	- argues [[America]] could (and did) emulate [[UK]] in 20th c. starting with President [[Theodore Roosevelt]]'s pledge to build an American fleet that could circle the globe
		- basis for extension of American power and influence to [[Asia]] and [[Pacific]]
	- perspective remains part of US navy thinking
		- reflected in present-day [[Realism]] writings as "offshore balancing" grand strategy proposal
- Sir Halford [[Mackinder]] (1861 – 1947)
	- [[UK]], British geographer
	- focuses on control of land areas
	- [[The Geopolitical Pivot of History (1904) by Sir Halford Mackinder]] paper
		- "who rules [[Eastern Europe]] commands the heartland, who rules the heartland commands the world island [[Eurasia]], who rules the world island commands the world"
- Nicholas J. [[Spykman]] (1893 – 1943)
	- Yale political scientist
	- modifies [[Mackinder]]'s argument
		- defines "heartland" as core within the [[Eurasia]]n land mass, but not including the sea coast areas
			- sea coast areas – "rimland", extended to south and southeast [[Asia]] and all of Araby in [[Middle East]]
	- controlling the "rimland" was key to preventing dominance by any "heartland" power
	- "Who controls the rimland rules [[Eurasia]]" and "who rules [[Eurasia]] controls the destinies of the world"
- geopolitical basis for [[Cold War]] [[containment doctrine]] advanced by George [[Kennan]]
	- keep [[USSR]] within its borders by pursuing a policy of [[balance of power]] alliances that surrounded and contained the [[USSR]]
		- included [[China]] after [[Communism]] revolution there in 1949
	- both countries controlled eastern rimland, but neither had control of southern or western rimland of the [[Eurasia]]n "world island"
- Friedrich [[Ratzel]] (1844 – 1904)
	- [[Germany]]
	- Germany's failed effort to control [[Eurasia]]n "heartland" was an application of geopolitical thought influenced by him
	- racialized concept by writing of [[Lebensraum by Friedrich Ratzel]]
		- *Lebensraum* – "living space"
		- defined by National Socialists ([[Nazi]]s) under [[Hitler]] as land in [[Eurasia]] required by the Aryan "master race"
	- defeat of [[Germany]] in [[WWII]] led to rejection of such thinking
		- discredited geopolitical enterprise as a whole
#### 5.2.2 Mid-20th C. Writers
- [[E. H. Carr]] (1892 – 1982)
	- intellectual precursor for [[Realism]] and [[The English School]]
	- influential on the thinking of [[Liberalism]] and [[neoliberalism]]
- [[The Twenty Years' Crisis, 1919 – 1939 by E. H. Carr]]
	- an [[IR]] classic
	- written in 1939, shadow of war looming over [[Europe]]
	- attempts to analyze underlying and significant, rather than immediate and personal, causes of [[WWII]]
		- if this wasn't done, Carr argues we'll fail to understand how war could break out 20 short years after signing the [[Treaty of Versailles]] in 1919
	- dedicated book "to the makers of the coming peace"
	- echoes of [[Thucydides]], attempting to understand more profound causes of contemporary int'l crisis
	- emphasizes role of fear in explaining [[WWI]]
	- Carr refers to impact of [[Machiavelli]] and [[Hobbes]] on [[Realism]]
	- a critique of [[utopian]]/[[Idealism]] thought
		- these thoughts dominated fledgling discipline of [[IR]]
	- challenges more extreme versions of [[Realism]] that posit divorce of morality from politics in IR
		- Carr's argument: sound political thought must be based on elements of *utopia* (values) and *reality* (power)
			- [[Realism]] exposes [[utopian]]ism as a disguise for the privileged
	- [[classical realism]] understandings beyond power and interest
		- [[Grotius]]'s topics of morality in [[IR]], foundations of law, sanctity of treaties, judicial settlement of int'l disputes, peaceful change, prospects for new int'l order
	- assess strengths and weaknesses of [[utopian]]ism and [[Realism]]
	- insightful critique on proposed [[Liberalism]] solutions to problems of recreating int'l following [[WWI]]
	- relevant to [[post-Cold War]] era given [[China]]'s rise and [[Russia]]'s reassertion of power
- Reinhold [[Niebuhr]] (1892 – 1971)
	- [[America]]n theologian
	- [[utopian]]/[[Idealism]] belief
	- his [[Christianity]], informed by horrors of [[WWI]], made him a pacifist during interwar period
	- doubt of his [[Idealism]] as ineffectual for dealing with real-world problems leads him to adopt "[[Christian realism]]" perspective
		- absence of an effective, power-based challenge to the rise of national socialism ([[Nazi]]) and [[fascism]] 
			- [[Niebuhr]] abandons pacifist sentiments
			- [[Niebuhr]] embraces [[Realism]] combined with moral understandings
				- same conclusion as [[E. H. Carr]]'s secular argument on essential relation between value and power
- Hans J. [[Morgenthau]] (1904 – 1980)
	- [[classical realism]]
		- emphasis on holistic approach to [[IR]] that encompasses all levels of analysis to include:
			- impact of human nature
			- blurring of the distinction between society and int'l system
			- concern for justice
	- born in [[Germany]], [[Jewish]]
		- flees to [[America]] when [[Nazi]]s rise to power
	- professor at University of Chicago
- [[Politics among Nations (1948) by Hans J. Morgenthau]]
	- tutorial for [[Post-WWII]] [[America]]n policy makers
		- who now lead a country of preeminent int'l power
		- who no longer could seriously contemplate [[isolationism]] from the rest of the world
	- 6 principles of [[political realism]]:
		1. "politics, like society in general, is governed by objective laws that have their roots in human nature"
		2. in int'l politics, "interest is defined in terms of power"
		3. interest defined as power is not a fixed definition: "the kind of interest determining political action depends on the political and cultural context within which foreign policy is formulated
		4. there is "tension between the moral command and the requirements of successful political action," but as a practical matter, "universal moral principles must be filtered through the concrete circumstances of time and place"
		5. "[[political realism]] refuses to identify the moral aspirations of a particular nation with the moral laws that govern the universe"
		6. "interest defined as power" is an understanding that gives int'l politics a separate standing and thus emancipates it from other fields of study
	- [[Morgenthau]] helps establish [[IR]] as a separate discipline from [[political science]]
		- not just a part of [[History]], int'l law, or [[Philosophy]]
	- "the essence of int'l politics is identical with its domestic counterpart. Both domestic and int'l politics are a struggle for power.... The tendency to dominate, in particular, is an element of all human associations."
	- like [[Thucydides]] and [[Ancient World]] [[Greek]]s, [[Morgenthau]] had tragic view of [[IR]]
		- [[History]] is full of examples where individuals and the states they represent made a grab for int'l domination
			- alliances and [[balance of power]] failed to keep the peace
		- temptation to overturn existing power arrangements and norms of int'l conduct strongly pulled at leaders
			- e.g. [[Ancient World]]'s [[Athens]], [[Rome]], absolutist in [[France]] in [[Napoleonic period]], imperial [[Germany]], [[Hitler]]'s [[Third Reich]]
- John H. [[Herz]] (1908 – 2005)
	- [[Germany]]-born, [[America]]n professor
	- nuanced view on the way values, not just raw power, influence the conduct of [[IR]]
	- synthesis between power and values: [[realist liberalism]]
		- [[Idealism]] int'list
		- one can still achieve ideals in a world of power politics
		- compliance w/int'l law may be in the enlightened self-interest of states
		- [[classical realism]] incorporated both power and values/aspirations
	- [[security dilemma]] arises from military or other measures taken by states to compete
		- leads to countermeasures taken by adversaries
		- consequence of action-reaction behavior:
			- neither side advances its [[security]]
		- actions taken by one side threaten the opponent and lead to a response that undermines the security of one's side
- debate between [[Herz]] and [[Morgenthau]]
	- 1980 Int'l Studies Association
		- [[Herz]]: universalist vision
			- consistent with [[Realism]] understanding
			- states could take on difficult global challenges
				- challenges including: population pressure, resources depletion, environmental destruction, arms races, nuclear superarmament
- [[Realism]]'s occupation with armed conflict/[[War]]
	- concern with causes and consequences of conflict
		- helps explain why the [[Realism]] perspective is held widely by policy makers throughout the world
			- over centuries, leaders engaged in the very battles and struggles described by [[Thucydides]] to [[Morgenthau]]
	- [[Realism]], from the statesman's POV, is realistic as it tends to correspond to historical understandings and personal experiences in [[diplomacy]] and [[War]]
- [[Realism]]'s two basic concepts traditionally the foci of analysis at state and int'l levels
	1. *power* in which states are the principal actors
	2. *system* in which states are the principal actors
	- will discuss how realists have attempted to define these terms
	- will give examples on how theorists have used these concepts to generate insights and explanations of the causes of [[War]]
	- will discuss how realists deal with the concepts of [[Globalization]], [[interdependence]], and change
	- concludes with a critique of [[Realism]] image of [[IR]]
