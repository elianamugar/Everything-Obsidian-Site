- Homer to Thucydides (8th c. BCE – 400 BCE)
	- time of turmoil in which security of Greek city-states was a central issue
	- realist notions of power and balance-of-power politics
- ***[[The Iliad]]*** and ***[[The Odyssey]]*** (Homer): aristocratic warrior-chiefs rule over loosely united and defined territories
	- many city-states during Homer's time, small populations
	- domination of cities by family was weakening by the time of his death
- **Trojan War** (Thucydides): "these various Hellenic states, weak in themselves and lacking communications with one another, took no kind of collective action before the time of the Trojan War.... Indeed, my view is that at this time the whole country was not even called 'Hellas' \[Greece]"
	- Aristotle: village = union of families, city = union of villages
	- far from the modern state
- Introduction of the alphabet –> laws –> strengthening rights of citizens
- Greek population grew –> groups leave to form colonies
	- Italy, Sicily, coast of the Black Sea
- Increased contact with world –> more trade –> more warfare
- **Hoplite Phalanx:** heavily armed, spear- and shield-carrying Greek infantrymen working close together, basic form of military organization
	- encouraged development of the **polis**
		1. devalued the role of Homer's warrior-chieftain who heroically engages in single combat; steadfastness among the ranks was the key
		2. required a communal effort and drew on the dedication and courage of all male citizens
			- courage was enhanced if the soldier believed he was fighting for his liberty and the continuation of his way of life
#### 2.1.2 Homer's Epic Poems
- history and epic poetry were closely associated
- Homer's influence on later Greek writers
- raised questions about the ability of individuals to control their fates and the nature of cause and effect in politics and int'l relations
	- Herodotus, Thucydides, Plato, Aristotle, Polybius, later Roman historians
##### 2.1.2.1 Determinism and Voluntarism
- [[The Iliad]]
	- tragedy about war
	- a vivid and tragic view of the world, depicting impact of events on both humans and the divine
	- choices by individuals have dramatic consequences (cause of war between Greeks and Trojans)
		- different from modern world: divergent ideologies, militaristic societies, extreme nationalism
	- depictions of battles are combat between individuals
		- bravery and other virtues decide the outcome
			- different from modern warfare: superiority of weaponry
	- humans share center stage with gods
		- gods determine matters of life and death
			- constant intervention places boundaries/limits on the ability of humans to control their fates
	- humans are not merely the captive of fate, the gods, or human nature
	- individuals can have a decisive effect on the initiation, conduct, outcome of wars because human agency (free will, volition) is possible
	- belief that gods/supernatural forces play a role = early example of **determinism**
		- **[[determinism]]:** chance phenomena or the uncertainty that surrounds human events
- [[Machiavelli]]
	- coping with uncertainty or fortune and the issue of volition, voluntarism, free will
	- *[[Fortuna]]* - chance phenomena feminized in effect as "Lady Luck"
- [[Carl von Clausewitz]] (1832)
	- treatment of uncertainties that obtain in the "fog of war" and "friction"
		- when put to the test, plans for combat are often at odds with realities that confront military commanders
- The *Odyssey*
	- romance about war
	- importance of physical, moral, and intellectual strength that allows humans to prevail in their competition with external forces
#### 2.1.3 Herodotus – The "Father of History"
- details the Persian wars
	- **Cyrus the Great** (d. 529 BCE)
	- **Cambyses** (d. 521 BCE)
	- **Darius** (d. 486 BCE)
	- **Xerxes** (d. 465 BCE)
- first half - rise of Persia
- second half - conflict between Persia and Greece
- purpose: "set down to preserve the memory of the past by putting on record the astonishing achievements both of our own and of other peoples; and more particularly, to show how they came into conflict"
- importance to IR
	1. conflict among different city-states, empires, and peoples is the pervasive theme throughout his work
	2. the primary interest of leaders has historically been the realist one of maintaining the security and autonomy of the state through the marshaling of a state's internal resources or by its participation in alliances (e.g. *The Histories*)
	3. interest in how fate or the gods tended to frustrate the aspirations of statesmen and military leaders
##### 2.1.3.1 Herodotus on Conflict and War
- *The Histories*
	- emphasis on description than on causal or other analysis of events
	- does not lay out a basic argument as to the underlying cause of war between Persia and its neighbors
	- war as simply a fact of life, an occurrence as natural and inevitable as the changing of the seasons
- familiarity with other cultures, states, or empires doesn't mean acceptance of them, much less any notion that there could be a community among them
	- "Like the Egyptians, the Scythians are dead-set against their foreign ways, especially against Greek ways"
	- warring among the ancient people was the dominant mode of IR
		- defeat meant the destruction of a city, death of all male adults (or enslavement), selling of women and children into slavery
- emphasis on *individual level of analysis*
- the desire of a ruler to secure power, glory, wealth, revenge is often all that's required to plunge a state/empire into war
	- **Croesus** (d. 547 BCE), king of Lydia - craving to extend his territories
	- **Xerxes**, king of Persia - his plan to invade Greece and destroy Athens
##### 2.1.3.2 Herodotus on Security
- desire of leaders to expand power, leading to conflicts
	- key interest: maintaining the state's respective security and autonomy
- realist - emphasis on survival/preservation (security)
- Athens wanted to command a combined fleet from several Greek city-states
	- met with dissent
	- Athenians "waived their claim in the interest of national survival"
		- understood the danger with a lack of unity - tried to avoid internal strife
			- "a quarrel about the command would certainly mean the destruction of Greece" at the hands of the Persians
- use of deception as a means to survive and reap benefit
	- **Darius**, king of Persia: "If a lie is necessary, why not speak it? We are all after the same thing, whether we lie or speak the truth: our own advantage. Men lie when they think to profit by deception, and tell the truth for the same reason – to get something they want, and to be the better trusted for their honesty. It is only two different roads to the same goal. Were there no question of advantage, the honest man would be as likely to lie as the liar is, and the liar would tell the truth as readily as the honest man."
- Herodotus believes the highest reward for an individual was to be honored and respected by citizens of the polis (a special political unit)
	- the worst possible fate was to be exiled
	- to fight bravely for a city-state was commendable, and death would bestow an individual some immortality
- emphasis on state as the object of one's loyalty and devotion
	- **Croesus**, king of Lydia and **Solon**: **Tellus** of Athens will be king because he had a glorious death in battle
- Basic approaches leaders can follow to maintain a state's security
	1. marshaling internal resources
	2. creation of an alliance
- **Kenneth N. Waltz:** realist who observed how states resort to internal or external means to enhance their security
- if states cannot deter an aggressor through the existence of a [[balance of power]], then sufficient internal resources have to be mustered to repel the invader
- importance of a state controlling economic and military capabilities
	- Herodotus on Darius: "having an immense revenue in money and an unlimited number of men to draw upon" = strong enough to take on enemies
	- Herodotus on Athens vs. Aegina: used large sum of money to build 200 ships
- superior economic and military capabilities were not enough to guarantee victory – the nature of a social-political system is just as important a capability
	- Herodotus on Athens: fighting capabilities were greater when Athens was in a democratic period with freedoms assured than "while they were oppressed under a despotic government"
		- **Oliver Cromwell** in the English civil war
		- **George Washington** in the American revolution
		- **Napoleon Bonaparte** in the French revolutionary war
			- all depended on citizen soldiers to defeat armies made up of professionals and mercenaries
- emphasis on rule of law that set the Greeks apart from the Persians and other "barbarians"
	- e.g. Xerxes vs. [[Demaratus]]
- Herodotus thinks the nature of a political-social system is a potential source of power that influences state behavior
	- around this time, debate on relative merits of democracy, oligarchy, and monarchy
	- belief that if the people rule, they will have "typical vices of a monarch" leading "to acts of savage and unnatural violence"
	- contrasting argument: when people are in power, they "do none of the things that monarchs do"
- Darius on systems
	- democracy - malpractices are bound to occur
	- oligarchy - violent personal feuds, quarrels that lead to open dissension, then to bloodshed
- Herodotus's point: democracies are best able to ensure the security of the state as the citizens have their liberty to lose
	- Livy and Machiavelli address this theme
		- argument: republican regimes in which people have some degree of influence in the government are the most secure, but not necessarily peaceful
- strength in unity and alliances
	- lack of unity undercuts the capability to provide for defense
	- unity against an external security threat is enhanced if the threatened states have developed some sense of common identity
		- e.g. Sparta and Athens for Greece
	- in the absence of an external threat, common bonds might not be enough to unite people sharing a common identity
		- Greeks tended to start fights among themselves
		- self-note: so this lack of unity amongst early Greek city-states seems to follow the pattern of the Middle-Eastern country borders, where they are more united by their people groups rather than their country. coincidentally the Middle-East is where these early civilizations started as well. the chaos runs in their history.
##### 2.1.3.3 Fate – Determinism vs. Voluntarism
- Herodotus includes a great deal of metaphysical ability of leaders as agents to control and predict international events
- oracles and prophecy do matter and heavily influence, if not determine, decisions and outcomes
	- oracle = truth teller
- Herodotus doesn't necessarily accept determinism, but instead is descriptive of these influences on decision-making
