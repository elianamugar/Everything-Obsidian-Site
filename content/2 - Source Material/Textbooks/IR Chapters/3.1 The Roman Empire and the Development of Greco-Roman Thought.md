#### 3.1.2 The Greek Stoics
- 4th c. BCE: Greek world in decline, but continued to exert cultural influence
	- Hellenization of "barbarians" continued for several centuries
	- rise of bureaucratic empires
		- increased individual mobility
		- people of different regions and backgrounds settle in Greece and infuse their own cultures into Hellenic world
- 300 BCE: [[Stoicism]] founded by [[Zeno]] (Cypriot of Phoenician background)
	- acceptance of one's circumstances and self-reliance (individuals)
	- we are part of a larger community of humanity (cosmopolitan notion, relevant to IR theory)
	- Stoic ideas influential in Rome
		- influenced worldviews of 17th c. Hugo [[Grotius]], 18th c. Immanuel [[Kant]]
	- at the core of contemporary liberal thought
		- liberal thought: sees the world made up not only of states, but also individuals, groups, other non-state actors who can influence events
- Stoicism does not develop a theory of state/state action
	- emphasis on individual as an independent actor
		- unlike Plato and Aristotle in which the individual was a member of an organized political unit
	- Stoicism requires individual to not focus only on laws/customs of their political community, but to live in accordance with *nature*
- Stoic conception of nature
	- nature involves certain goals and principles and processes by which they are attained with the supreme good being to lead a life of "virtue"
	- **it should be the duty of each individual to live according to nature and achieve in one's own life the harmony found in nature itself**
	- to secure the good life, an individual attempts to reduce elements of chance and circumstance
- Stoicism: ability to reason is a quality shared by all humans
	- early Christians incorporate Stoic understandings into their religion
	- reason is a divine spark, reflecting God within us
		- God is the author of laws of nature and universe
	- universal ability to reason AND laws of nature are universally applicable results in Stoic emphasis on equality of people and what unites over what divides
- Stoicism emphasis on equality and unity of humanity
	- different from Aristotle's view of laws of nature
		- Aristotle: equality is NOT inherently in the laws of nature
			- observes subordination of women to men in city-states, slaves to citizens, barbarians to Hellenes
	- differs from Thucydides's depiction
		- even the common cultural heritage of the Greeks could not prevent personal ambition, love of power, and dynamics of city-state competition from leading to war
- Stoics believe humans can discern through reason the laws of nature and live in accordance with them
	- to an extent, nature determines the course of an individual's life
	- an individual has a certain amount of free will as well
	- Stoicism balances determinism and voluntarism
		- contrasts [[The Republic by Plato]] ([[Plato]]): what an individual does in life is determined by heredity and environment with the state/city-state playing a critical role in the socialization process
- Stoic emphasis on unity and equality had political implications
	- one has to accept the philosophical notion that the world is held together by laws of nature that transcend the laws of any king or emperor
	- laws made by kings and communities are derived from the laws of nature (early forms of [[Divine Right of Kings]])
- Stoics raise a debated issue: if everyone is part of humanity, to what extent do we have obligations to humanity as a whole as opposed to the more narrowly defined political community in which we live?
- Stoic's early secular justifications for a world state
	- [[Zeno]] writes a tract that attacks [[Plato]]'s [[The Republic by Plato]] 
		- attacks the idea that an individual state should strive for the attainment of Plato's ideal community
		- [[Zeno]]: the ideal state is a world state with universal citizenship and one system of law based on reason as opposed to custom and convention #quote 
#### 3.1.3 The Roman Stoics
- Romans developed Stoicism and put it into practice
	- the organizing principle by which Rome ruled was a universal image of humanity that transcended boundaries of city-states and other small political units
- [[Seneca]] and [[St. Paul]] (Paul of Tarsus) were Roman citizens
	- St. Paul – early Jewish convert to Christianity in Asia Minor (modern-day [[Turkey]])
		- missionary work among non-Jewish people
		- credited with assuring "Romanization" of early Christianity
- the Catholicism of [[Christianity]]
	- open to all human beings regardless of origin
		- conformed to [[universalism]] present in Roman thought
			- departure from Judaic and other traditions that supported more exclusive communities
- autonomy to local rulers for practical reasons
	- transportation and communication were difficult
	- Stoic ideas provided legitimacy to this approach
- Roman Empire expansion
	- ideas central to Greco-Roman thought spread
		- influential to Western political thought
	- *[[jus naturale]]* – ideas of natural law
	- *[[jus gentium]]* – a law commonly binding on all people

##### 3.1.4.1 Laws of Nature
- influence of Stoicism on Cicero
	- conception of one world ordered by laws of nature
		- discoverable by using human reason
	- influenced by Greek Stoics, Cicero later influenced Roman Stoics
- people should be united: "as one and the same Nature holds together and supports the universe, all of whose parts are in harmony with one another"
	- beyond civil law of a commonwealth
	- law among nations applicable to humanity as a whole (*jus gentium*) AND people individually
- how Cicero views law and justice
	- [[Cicero]]: "the bond that united the civic association, the justice enforced by law" #quote 
	- law is a natural force, the root of justice is in nature
		- "True law is right reason in agreement with nature"
	- true law is of universal application, unchanging and everlasting
	- Cicero: one eternal and unchangeable law should be valid for all nations and all times
		- if some laws are based on local customs, the law of nations and of particular communities should conform with what nature dictates uniformly
	- Cicero: law is based on rational principles
		- human reason is how you discover the law in nature
		- law is not merely a product of human thought
			- law is "something eternal that rules the whole universe"
		- law has a divine quality
- Cicero's liberal worldview
	- individuals and states matter
	- law should play an important role in regulating relations among people and states
- Cicero's economics
	- individually oriented position (economic individualism)
	- advocates moderation, saving money, increasing one's wealth
		- when accompanied with hard work –> [[Max Weber]]'s [[The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism by Max Weber]]
	- private property an important component of his political works
	- follows Stoic tradition of acquisition of wealth and material possessions as a natural activity
		- influences [[Adam Smith]] and social contract theorists like [[Locke]] ([[social contract theory]])
##### 3.1.4.2 State and Statecraft
- Cicero contributes to realist perspective on int'l politics
	- how he conceptualizes the state
	- his advice to political leaders
- Cicero's "social contract"
	- a result of individuals' natural quest for security
	- to attain this safety, commonwealth comes into existence by mutual agreement
	- to make human life more secure, increase the resources of humankind (multiply wealth, extend boundaries, etc.)
	- justice requires us "to consider the interests of the whole human race"
- Cicero believes the noblest use of virtue is service to the gov't of the state
- political leaders' responsibility
	- maintaining domestic order and stability
	- security of the state
	- Cicero: law and legal system are central to security
		- two elements contribute to stability of state
			1. religion
			2. "spirit of tranquility"
				- in Rome's earlier history, fighting was needed to quench people's ardor for warlike life
				- popular passions can be turned towards the market, games, large gathering events, etc.
					- "turn toward benevolence and kindliness the thought of those who had become savage and brutish through their passion for war"
- Cicero: a "certain corruption and degradation of morals" may stem from foreign influences
	- especially vulnerable were maritime cities
		- exposure to traders from all over the world
##### 3.1.4.3 Justice and War
- war should be subordinate to law
	- Cicero initiated this idea in Western political thought
	- [[Augustine]] and [[Thomas Aquinas]] would build on this
- Cicero's idea of a [[just war doctrine]] – first known systematic treatment of this subject
	- basis for [[just war theory]]
	- law and justice *through* law apply to the decision to go to war (*jus ad bellum*) AND the conduct of the war itself (*[[jus in bello]]*) AND restoration of peace
	- war to be waged for *legitimate objectives*
	- unjust wars – undertaken without provocation
	- just wars – revenge or defense
		- waged by legitimate authorities
			- [[Cicero]]: "no war is considered just unless it's been proclaimed and declared" by State authorities #quote 
		- upon declaration, authorities should wage just wars justly
		- assumed that commanders' orders are justice
			- expected obedience from troops on battlefield
	- legal provisions for diplomacy of war AND peace

##### 3.1.5.1 Humanity and Reason
- [[Seneca]]
	- acknowledges [[Zeno]] as founder of Stoic school
	- far removed from [[Realism]], believes in fellowship and humankind working together as a community
- Stoic concept of humanity
	- beyond individuals and their relationship
- values embrace all humanity
	- universals exist, discoverable through use of human reason 
		- different from Greek and Roman Stoics who wrote more about particular political communities
- broader Stoic perspective influences scholars in [[Middle Ages]]
	- accepted the idea of [[universalism]], central to [[classical liberalism]] and strategies for universal peace
- [[Marcus Aurelius]]'s influence on [[Kant]]
	- favors community based on equality, freedom of speech for all
	- individuals naturally part of a social/human community
	- does not agree with estrangement/allow one to be cut off from society
	- [[Liberalism]] worldview – unity despite wide diversity
		- at odds with [[Plato]] and [[Aristotle]]'s thinking – society is highly stratified and people outside of Greece are barbarians
	- each person should conform their own nature ([[Plato]])
		- identity is not defined in [[class conflict]] terms
- order and harmony can exist
	- [[Marcus Aurelius]]: "the interest of every creature lies in conformity with its own constitution and nature. My own nature is a rational and civic one; I have a city, and I have a country; as Marcus I have Rome and, as a human being, I have the universe; and consequently, what is beneficial to these communities is the sole good for me." #quote 
##### 3.1.5.2 State of Nature
- Seneca develops an image of the state of nature as foundation for his political theory
	- fellowship has always been natural among humans
		- primitive [[Communism]] – share and share-alike
	- influence on [[social contract theory]], [[Locke]], [[Rousseau]]
		- state of nature as a metaphor with implications for both domestic politics and IR
- Seneca claims human innocence is a result of ignorance
	- [[Seneca]]: humans "lack cardinal virtues of justice, moral insight, self-control, and courage" – vulnerable to having their happy world upset #quote 
- Seneca on virtue
	- [[Seneca]]: "Virtue only comes to a character that has been thoroughly schooled and trained and brought to a pitch of perfection by unremitting practice. We are born for it, but not with it. And even in the best of people, until you cultivate it there is only the material for virtue, not virtue itself." #quote 
- Marcus Aurelius sees humans as motivated to serve the common good and community
	- influence on [[Rousseau]]'s 18th c. concept of *general will* and [[Realism]] concept of *unitary state*
		- Marcus: one ought to act consistently with the "general accord" of one's community
- cosmopolitan worldview – common good or interest goes beyond the confines of any given community
	- Marcus: mutual integration is a universal principle
- Seneca adopts Aristotle's anti-market bias
	- [[Seneca]]: he has "no respect for any study whatsoever if its end is the making of money" #quote 
	- [[Seneca]]: "there is really only one liberal study that deserves the name–because it makes a person free–and that is the pursuit of wisdom" #quote 
- Seneca believes privatization of property upsets harmony of interest in the state of nature
	- [[Seneca]]: "...avarice... only succeeded in making everything somebody else's property and reducing its possessions to a fraction of its previous unlimited wealth. Avarice brought in property." #quote 
	- humans now live with insecurity that contrasts early happy state of life in conformity with nature when to govern is to serve, not to rule
- wise leaders are devoted to bringing well-being and prosperity to their subjects
	- [[Seneca]]: "men's greed broke society up" #quote 
	- [[Seneca]]: with time, "avarice and luxury split humans up and got them to abandon partnership for plunder" #quote 
- [[Seneca]]: although "we were born into a world in which things were made ready to our hands, it is we who have made everything difficult to come by through our disdain for what is easily come by" #quote 
	- against love of money and extravagance
	- proper limit of person's wealth should be restricted to what's essential
	- theme of distribution of wealth in society
		- appears later in [[social contract theory]] and [[classical economic theory]]
- Seneca departs from Aristotle's defense of slavery
	- condemns slavery harshly and its inhuman behavior, especially abuse of slaves
	- they are too important to be subordinate to narrow constructions of economic practicality
	- [[Marx]] in 19th c. identifies slavery as a historically distinct mode of production found in ancient societies ([[Egypt]], [[Greece]], [[Roman]])
##### 3.1.5.3 Causation and Fortune
- [[Seneca]] contributes to [[Epistemology]] (the study of how one knows or how one gains knowledge)
	- like [[Polybius]], Seneca argues causation can be understood as those elements necessary for its existence/occurrence
- [[Marcus Aurelius]]: "what follows is ever closely linked to what precedes; it is not a procession of isolated events, merely obeying the laws of sequence, but also a rational continuity" #quote 
	- humans have the capacity to reason and discover these causal relations
- to be human is to live with uncertainty and skepticism about reality
	- [[Seneca]]: "I should find it difficult to say which of these people annoy me most, those who would have us know nothing or the ones who refuse even to leave us the small satisfaction of knowing that we know nothing." #quote 
	- chance is part of the causal equation
		- [[Seneca]]: one should "always take full note of fortune's habit of behaving just as she pleases, treating her as if she were actually going to do everything it is in her power to do" #quote 
	- life is fragile and we are all vulnerable to the unexpected
- Seneca encourages individuals to keep on course in the pursuit of ideals even when confronted with setbacks
	- Marcus Aurelius is more of an optimist
		- humans can observe the world of space and time
		- we can expect changes over time
		- reason allows humans to find patterns
			- [[Marcus Aurelius]]: we can "look back over the past, with its changing empires that rose and fell" and, turning to prediction, we "can foresee the future too" #quote 
- human rational faculty gives humans the capacity to control our conduct
	- even with uncertainties and chance, humans retain free will
	- not everything is determined for us
	- individuals matter and what they do matters
	- [[Marcus Aurelius]] cites [[Epictetus]]: ([[voluntarism]]) the "robber of your free will does not exist." #quote 
- Seneca and Marcus Aurelius in favor of the dignity of all individuals
	- reflects [[cosmopolitanism]] alien to classical Greek thinking and supporters of Roman Empire who viewed most people beyond borders as barbarians
	- high degree of [[voluntarism]] with respect to counteracting fate and fortune
	- intellectual precursors of [[Liberalism]]
#### 3.1.6 Titus Livy

	- [[Cicero]]'s works provide guidance on theory and practice of historical writing
	- known for [[History of Rome by Titus Livy]] – 142 books
		- 9 BCE: ends with the death of Drusus, brother of [[Tiberius]]
		- only 35 books survive
- Cicero and Livy believe history should serve as a guide to life
- Livy's influence
	- [[The Discourses by Niccolo Machiavelli]] subject was Livy's first ten books
	- [[Montesquieu]] and [[Rousseau]]
	- [[The Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire by Edward Gibbons]] ([[Gibbons]])
		- Livy sees moral decay as undermining Rome's power position
			- [[Livy]]: "wealth has made us greedy, and self-indulgence has brought us, through every form of sensual excess, to be, if I may so put it, in love with death both individual and collective" #quote 
- [[Livy]]: "In history you have a record of the infinite variety of human experience plainly set out for all to see; and in that record you can find yourself and your country both examples and warnings; fine things to take as models, base things, rotten through and through, to avoid." #quote 
- Livy's factual problems of early Roman history
	- founding of Rome by Romulus and Remus is a myth recorded by a historian

- Livy's moral concerns
	- deplores prevalence of sexual promiscuity, murder, plunder, etc.
- Livy's themes related to IR
	- security and war
	- fortune and volition
	- class
	- [[Republicanism]]
##### 3.1.6.1 Security and War
- Livy: survival (the safety of the state) has always been the primary goal of Roman leaders
	- consul should protect the state
	- external threats
		- Senate had to hold their allies down by fear
		- use of citizens and not mercenaries influences [[Machiavelli]]'s [[The Prince by Niccolo Machiavelli]] and [[The Discourses by Niccolo Machiavelli]]
- what are the causes of war?
	- Livy: war is the product of rational choice
	- councils evaluate pros and cons of going to war and reach a decision
	- wars are not accidental; they're calculated to achieve certain objectives ([[Realism]] assumption)
		- territorial expansion maybe
	- war was how the Roman Empire was built
- war between two parties are observed by those likely to be affected by the outcome
	- example: King Philip of Macedon watched two wealthiest parties (Rome and Carthage) fighting
	- sometimes two parties fight due to relations with third parties
- Livy identifies important economic underpinnings of military capability that contribute to power of state
	- ability to raise money to finance wars is important
	- resource limitations constrain military options
	- stocks of weapons, provisions, money are essential to successful war efforts
	- availability of personnel
- Livy: military service for the public good takes precedence over private or personal concerns
	- soldier qualities of courage, discipline, endurance
	- fairness and consistency are essential in military discipline
- favorable geography is important
	- natural defenses
	- population for military
- forming alliances can increase one's power, reduce external threats, and enhance security ([[Realism]])
	- Livy sees the pitfall of being drawn into war by alliance commitments
	- Livy also sees these arrangements as necessary to compensate for a state's lack of independent economic or military capability
	- alliances allow for coordination of strategy and pooling of resources
- Livy: war is not necessarily all bad, but there are horrors of war
- Livy: the horrible effects of war should be moderated
	- consistent with [[Cicero]]: resort to war should be justified (*jus ad bellum*)
##### 3.1.6.2 Fortune and Volition
- Livy: [[Alexander the Great]] as model commander
	- enjoyed good fortune that never failed him
	- unaccounted-for variables might affect military equation positively or adversely
		- developed previously by [[Thucydides]] and [[Polybius]]
		- developed later by [[Machiavelli]] and [[Carl von Clausewitz]]
- Livy: fortune is an extremely important factor
	- fortunes may change
	- mark of a good commander, Livy: one able "to seize his good fortune when it offers and turn to good use any unexpected stroke of luck"
	- [[Hannibal]] (military commander): "Many problems naturally difficult are solved by a little brainwork." #quote 
		- caution and patience are virtues
		- one does not rush into battle
		- one should avoid precipitate action that's associated with the impatience of youth
		- when one's prepared, there's value in taking the offensive
			- devastating the enemy's country instead of seeing your own ravaged
		- one engages in "removing the threat from oneself" and "bringing the other man into peril"
- humans are not powerless and can make efforts to effect desirable outcomes
##### 3.1.6.3 Class Analysis
- Livy's work analyses of [[class conflict]] and interclass conflict between patricians and plebeians
	- influences [[Marxism]]/[[Marx]] and [[Economic Structuralism]]/[[Economic structuralists]]
	- members of Roman Senate opposed legalizing intermarriage between nobility ([[patrician]]s) and commons ([[plebeian]]s)
		- "patrician blood would thereby be contaminated"
		- "hereditary rights and privileges of the *gentes*, or families, would be lost"
	- plebeians would elect "men of their own class, and the most turbulent demagogues at that"
- Livy: rulers who favor their own patrician class and alienate the lower classes forget that their power rests ultimately on popular support
	- leaders may be confronted with revolutionary regimes
	- 371 BCE: the opportunity for a revolution came as a result of the debt incurred by state authorities
		- same in 210 BCE
- [[Livy]]: "nobles were grabbing possession of public land, and there would be no room left there for the common people unless it was shared out before they seized it all" #quote 
	- [[Livy]]: patricians preoccupied with "all the objects for which men's desire knows no bounds–land, money, advancement" #quote 
	- exploitation of plebeians resulted in their enslavement for debt
- class conflict does not advance society and can be a catalyst for revolutionary activity
	- Livy: divisiveness can weaken the state, exposing it to the threat of foreign invasion
- Livy: authority in the able hands of an effective ruler would keep the state secure
- Livy: public support was important to Rome's power growth
- Livy: an individual's abilities, not their class origin, should be the decisive factor
##### 3.1.6.4 Republicanism
- Livy: domestic factors affect the security of the state
	- the strength of the state depends on the legitimacy and cohesiveness of the political regime
- Livy's preferred government – [[representative government]] or [[Republic]] ([[The Discourses by Niccolo Machiavelli]], [[Machiavelli]])
	- idealizes republican Rome
		- [[Livy]]: "governed by annually elected officers of state and subject not to the caprice of individual men, but to the overriding authority of law" #quote 
	- ultimate power in the state rests with the people rather than an individual ruler who exercises authority gained from popular support
		- patriotism is an identification with the political community that's founded on respect for family, love of country, and contributes to cohesiveness of state
	- declaration of laws makes possibly a unified body politic
		- ruler's legitimacy enhanced
		- Rome was originally founded by force of arms, but a new basis of law and religious observance replaced brute force for a political community
	- changes in int'l environment effects domestic law
		- [[Livy]]: "Laws passed in peacetime are frequently cancelled by war; and peace often repeals the legislation of wartime: just as, in the handling of a ship some methods are of service in fair weather, other methods in time of storm" #quote 
- Roman idea of int'l law
	- treaties are binding (*pacta sunt servanda*)
		- Livy criticizes those who break treaty obligations
	- circumstances at the time the treaty was signed may have changed (*rebus sic stantibus*)
		- results in alteration of original treaty commitments
- Livy's fleeting reference to "matters of divine and human law"
	- developed later in political theory of [[Middle Ages]]
- Livy's observations on it'l law and war
	- followed [[Polybius]]
	- influenced [[Grotius]]
	- nature of republican Rome accounts for its ascendancy in such a short period
	- [[Republicanism]] enhances domestic security and stability of the state
		- aids in dealing with foreign threats and expansion of empire
	- [[Machiavelli]] analyzes and praises benefits of domestic and international security of [[Republic]]s
	- [[Kant]] believes in int'l system composed of [[Republic]]s as the best hope for int'l peace, not a source of imperialist [[expansionism]]
#### 3.1.7 Plutarch
- [[Plutarch]] (46 – 120 CE)
	- one of the last classical Greek historians
	- born in Chaeronea (east of Delphi)
	- studied philosophy in [[Athens]]
	- influenced by [[Plato]] 
	- influenced by the idea that knowledge is virtue
	- well-known scholar and diplomat to [[Rome]]
	- [[Lives of the Noble Greeks and Romans]] portraits:
		- [[Solon]], [[Pericles]], [[Lysander]], [[Alexander the Great]], [[Julius Caesar]], [[Pompey]], [[Cicero]], [[Cato]]
	- agrees with [[Livy]]: better understanding of past encourages contemporary leaders to emulate the more virtuous of their predecessors
	- his work showed Greeks and Romans that they could benefit from each other's traditions
- Plutarch's view of momentous events
	- in personal terms
	- not interested in uncovering underlying historical processes (unlike [[Thucydides]])
	- does not claim there's a natural and historical sequence in the life of a state (unlike [[Polybius]])
	- individual level of analysis was supreme
		- statesmen's policies are a function of their personalities, not the nature of the state or int'l system
- Plutarch's view on service of the state
	- service to the state rather than the Stoic emphasis on humanity as a whole is a virtue
	- praises [[Pericles]]
- Plutarch's criterion of greatness
	- whether or not a soldier or politician is willing and able to overcome personal vices in aid of security of the state
		- respect for patriotic men of action
- Plutarch reserves a deep respect for individuals who lead a virtuous private life, but when a state's interests are at stake, another standard of behavior is acceptable
	- [[Aristides]] on foreign policy: "followed whatever policy his country had adopted, recognizing that this must involve a great deal of injustice on occasion" #quote 
- Plutarch is not completely voluntarist; recognizes limits to effective action
	- one commendable leader may be up against a strong rival
	- admits to the role of fate in determining outcomes
	- BUT emphasizes volition/free will the most
#### 3.1.8 Reflections on Thought in the Roman Empire
- writers associated with [[Stoicism]] and their analyses of [[Republic]] of Rome and imperial Rome
	- intersection of the two: spiritual character of Stoicism made it the preferred and professed philosophy of Rome's educated elite
- Conversion of [[Constantine]] (306 – 337 CE) to Christianity
	- [[Christianity]] new religion of Roman Empire
	- embedded Stoic idea of [[universalism]]
- Stoic [[universalism]] provided a convenient pretext to justify imperial control from the center
	- incorporated within [[Christianity]]
- [[Stoicism]] legitimized the actions of imperial Rome
	- [[Pax Romana]] (Roman peace) was established by conquest with the violence and brutality of war
	- subjugated people were incorporated into the empire and given autonomy – key to maintaining the empire
- Rome promoted unity and [[universalism]] of citizenship
	- Rome moved Stoic universalism from philosophical abstraction to political reality
- Roman Empire in decline
	- Stoic ideas survive in the [[Middle Ages]]
	- rise of the state system in present-day
- Stoic universalism – concept of "political community" shouldn't be restricted to territorial dimensions of city-states ([[Plato]] and [[Aristotle]]), the modern bureaucratic state, or empire
	- Stoics substituted [[universalism]] doctrine that transcended territorial divisions, human diversity, and time
		- laws assumed to be perpetually valid
- int'l law
	- [[Grotius]] and others drew from Stoicism
	- [[The English School]] image focuses on int'l society in which states serve their interests but also follow agreed rules
		- global society driven by universally accepted norms
- Stoic tradition emphasizes individual level of analysis
	- forgotten by [[Realism]] that focuses on states and elite decision makers
	- forgotten by [[Economic Structuralism]] concerned with sweeping economic and social forces
	- linking natural laws to the individual
		- future generations and political activists argue for existence of universal human rights that no state should be able to intervene
- Stoicism's emphasis on virtue, humanity, and natural law
	- easy fit with [[Christianity]] – combines divine (and revealed) law with the laws of nature
	- [[Islam]] is open to people of all races and ethnicities, continuing Stoic universality from [[Africa]] to [[Middle East]], to South and East [[Asia]] to western [[China]], [[Malaysia]], [[Indonesia]], [[Brunei]], [[Philippines]]
