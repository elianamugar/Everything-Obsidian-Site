- decline and fall of Roman Empire
	- decentralization of authority
	- leads to [[pluralism]] in western Europe
- travel
	- elite privilege
	- religious pilgrimages
	- crusades brought people to the Holy Land

	- marginal activity in relation to localized concentration of economic activity
		- characteristic of [[feudalism]]

	- frequent
	- subject to the restraints of the developing [[just war doctrine]]

	- sense of unity
		- central idea to understand [[Augustine]], [[Thomas Aquinas]]

#### 3.2.1 Historical Context
- unity of Roman Empire
	- Roman law, Roman legions, Stoic philosophy – ideology of the empire
- unity reinforced when Roman Empire was Christianized by Emperor [[Constantine]] (272 – 337 CE) ([[Christianity]])
- 5th c. CE: final collapse of Rome
	- next millennium marks the [[Middle Ages]]
	- ends with [[Renaissance]]/[[Protestant]] [[Reformation]] (15th – 16th c.)
- period of interest for IR scholars
	- encompasses period immediately before the onset of the current state system
	- organization of world into territorially based states claiming sovereignty was not an inevitable outcome of the Middle Ages
		- other possibilities existed
	- writers in the period addressed morality of [[war]] and war-fighting –> development of law of war
- unity of humanity during the [[Middle Ages]]
	- [[Christianity]] church a major purveyor
		- 393 CE: [[Christianity]] sanctioned as Rome's official state religion
	- Rome's collapse
		- Christians concerned with how it could provide necessary worldly power to help the church in its crusade to spread Christianity's teachings
- [[Christianity]]'s power
	- church became increasingly wealthy and privileged organization
	- church had much to lose from invasions and general chaos
	- more "barbarians" came under influence of Christianity
		- church in self-defense
			- strengthened organization
			- centralized authority in papacy
	- corrupt and hypocritical behavior of members of church hierarchy
	- Christianity framework of medieval life, private and public
- scholars of [[Christianity]]
	- responsible for justifying desired creation of a unified int'l system dominated by theocratic principles
- universality of church vs. secular realm
	- political power in secular realm was fragmented in different actors claiming legitimacy
- [[Charlemagne]]'s (742 – 814) early [[Holy Roman Empire]] (modern-day [[Germany]])
	- [[Voltaire]]: not very holy, Roman, or much of an empire #quote 
	- successors provided limited secular counterweight to growing power of church
	- Christian doctrine initially allowed two separate but equal **papal** and **imperial** powers
- 814 CE: death of Charlemagne
	- turmoil and breakdown of unity of empire
	- smaller political units due to internal weaknesses
	- invasions by [[Saracens]] ([[Arab]]s/[[Turkey]]), [[Magyars]] ([[Hungary]]), Norsemen ([[Scandinavia]])
- Kingdoms
	- in existence
	- lacked efficient bureaucracies and permanent military forces
	- kings had little power over local barons
	- contradiction between pluralism and diversity of medieval institutions, religious/philosophical desire for greater unity
- [[feudalism]]
	- 10th c. prominent form of authority
	- political, social, economic response to disorder resulting from collapse of Roman Empire
	- public authority placed in private hands
	- barons enjoyed legitimacy from ownership of property and security they provided their domains
- chaos of late 9th c. [[Europe]]
	- stability provided by Roman law and legions were fading
	- public authority treated as private possession of local lords who controlled territory ([[fiefdom]])
		- authority devolved to local lords by weak and distant kings
	- justice courts administered privately by lords
	- [[vassal]]'s loyalty and obligation to a lord was personal
		- no owed to some distant and abstract entity as "the state"
		- oaths of fealty/allegiance in exchange for protection of a lord were personal contracts
	- authority with ruler who had the right to administer inhabitants of his [[fiefdom]]
- vassal's ownership of property within a fiefdom
	- conditional on owner accepting explicit obligations
	- lord promised to provide vassal protection from hostile world
	- privatization of public authority to local nobles was cause and consequence of predominance of local gov't over claims of kings
		- general fragmentation of political authority throughout [[Europe]] ([[feudalism]])
- political authority during [[feudalism]]
	- claimed by institutions and individuals (local barons, bishops, kings, popes)
	- middle-class merchants ([[bourgeoisie]]) became a political force
		- lent support to religious/secular leaders in return for charters allowing them to establish free communes/commercial leagues
		- could be granted/denied right of embassy (conduct diplomatic relations)
- medieval system
	- "patchwork of overlapping and incomplete rights of government"
	- "different juridical instances were geographically interwoven and stratified, and plural allegiances..."
- slow development of strong, centralized governments
	- poor communication and sparse populations
	- 3 sets of social, economic, and political institutions caused the slowing of development
		- if feudal institutions were strong and authority fragmented/decentralized, it was difficult for modern state to develop
		- feudalism worked to restrict development of state "from below" and universal claims of authority by the church had similar effect "from above"
		- [[Holy Roman Empire]] claims 
			- impact similar to the church through sweeping claims of authority over Europe in secular realm
	- little political space during [[Middle Ages]] to develop modern state
- forms of political authority in latter half of [[Middle Ages]] considered an int'l political system
	- pluralist [[Liberalism]] image of multiple non-state actors playing substantial roles
- what the modern state has, far from feudalism
	- distinction between internal and external political realms
	- rigid territorial demarcations
	- centralized bureaucratic structure (the state) claiming to exercise public authority
		- claim to act independently in the world
- religious/moral philosophy viewed universe as a whole and humanity as organic, interdependent society
- diplomacy in the [[Middle Ages]]
	- papacy adopted Roman principles and established new ones that have become part of int'l law
		- safe conduct of ambassadors
		- secrecy in diplomatic negotiations
		- condemnation of treaty violations
	- personal relationships a key to diplomacy
		- marriages
- territorial borders fluid
	- relations between kingdoms based on dynastic connections
	- no "national interest"
		- interest of particular rulers/dynasties
- high [[Middle Ages]]
	- cosmopolitan era for elites
	- political courtships and marriages 
		- can result in prince of Hungary becoming heir to throne in Naples
		- can result in English prince legitimately claiming Spanish throne in castile
	- web of dynastic interdependences
		- royal mobility
	- rising merchant classes
		- interest in commerce made more cosmopolitan view of world
- "feudal anarchy" of Europe
	- philosophical conception of Europe as organic entity
	- "system of rule"
		- "Europeans lived in a society whose organization may seem excessively simple compared with ours, but it was organized. They shared certain mores, certain patterns of accepted behavior, certain ideals, and a store of acquired techniques."
- 12th c.: reconcentration of political power to kings because of changes in [[feudalism]]
	- three trends in [[feudalism]]:
		- monetarization
			- feudal relationship changed from personal service to monetary payment
			- vassals desired more time to develop fiefs to pass to heirs
				- became less interested in spending time in personal service for the lord
			- lords eventually accepted monetary payment from those running fiefs
			- benefited rulers of provinces and kingdoms more than local lords
				- greater lords attempted to monopolize payments from fiefs or take form local lord (most common)
			- increase in revenues strengthened kingdoms and increased centralized gov'ts
		- systematization
			- feudalism started to have distinct lines of authority
			- kings made great strides pulling pieces of kingdoms
				- reinforced claims of control
			- established precedent for king's legal superiority
				- France: king explicitly demanded reaffirmation of homage from lords of the realm
			- England: development of "franchise theory of justice"
				- when a king granted a lord right to dispense justice, the king reserved the right to intervene in the judicial process and define the original terms of the grant
					- basis of appeals from local courts
				- links weakened between local lords and vassals
					- vassals looked to higher authorities for military and legal protection
		- bureaucratization
			- development of judicial courts
			- establishment of administrative and financial offices
			- educated people needed to oversee expanding responsibilities and authorities of lords and kings
			- clerks ran centralized administration
			- enforcement powers granted to laymen who acted as local representatives of the king, staffed courts, collected revenues
				- introduced [[Europe]] a new source of income independent from ownership of a fief
			- officials had to remain loyal to king or else they're removed from their positions of authority and livelihood
			- critical factor towards development of modern state
- 12th c: gradual process of concentrating power helped by larger trends
	- no more invasions from Europe's periphery
		- allowed kings and nobles to devote attention and resources to internal affairs
			- increase in size of European population
				- helped revive towns
				- increase in artisan class
					- encouraged greater trade
	- expanded economic activity
		- taxation reappeared
			- levied against churches, towns, nobles
			- required establishment of salaried officialdom
	- greater royal income encouraged payment of troops
		- opposed to relying on vassalic contract based on mutual obligation
	- kings began to acquire two key elements for effective rule
		- financial resources
		- coercive power
- 12th and 13th c.: strides in education
	- growth of literacy
		- leads to rise of the state
	- end of 11th c., Europe was a non-literate/oral society
		- education restricted to clergymen
		- writing seen as an art form versus written communication
	- literacy expanded
		- written contracts gained currency
		- ideals, norms of behavior, laws easily passed from generation to generation
		- universities established
			- [[Roger Bacon]] – experimental science
			- [[Dante]] – wrote in language of common person
			- [[Thomas Aquinas]] – inspiration from ancient Greek writers
			- [[Giotto]] – raised art to a higher level
	- rise of educated bureaucrats
		- states formed archives essential to continuity of gov't
- 12th and 13th c.: major classes ensued between sacred and secular realms in 3 areas
	- learning
		- 13th c.: France at center of Western culture and scholarship
			- University of Paris (1200)
				- institution was exempt from civil control
				- members of university were arrogant toward ecclesiastical authority
					- came into conflict with bishops and popes
			- clash between reason and church authority continues through the ages
	- commerce
		- growth of market-oriented economic transaction
			- leads to clash between church's emphasis on religion and emerging capitalist view on economics
				- [[Christianity]] vs. [[Capitalism]]
		- Christian attitude towards commerce
			- those engaged in business should expect only a fair return for their efforts
			- earning interest on loan of money (usury) and taking large profits were "sinful"
		- move away from feudal notion that ownership of property was conditional on explicit social obligations that must be carried out
			- modern, liberal notion that property was private and possession/disposal is up to the individual
	- politics
		- clash between sacred and secular
			- breakdown in [[balance of power]] between pope and emperor
			- leads to breakup of unity of [[Christianity]]
				- helps rise of national states
		- 1076: [[Pope Gregory VII]] and German Emperor [[Henry IV]] in power struggle
			- emperor deposes pope
			- pope excommunicates and deposes emperor
			- emperor unconditionally surrenders to pope and granted pardon
				- high point of religious power over temporal realm
- 13th c.: center of secular power moved from weakened empire to emerging nation-state
	- King [[Philip]] of France had Pope [[Boniface VIII]] arrested
		- Philip levies taxes on clerical income without pope's blessing
		- Boniface responds by forbidding clergy to pay any tax to secular rulers
	- 1302: [[Boniface VIII]] declares papal supremacy
		- Philip's response: form a council to judge the pope on charges (heresy, blasphemy, murder, sodomy, sorcery)
		- Pope moves to excommunicate Philip
	- 1303: Philip arrested by Pope
		- Pope dies of age
	- Frenchman elected pope settles in Avignon (southern [[France]])
	- these incidents were a consequence of struggle between church and empire
		- weakened both church and empire
		- worked to the advantage of the rising nation-state
- 13th c.: four outcomes were possible besides the modern state
	1. political federation/empire with loose centralized control
		- empires were already created in Europe under Romans
		- loosely connected [[Holy Roman Empire]] had a certain sense of unity that lasted a millennium from [[Charlemagne]]'s coronation (800) to abdication of [[Habsburg]] emperor [[Francis II]] (1806)
		- 13th c.: Europe was culturally and economically a more homogenous region than earlier years because of the changes aforementioned
			- better candidate for unification than at any time since Roman Empire
	2. theocratic federation unified by church
		- papacy's claim to universal jurisdiction "was made effective, through a massive international bureaucracy, that was the chief limitation on the rudimentary sovereignty" of smaller and and separate political communities
			- some evolve into modern states
			- dismantled after the [[Council of Constance]] (1414 – 1418)
				- marked development of national churches aligned with emerging national states
		- 10th – 13th c.: conflict between church and empire intense
			- 13th c.: victory of secular over sacred power was not guaranteed
				- religious notables held great deal of political power for centuries to come
			- 19th c.: papal states survive until this period
				- outside Europe, clergy-dominated empires persisted (decentralized)
	3. intensive trading network without centralized political organization
		- developed as a result of spread of market [[Capitalism]]
		- trading cities in [[Germany]] and northern [[Italy]]
			- resisted being swallowed by large territorial states
			- could have lasted much longer and increased in power
		- 12th c.: [[Hanseatic League]] becomes successful commercial federation of trading cities/towns on Baltic and North Atlantic coasts
		- 1397: [[Medici]] family of Florence, [[Italy]] opens first branch bank in [[Rome]] and later in other European cities
	4. feudalism
		- could have persisted much longer
- by 1500: why did "the state" win over these possible alternatives?
#### 3.2.2 Medieval Writers
- 410: Rome is sacked
	- permanent fixture of western European system of rule and source of stability was swept away
- secular support base for [[Christianity]]
	- east: [[Constantine]] (324 – 337) and [[Justinian]] (527 – 565)
	- west: contemplation of world fell to writers associated with religious orders
- early writers desired to hold together Christian community when it was under attack
	- provided moral compass for individuals
	- assured gains achieved by church during later years of Roman Empire weren't lost
- power of church increased over centuries
	- later writers concerned with delineating proper relations between religious and civil powers
- [[Augustine]], [[Thomas Aquinas]], [[Dante]] – [[utopian]]s
	1. they were writing at the time the establishment of a religious or secular-based empire was possible
		- today we view them through a modern-state lens
	2. they were influenced by [[Greek]] tradition of natural law that suggests there's a source of authority beyond temporal powers
		- modern liberal thought, seeking to find basis for unity in diversity
	3. they all resonate with realist thinking
		- sobering view of human nature
		- understood obstacles to world peace
#### 3.2.3 Augustine and Aquinas
- [[Augustine]] (354 – 430)
	- grew up in north African realm of Roman Empire
	- son of pagan father and Christian mother
	- went to Carthage (modern-day [[Tunisia]]) for education
	- interest in philosophy: [[Cicero]]
	- known for [[Confessions by Augustine]] and [[City of God by Augustine]] 
- 383: Augustine goes to Rome to teach rhetoric
- 386: Augustine moves to Milan and converts to [[Christianity]]
- Augustine returns to North Africa
	- founds religious community, ordained a priest
	- selected as bishop of [[Hippo]] (present-day [[Algeria]])
- [[City of God by Augustine]]
	- differentiates between city of man and how humans live in contrast to goodness found in heavenly city of God
	- written to refute [[Cicero]]'s conception of providence (argument favoring free will)
- [[Thomas Aquinas]] (1225 – 1274)
	- born in southern [[Italy]]
	- attended University of Naples
	- joined Dominicans
		- sent to Cologne ([[Germany]]) for further studies
- 1252: Aquinas goes to Paris to begin teaching at Dominican college of [[Jacobin]]s
- 1259: Aquinas appointed theological adviser and lecturer to papal curia in Rome
	- studied [[Aristotle]]'s works
- 1269: Aquinas returns to Paris to defend former colleagues against political and religious attacks
- Aquinas goes to Naples, becomes director of Dominican studies at university
- 1274: Aquinas dies while on his way to attend the second [[Council of Lyons]] in [[France]]
- Augustine and Aquinas's interesting contrast
	- works reflect changing position and power of [[Christianity]] church over the millennium of the [[Middle Ages]]
	- Augustine wrote when the church was in a weak position
		- influenced by [[Plato]] and [[Cicero]]
		- [[Martin Luther]] (1483 – 1546) influenced by [[Augustine]]
			- fashioned theology of his reformation movement ([[Germany]])
		- [[Roman Catholicism]] influenced by [[Augustine]]
		- "protestant saint"
	- 13th c. Aquinas wrote when church was at height of its power and in need of universal and systematic philosophical grounding
		- provides synthesis of theological doctrine from Greeks ([[Aristotle]]) and Romans
		- "Catholic saint"
- Augustine and Aquinas's work, 4 areas of political inquiry:
	1. Aquinas's view of natural law
	2. respective nature and relation between sacred and secular realms
	3. Augustine's [[Realism]]
	4. the [[just war doctrine]]
##### 3.2.3.1 Natural Law
- Augustine and Aquinas
	- believed ancient Greeks were going somewhere with the concept of [[jus naturale]] (natural law) that sets limits on earthly political authority
		- laws made by humans were subordinate to [[jus naturale]]
		- divine law (laws of God) should dominate
- [[Treatise of the Laws by Thomas Aquinas]]
	- "whole community of the universe" is governed by "Divine reason"
		- [[Thomas Aquinas]]: "the very Idea of the government of things in God, the Ruler of the universe, has the nature of law" #quote 
	- [[Thomas Aquinas]]: this law is eternal because "Divine Reason's conception of things is not subject to time" #quote 
		- [[Thomas Aquinas]]: "no one can know the eternal law as it is in itself, except God himself"
- severe limits to human reason that differentiate humans from God
	- a rational individual has a share of the "Eternal Reason", through reasoning, comes to know as [[jus naturale]]
		- applicable to domain of human laws
	- human laws are to be in accord with [[jus naturale]]
- beyond *jus naturale*, there is [[divine law]] (laws given by God)
	- fills gap in human understanding of what one should do/avoid
- what if certain human laws are not in accordance with natural and eternal law?
	- [[Thomas Aquinas]]: laws that are not just are not laws at all
		- [[Thomas Aquinas]]: if a human law "differs from the law of nature, it is no longer a law but a corruption of law" #quote 
- beyond civil law (human law), there is a [[jus gentium]] (law of nations/int'l law) derived from [[jus naturale]]
	- [[Thomas Aquinas]]: [[jus gentium]] exists because it conforms with humanity's nature as social animals who engage in commerce
		- [[Aristotle]] and [[Stoicism]] influences
		- [[Thomas Aquinas]]: "To the law of nations belong those things that are derived from the law of nature as conclusions from premises, just \[or morally right] buyings and sellings, and the like, without which human beings cannot live together, which is a point of the law of nature, since humans are by nature social animals, as is proved in [[Politics by Aristotle]]" #quote 
- [[jus gentium]] derived from general principles of [[jus naturale]] does not deny customary bases
	- on human law
		- [[Thomas Aquinas]]: customs can have the force of law
			- [[Thomas Aquinas]]: "when a thing is done again and again, it seems to proceed from a deliberate judgment of reason" #quote 
		- to have legal effect, customary practice should be in accordance with [[jus naturale]] and [[divine law]] as revealed to humans
##### 3.2.3.2 Sacred and Secular Realms
- hierarchical conception of divine, revealed, natural, int'l, and human law
	- 2 implications for church's preferred organization in [[Middle Ages]]:
		- unity of physical and metaphysical worlds is reinforced by conceiving these realms of parallel and mutually reinforcing hierarchies that reflect revealed and natural laws
			- [[Middle Ages]]: metaphysical reflects same structure as the familiar physical world
			- heavens have hierarchy of saints who can intercede on behalf of the faithful to God via prayer
				- parallels physical world of vassal seeking favor of their lord to intercede for him to a higher authority
			- [[Dante]] adopts this perspective (even though the church condemned his work)
			- [[Augustine]]'s "city of man": world is beset by human imperfection/sin
				- seeds of a perfect world "city of god"
			- city of man and city of god are not distinct
				- individuals representing both are scattered
				- human imperfection stems from error of Adam (perpetuated in "city of man"), commitment to self-oriented, earthly values
		- relation between sacred and secular realms
			- political explosive
			- [[Augustine]] primarily concerned with individual faith and salvation
				- does not view the world as struggle between church and state
				- views struggle between two opposing ways of life: love of self vs. love of God
				- does not call for [[theocracy]]
			- [[Thomas Aquinas]] argues secular kingdoms are subject to the church
				- the church is concerned with the most important end: salvation of souls
##### 3.2.3.3 Augustine's Realism
- human imperfection –> [[Augustine]]'s political [[Realism]]
	- [[Augustine]]: evil is to be found at the individual level of analysis
- classical [[Greek]]s placed emphasis on ability of mind to control human passions, impulses, lusts ("rule of flesh" dominates "rule of spirit")
	- Augustine doesn't necessarily agree, more pessimistic
		- [[Augustine]]: "The corruption of the body, which weighs down the soul, is not the cause of the first sin, but its punishment. And it was not the corruptible flesh that made the soul sinful; it was the sinful soul that made the flesh corruptible." #quote 
		- not simple compared to [[Stoicism]] (an individual can be happy through right conduct)
		- given corrupted nature of humans, Augustine would be more realistic about slim possibility of achieving [[Plato]]'s ideal [[Republic]] than Plato himself
- level of human nature –> state-societal level
	- [[Augustine]]'s realism is evident
		- problems of individuals living together in communities
		- aware of how secular authorities and state power could benefit church and its missions
	- [[City of God by Augustine]] – criticisms of decadence and depravity of Romans in Part I
		- recognizes the empire served as a vehicle for the spread of [[Christianity]]
		- praises [[Constantine]]
			- expects Christian rulers should serve God and extend his worship
		- good Christians make better citizens, benefits the state
- unity of state is an important source of domestic stability and int'l security
	- later influences [[Rousseau]]'s [[general will]]
	- interests of state as [[Republic]] are common to all, given the state amounts to "many people bound together by some bond of accord"
- int'l system ("third level of human society")
	- [[Augustine]] aware of obstacles to a sense of world community
		- [[Augustine]]: "the world, being like a confluence of waters, is obviously more full of danger than the other communities by reason of its greater size" #quote 
	- cooperation is difficult as diversity of languages separates people
	- attempt by Roman Empire to create unity led to civil wars
		- pessimism rooted in [[Augustine]]'s conception of humans whose self-love is the source of evil
		- Augustine's realism is the recognition of power of egotism at the individual and collective level
			- hopes to attain the most achievable form of peace and justice
- Augustine's optimism and [[voluntarism]]
	- holds out hope for individuals being able to enter the "City of God" because human behavior is not permanently fixed/predetermined
	- individuals can make choices
		- role of church to help guide people to City of God
	- City of God is not [[Plato]]'s territorially defined and restrictive city-state, nor [[Cicero]]'s empire, but rather a spiritual bond that aspires to include the entire human race
- differences among people will continue to exist
	- what's more important is for more individuals to enter the City of God
		- doing so will enhance peace on earth
##### 3.2.3.4 Justice and War
- Augustine and Aquinas
	- did not expect war to be abolished anytime soon
	- addressed [[just war theory]]
- Augustine's just war
	- modifies [[Cicero]]'s doctrine, infused with [[Christianity]]
	- accounts criticisms of Roman doctrine by [[Tertullian]] and other pacifist Christian writers
		- they argue against Christian participation in war
		- Augustine disagrees
			- participation in war is acceptable/just under certain circumstances
			- Jesus accepted soldiers as performing a legitimate function
	- Augustine opposes "those who say the doctrine of Christ is incompatible with the State's well-being"
		- it's possible to please God while engaged in active military service
	- [[Augustine]] credits [[Cicero]] for discussing [[just war theory]]
- Augustine's objective of war
	- "A state should engage in war for the safety that preserves the state permanently in existence"
		- wars are waged for legitimate purposes not by individuals, but by legitimate authorities
- Augustine strays from absolute pacifism of early Christians
	- believes in doctrine of war avoidance and restraint in war
	- war is not the final resort, but even the good may need to engage in war
- war is waged so peace may be obtained
	- means of war are important if wars should be just
	- use of force in just wars are restrained
		- after conquering nation(s), provision should be easily made for enjoying peace in mutual bond of piety and justice
- effect of Augustine's just war
	- doctrinal basis for church in its struggle to contain/end violent feuds that were pervasive in the centuries after the collapse of the Roman Empire
- Aquinas's [[just war doctrine]]
	- precise, ordered form of logical exposition (scholastic method)
		- takes a proposition and reaches his own conclusion
		- considers set of objections that can be raised against proposition
		- considers set of replies to those objections
- [[Summa Theologica by Thomas Aquinas]]
	- rejects proposition that it's always sinful to wage war
	- consistent with [[Augustine]], specifies 3 conditions for war to be just ([[Thomist doctrine]]):
		1. *authority of the sovereign*
			- war must be initiated, declared, managed by a legitimate public authority, not private individuals
			- responsibility for common good (public good) of city/kingdom/province
		2. a *just cause* is required
			- those who are attacked should be attacked because they deserve it on account of some fault
			- critical prerequisite for going to war
		3. belligerents should have a *right intention*
			- they intend the advancement of good or avoidance of evil
	- Aquinas: 3 prerequisites are norms of moral theology
		- issue of just war falls within jurisdiction of the church
- [[Thomas Aquinas]]'s views are not too different from [[Augustine]] or [[Cicero]]
	- elaboration of just war within jurisdiction of the church became basis for [[Christianity]]'s doctrine on war
- writers argue for existence of universal moral community based on [[Christianity]]'s brotherhood among individuals
	- modern territorial state ("national interest") did not exist
	- [[Augustine]] concerned with bloody and destructive feuds between individual princes
	- 16th c.: [[Francisco de Vitoria]] (Spanish Dominican, 1480 – 1546) breaks with the idea of universal empire
		- views world in terms of independent states
		- interprets [[just war doctrine]] as part of [[jus gentium]]
			- concerns "what natural reason has established among all *nations*" opposed to among *individuals*
			- thinking followed by [[Francisco Suarez]] (Spanish Jesuit, 1548 – 1617)
#### 3.2.4 Dante
- [[Dante]] Alighieri (1265 – 1321)
	- born in Florence, [[Italy]]
	- [[medieval]] and modern views
	- known for [[The Divine Comedy by Dante Alighieri]] and [[De Monarchia by Dante Alighieri]]
- [[The Divine Comedy by Dante Alighieri]]
	- reflects clearest expressions of [[medieval]] worldview
	- essential unity of physical and metaphysical worlds (concentric circles), all parts of a larger whole
	- differentiates between temporal and spiritual realms
	- sees heaven, earth, and hell as interconnected places
		- passageways between these places, unified worldview
- Dante's works
	- expresses several ideas later associated with 15th c. [[Renaissance]] view of politics
		- separation of philosophy from theology
		- separation of church from empire
- Augustine and Aquinas
	- philosophy is part of, but not subordinate to, the revealed wisdom of God contained within religious understandings
	- both accept notion of parallel secular and sacred kingdoms
	- both argue for sacred over secular
- Dante's active participation in Florentine politics
	- similar to [[Machiavelli]], 200 years later
	- [[voluntarism]] belief "all that concerns polity is subject to our power"
	- criticized the pope for summoning a foreign army to repress Florence's pro-republican, anti-church party (Dante belonged to)
- 1302: Dante is charged and banished for life
- Dante addresses IR topics more than Augustine or Aquinas
	- [[De Monarchia by Dante Alighieri]]
		- lays out solution to problem of war and the anarchic structure of late [[Middle Ages]] – a world gov't run by a monarch
		- universal peace is most important blessing for humanity
			- to achieve it, world needs to be organized properly
			- levels-of-analysis argument (individual household, village, city, kingdom)
				- each unity must be rightly ordered to achieve on particular end
				- kingdom – ultimate goal is tranquility or peace
					- [[Dante]]: to achieve it, "there must be one king to rule and govern, else not only do they in the kingdom fail to reach the goal, but the kingdom itself lapses into ruin...." #quote 
			- for the well-being of the world, there must be a monarchy or empire
		- Dante's argument for world gov't
			- call for peaceful settlement of disputes
			- needs some power with "wider jurisdiction... and he will be monarch or emperor"
			- quotes [[Aristotle]]: "Things love not to be ill-disposed; but a multiplicity of princedoms is ill; therefore, one prince."
				- ultimate power to keep principalities in line should be in the hands of secular ruler with the church playing no role
			- reflects Dante's admiration of the Roman Empire and the peace it enforced for many years
		- power of monarch to intervene is to be derived directly from God, not via religious intermediary like the pope
			- early form of [[Divine Right of Kings]]
			- "The authority of the Empire by no means depends on the Church."
			- revolutionary idea at the time that calls for strict separation of church from world state
				- refusal to subordinate secular political life to religion
		- church and universal empire are coordinate powers, each autonomous and supreme in their respective realms
			- empire guided by reason and philosophy
			- church guided by faith and theology
			- empire is not subordinate to church
			- philosophy is not subordinate to theology
			- all non-medieval views
- [[De Monarchia by Dante Alighieri]] placed on church's banned books list, not removed until 20th c.
#### 3.2.5 Reflections on Thought in the Middle Ages
- works not viewing politics through [[Realism]] lens are labeled [[Idealism]], SHOULD BE AVOIDED
	- mistake: comparing [[Stoicism]] to works of [[Augustine]] and [[Thomas Aquinas]]
- while [[Stoicism]] emphasis on community of humanity parallels Augustine's City of God, there are basic differences
	- [[Stoicism]] is a primarily individualistic philosophy
		- emphasizes ability to accept calmly adversity
		- belief in universal brotherhood
			- does not tell practical steps to transform society to create a wider sense of humanity
		- philosophy of acceptance of the world, not transformation of it
	- Augustine and Aquinas – acceptance of status quo is wrong
		- Augustine refutes complete free will, strikes a balance between God's foreknowledge of one's acts and an individual's free will
		- two religious scholars realize the road to the City of God will not be easy
			- results in pessimistic view of human nature
			- not easy for individuals to triumph over self-love and corruption of the soul
		- like [[Kant]] (nature of non-republican forms of gov't), both understand difficult of achieving a peaceful world community given the nature of humans)
		- considered part of [[Liberalism]] – they recognize non-state forms of int'l authority that transcend borders (the church) and universal norms derived from [[divine law]]
- similarities of [[Dante]], [[Augustine]], [[Thomas Aquinas]]
	- desire for universal community and end to anarchy of [[Middle Ages]]
		- Augustine and Aquinas: universal community in terms of faith and church
		- Dante: political triumph of secular over sacred in temporal matters
			- [[Marsiglio]] (1275 – 1343) and [[William of Ockham]] (1290 – 1348) extend his ideas of separate religious and temporal realms
				- call for a much stricter separation of state from ecclesiastical authority
- [[Renaissance]]
	- widespread reemergence of Greek classical thought
		- supported philosophy over theology for how to interpret and bring order to the world
- secularization of political thought –> modern state
