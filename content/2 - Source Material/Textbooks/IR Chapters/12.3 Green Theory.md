#### 12.3.0 Green Theory
- green theory's diverse approaches
	- social science
		- derive empirically testable hypotheses
		- [[Positivism]], [[rationalism]]
		- diffusion of innovative environmental policy among states and role of domestic actors
	- most green literature is more categorized as interpretive understandings
		- draws on [[critical theory]] and [[constructivism]]
		- [[normative]]
		- seeks to provide policy guidance
	- radical change in mindset is required to deal with the environment broadly conceived
		- anthropogenic climate change has an impact on human societies and plant and animal kingdoms and the physical world 
			- more than traditional theological view that humanity has to take seriously its stewardship of Nature as God's creation
			- requires humans to go behind traditional human/nature dichotomy, focus on symbiosis between the two
		- radical green theorists would suggest adoptin gan ecocentrist perspective, prioritizing environment
	- how do we conceptualize IR? green theory's perspective on state sovereignty
		- concept of [[anarchy]] system should be viewed within broader context of Earth's biosphere
			- maybe even higher level of analysis encompassing states and [[The English School]]'s focus on [[world society]]
		- impact of climate change on natural world requires critical examination of constructed norm of state [[sovereignty]]
			- can states continue to claim the sovereign right to pollute within their borders when the effects transcend borders?
			- what does sovereignty mean in a world were small islands (e.g. Maldives) are threatened with physical extinction?
			- how might shifting shorelines impact agreements under [[United Nations]] [[Law of the Sea]] concerning territorial waters and maritime claims?
				- if coastlines change, won't maritime boundaries have to change as well?
				- what would this mean for int'l law?
	- green theory scholars embrace idea of expanded notion of political community that transcends state borders
		- consequence 1: question relentless production and consumption of goods that meet immediate needs and desires, but is at the cost of the environment and long-term ecological sustainability required for life
			- continuing material consumption and [[exploitation]] of earth's resources gives rise to questions concerning [[Politics]], [[Economics]], and broader communitarian/global perspective moral/[[normative]] questions
				- shouldn't we consider placing greater limits on individual choice due to long-term concerns for the global environment and humanity?
					- at odds with prevailing [[Liberalism]] assumption that emphasizes the autonomy of the individual
				- what about global environmental justice?
					- normative concerns for those individuals (in the [[Third World]]), whose voices are ignored in corridors of power (local, nat'l, int'l)
