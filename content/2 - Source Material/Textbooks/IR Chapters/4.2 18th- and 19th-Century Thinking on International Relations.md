#### 4.2.0 18th- and 19th-Century Thinking on [[IR]]
- 1648: [[Peace of Westphalia]] – formal emergence of sovereign state and system of sovereign states
	- 15th – 17th c.: Greco-Roman and medieval thought
	- 18th – 20th c.: building upon the intellectual foundation of 15th – 17th c. writing
- 17th – 18th c. writers focused on domestic aspects of the state, not looking beyond borders
	- except [[Grotius]]
- state-societal and int'l levels of analysis becomes more of a focus
	- what degree structure/lack of it in the int'l environment affects the nature and behavior of states?
		- addressed by [[Montesquieu]], [[Rousseau]], [[Kant]]
	- is it the nature of the domestic regime/society that matters more?
		- addressed [[Economics]] by [[Adam Smith]], [[Marx]], 19th c. [[Liberalism]]
	- their works are influenced by the basic assumptions and principles of 17th – 18th c. [[Enlightenment]]
		- "Age of Reason" embraced secular thinking independent of theological understandings

#### 4.2.1 Historical Context
- after 1648: European state system marked by rivalries and shifting alliances
	- 1649: King [[Charles I]]'s execution on charge of treason ([[English Civil War]])
	- 1653–1658: "Lord Protector" [[authoritarian]] [[Oliver Cromwell]] rises to power
	- 1660: return to parliamentary consent of [[Stuart]] dynasty under [[Charles II]] ([[Stuart Restoration]])
	- 1685: [[Roman Catholicism]] [[James II]] accedes the throne
	- 1688: parliament invites [[Protestant]] [[William of Orange]] (Holland) and [[Mary Stuart]] (daughter of James II) to take the throne, deposing James II ([[Glorious Revolution]])
	- 1683: Ottoman invaders ([[Turkey]] challenge) defeated by [[Austria]]n power outside Vienna
	- British military and House of [[Savoy]] in northern [[Italy]] block French ambitions in a series of battles
	- 1713: [[Treaty of Utrecht]] curbs [[France]]'s power for 75 years
	- 1789: [[French Revolution]]
- [[French Revolution]]
	- competing ideas and emerging ideologies break through in this era
	- contrasting democratic ideas (*liberté*, *egalité*, and *fraternité*) vs. authoritarianism
		- [[authoritarian]] models find many emulators in next 2 centuries
		- [[Jacobin]], [[Napoleon]]
- [[Napoleonic period]]
	- upset [[Peace of Westphalia]]
	- replaces [[Peace of Westphalia]] with the [[First French Empire]]
		- spread of French language and culture, French-style central administration of state affairs, idea of universal, national military service by citizen-soldiers (*levée en masse*)
	- 1815: after [[Napoleon]]'s defeat, restoration to "Westphalia system" of sovereign, independent states at [[Congress of Vienna]]
- [[Congress of Vienna]]
	- attention to restore balance among great powers
	- attention to provide a conflict-management system ([[Concert of Europe]])
	- [[France]] restored as major power
	- smaller wars fought, but [[Europe]] remained free of general [[War]] until 1914
- [[Concert of Europe]]
	- periodic conferences of "concert" approach to managing conflict within [[Europe]]an balance was successful
	- concert approach broke down in the latter half of 19th c.
		- [[Crimean War]] (1853–1856): [[UK]] joins [[France]] and [[Turkey]] in dispute against [[Russia]]
			- curbs [[Russia]]'s role in south[[Eastern Europe]]
		- [[Franco-Prussian War]] (1870–1871): France is defeated by Prussia
		- unification of [[Germany]]
	- marked beginnings of great power rivalry
		- development reached conclusion with outbreak of [[WWI]] in 1914
- contemporary IR theory and practice are drawn primarily from this European experience
	- 18th–19th c.: expansion of European system of sovereign states and European notions of [[Capitalism]]'s market economy to include entire globe
- [[Colonialism]]
	- began in late 15th c.
	- reaches global scope in 18th–19th c.
	- some European states established colonies in [[Africa]] early
		- rest of [[Africa]] and [[Asia]] not colonized until the imperial era of 19th c.
	- most [[America]]n states established independence from [[Europe]] in early 19th c.
	- large-scale decolonization in [[Africa]] and [[Asia]] didn't occur until 20th c. in aftermath of [[WWI]] and [[WWII]]
- all states came into existence in accordance with a European pattern formally established in [[Peace of Westphalia]]
- ideas originating in [[Europe]] came to define IR for the whole world
- Europeans and Americans who were a product of the European ideas directly influenced approaches to world politics in present-day IR theory

#### 4.2.2 The Enlightenment
- writers during [[Enlightenment]] were all influenced by basic assumptions and principles of 17th–18th c. [[Enlightenment]]
	- interests, analyses, prescription varied
- 18th c.: [[France]]'s [[rationalism]]
	- 1751: rationalists publish *Encyclopédie*
		- [[Diderot]], [[d'Alembert]], [[Helvetius]], [[d'Holbach]], [[Marquis de Condorcet]] influenced by scientific method of previous century
		- used mathematical reasoning to establish truth
			- method independent of God's revelation
	- French rationalists contributed to development of systematic philosophy
		- **classical[[ Greek]] assumption: individuals are rational, reasoning beings**
	- [[Descartes]] (1596–1650): mastery and possession of nature are possible
		- humanity can understand, control, and utilize natural forces for its own benefit, comfort, and safety
	- rationalists argue: through applied reason, it's possible to overcome superstition, prejudice, and tradition to understand the laws that govern nature and **human society**
		- these laws (especially of reason) were universal and applicable to all humanity
			- important to acquire knowledge and development science of politics (similar to natural sciences) –> social sciences + IR
	- [[Enlightenment]] concerned with issue of required appropriate methods to understand human behavior
- [[Enlightenment]]'s realm of *analysis* to realm of *action*
	- rational individuals can exercise reason to create an environment reflection the revealed laws of nature
		- example: through reason, nature of justice can be recognized and a just society can be created
	- [[Enlightenment]] thinkers had great faith in the progress of humanity, perfectibility of humanity was possible
		- result: political argument followed the Stoics
			- **all people are linked by nature in universal brotherhood**
		- some writers followed on that point and argue: the increasingly powerful sovereign state divided humanity
			- called for world citizenship
			- [[Voltaire]], [[Samuel Johnson]], [[Goethe]]
- political implications in [[France]]
	- revolutionary
	- rejected idea of original sin and innate aggressiveness
	- writers argue: the failings attributed to human nature are a result of a corrupt environment (not inborn)
		- if environment causes humanity to be less than reason and nature intended, then a program of societal restructuring becomes a moral imperative
	- [[French Revolution]]: extreme form of [[voluntarism]] emerges
		- duties of revolutionaries
			- liberate the people of France from *ancien régime* and weight of the past
			- engage in wars of liberation abroad
		- destroyed [[monarchy]]
		- broke the idea in the perfectibility of humanity
- [[David Hume]] (1711–1776) and his skepticism
	- critique of human reason
	- claim: morality should be in accordance with passions
		- contributes to disintegration of [[voluntarism]]
#### 4.2.3 Montesquieu
- [[Montesquieu]] (1689 –1855)
	- born to an aristocratic French family
	- studied law at University of Bordeaux
	- moved to Paris, then returns home after death of father
		- further studied Roman law
	- publishes [[The Persian Letters (1721) by Montesquieu]]
		- [[Satire]] analysis of contemporary [[France]]
	- foreign travel (especially to [[England]]) furthered his interest in politics and constitutions
- a student of [[Ancient World]]'s [[Greek]] and [[Roman]] writings
	- [[Montesquieu]] familiar with mixed constitutions
		- [[The Laws by Plato]]
		- [[Politics by Aristotle]]
	- like [[Machiavelli]]
		- emphasis on statecraft
		- rejects view of Greek classics in which the primary function of political theorizing was to encourage virtue
		- necessity and choice in formation of laws
			- diverges from [[Enlightenment]]'s supreme confidence in [[voluntarism]] and ability to transform societies
- [[Spirit of the Laws (1748) by Montesquieu]]
	- describes how historical, geographical, and climatic circumstances create diverse human cultures and "general spirit" of each nation
		- all nations live more by passion and prejudice, and less by reason
	- diversity is only intelligible in light of general causes
	- acceptance of diversity reflects his belief in limits to purposive human action ([[voluntarism]])
- how [[Montesquieu]] reflects [[Enlightenment]] thinking
	- defines laws as "necessary (universal) relations arising out of the nature of things"
	- follows [[Stoicism]]
		- claim: these laws govern the actions of all things and provide the standard by which human law is to be judged
	- contrasts [[Thomas Aquinas]]: nature is the ultimate source of law, not God
- [[Montesquieu]]'s view of natural law
	- expresses hope of establishing a science of human affairs
- [[Polybius]]'s influence on [[Montesquieu]]
	- advocacy of separation of powers
		- balances governmental powers among competing departments/branches
		- idea adopted by [[America]]n federalists
			- [[Hamilton]], [[Madison]], [[John Jay]]
			- want to avoid too strong of a central gov't
		- believes this most effectively promotes liberty
			- liberty is top priority, also believes in stable polity as a worthwhile goal
- [[Montesquieu]] prefers a [[Republic]]
	- aligned more with [[Kant]] than [[Machiavelli]]
		- argues republics can defend themselves AND best at [[expansionism]]
		- "peace and moderation are the spirit of the republic", rejecting Machiavelli's "great strokes of authority" (cruelty and tyranny) to maintain power
			- Montesquieu: focus on liberty and security of the individual
			- Machiavelli: focuses on power and glory of the sovereign/state
	- all 3 have in common: domestic character of a state and its society DOES influence its behavior int'lly
- [[Montesquieu]]'s influences and who he influenced
	- influenced [[Rousseau]], who influenced [[Kant]]
	- all 3 reject [[Hobbes]]'s and [[Machiavelli]]'s negative view of nature of man
- [[Montesquieu]]'s critique of [[Hobbes]]
	- in both [[The Persian Letters (1721) by Montesquieu]] and [[Spirit of the Laws (1748) by Montesquieu]]
		- man's nature isn't defective
		- humans entering civil society from their natural state is the issue
			- formation of separate nations and relations among them add to the problem
- [[Montesquieu]]'s levels of analysis: individual, society, and int'l system
- [[Rousseau]]'s argument on formation of society departing from state of nature builds on [[Montesquieu]]'s
	- state of nature: state of war occurs AFTER the formation of society, not before (as [[Hobbes]] would claim)
	- war is an artifact of society, not characteristic of human nature
	- how to overcome societal strife?
		- [[Rousseau]]'s idea of "[[general will]]"
		- [[Montesquieu]]: "the particular force of individuals cannot be united without a conjunction of all their wills" = "the civil state" #quote 
	- emphasis on [[Enlightenment]] concern with education by which individuals are prepared for civil life
- [[Montesquieu]] on [[divine law]] and [[jus naturale]]
	- recognizes them
	- focuses on different categories/"orders of laws"
		- applicable to moral, religious, domestic, int'l domains of humans and their societies
	- refers to [[jus gentium]] as "civil law of the universe" where "every nation is a citizen"
		- states are like people and are understood to be unitary actors
		- [[Rousseau]]'s [[general will]]: consistent with unitary notion of state as singular actor representing individuals comprising it
			- [[Realism]]: foreign policies of states are unitary and formulated in service of national interest
- [[Montesquieu]] on the state of nations
	- "each particular society beings to feel its strength"
		- result: there "arises a state of war between different nations"
	- how to regulate their conduct
		- [[jus gentium]]: different nations, in peacetime, "do one another all the good they can" and in wartime do "as little harm as possible, without prejudicing their real interests"
		- central interest for all nations: preservation
			- "safety of the people is the supreme law"
	- states have a right to go to war in their own defense
		- "when one nation sees that a longer peace will enable another to destroy it, and that to attack that nation instantly is the only way to prevent its own destruction"
			- personal note: sounds like current [[Israel]]'s justification for attacking nearby countries
	- states have a right to conquest
		- rests upon same preservationist principle
#### 4.2.4 Rousseau
- Jean-Jacques [[Rousseau]] (1712 – 1778)
	- reflects [[Montesquieu]], [[Locke]], [[Plato]]
	- born in [[Switzerland]]
	- associated with [[Diderot]] (editor of *Encyclopédie*)
	- *Emile* and *On the Social Contract* offended authorities, fled to [[France]]
	- ended up in [[England]] on behalf of [[David Hume]], they argued
	- Rousseau returns to [[France]]
- influence of [[Enlightenment]] thinking on [[Rousseau]]
	- claim: individuals escape the state of nature by a mutually beneficial agreement ([[social contract theory]])
	- creation of society is an act of volition/will
	- state of nature: an egocentric world in which "man's first sentiment was that of his own existence; his first concern was that of his preservation"
		- in this primitive state, individuals may collaborate (as required)
	- concerned with the near-future, not the distant future
	- [[Rousseau]]: "Were it a matter of catching a deer, everyone was quite aware that he must faithfully keep to his post in order to achieve this purpose; but if a hare happened to pass within reach of one of them, no doubt he would have pursued it without giving it a second thought, and that, having obtained his prey, he cared very little about causing his companions to miss theirs." #quote 
		- are states like primitive hunters, serving their individual, short-run self-interests?
		- do states recognize advantages of fulfilling mutual commitments?
		- will states be willing to forgo short-term gratification for themselves and act for common interests that serve longer-term advantages for all? (an enlightened self-interest?)
- conflict and lack of cooperation among states in human/states/societies nature
	- [[Rousseau]]: emphasis on how decentralized structure of a system contributes to suspicion, distrust, conflict
	- difficulty of cooperation among egocentric actors stems from [[self-help system]]'s nature similar to nature of the actors themselves
- [[Rousseau]]: structure and values of social setting are influenced by the behavior of individuals/states within it
	- like [[Montesquieu]]: emerging society leads to state of war since individuals are leaving a happier state of nature
	- property and divisions that stem from resulting inequality are the root of human conflict (individuals/states)
	- [[anarchy]] of state system, responsible for national wars, battles, murders, reprisals
- Rousseau's state-societal level of analysis
	- governance should be in accordance with the [[general will]]
		- political community is single person/unit
	- [[Realism]] view: state as a unitary, rational actor
	- it is only the [[general will]] that can "direct forces of the state according to the purpose for which it was instituted, which is the common good" #quote 
- [[sovereignty]] can't be divided into parts
- [[Rousseau]] departs from [[Montesquieu]] on states' right to conquest
	- advocates for *[[jus in bello]]*: "War does not grant a right that is unnecessary to its purpose" #quote 
	- war can happen if everyone bears arms, but once surrender exists, war ceases and there is no more enemy
- [[Rousseau]] influence of his [[general will]] concept
	- democratic thought
	- some see it as a justification for [[authoritarian]] rule
		- a more effective way to serve the common or societal interest
	- influenced [[Marx]] and [[Engels]]'s idea on property and its relation to inequality
		- [[Rousseau]]: "man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains" #quote 
		- [[The Communist Manifesto (1848) by Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels]]
			- by committing themselves to revolution, the working men have "nothing to lose but their chains"
- [[Rousseau]]'s contribution to [[nationalism]]
	- important role of political indoctrination in creating a national identity
- [[Realism]]'s remarks
	- [[Kenneth Waltz]], [[Stanley Hoffman]]
	- agree on international milieu: absence of any common superior over states is seen as the "permissive" cause of [[War]]
		- [[Kenneth Waltz]]: "[[Rousseau]]'s answer is really that war occurs because there's nothing to prevent it. Among states as among men there is no automatic adjustment of interests. In the absence of a supreme authority, there is the constant possibility that conflicts will be settled by force." #quote 
		- [[Stanley Hoffman]]: solution of war and peace, to [[Rousseau]], is not at the int'l level, but rather at the state level – "Establish ideal states all over the world, and peace will follow." #quote 
	- disagree on degree to which [[Rousseau]] emphasizes federation of states as solution to security problem posed by int'l [[anarchy]] of sovereign, independent states
		- [[Kenneth Waltz]]: federation is [[Rousseau]]'s prescription to remedy the problem of int'l [[anarchy]] if it were desirable to alter that condition
		- [[Stanley Hoffman]]: [[Rousseau]] puts emphasis on improving states and their citizens rather than making changes in the structure of int'l environment
		- [[Stanley Hoffman]]: Rousseau's preference for smaller communities
			- more conducive to effective realization of [[general will]] than would be possible in a large federation
			- Hoffman mainly credits [[Kant]] for this
- [[Rousseau]]'s critique of [[Perpetual Peace by Abbé de St. Pierre]]
	- [[St. Pierre]] proposes a confederacy should establish peace
		- Rousseau isn't hostile to the logic, but rather the impracticality of the proposition
		- Rousseau is concerned with avoiding universal monarchy or too strong a central authority, but that's not his central rebuttal
		- main problem to Rousseau: the [[utopian]] character of [[St. Pierre]]'s vision
			- not so much the failure to eliminate war
				- if it fails to eliminate war, it's also not a main problem that threatens the general will of smaller communities/states within federation
#### 4.2.5 Kant
- Immanuel [[Kant]] (1724 – 1804)
	- born in [[Germany]]
	- attended University of Königsberg, became lecturer on physics, logics, natural sciences
	- 1781: publishes [[Critique of Pure Reason (1781) by Immanuel Kant]]
	- shares [[Rousseau]]'s interest in problem of [[War]] and peace in IR
	- an optimist: believed in the ultimate perfection of individuals and their countries
		- slow but steady
		- humanity is not condemned to an endless repetition of wars and civil strife
	- supported [[American Revolution]] ([[America]]) against [[England]]
		- belief in validity of values of [[French Revolution]]
	- published [[Idea for a Universal History (1784) by Immanuel Kant]] and [[Perpetual Peace (1795) by Immanuel Kant]] under King [[Frederick William III]] of [[Prussia]]
	- influenced by [[David Hume]] and [[Rousseau]]
	- [[Stoicism]] roots
		- [[universalism]], world citizenship, advocacy of federation of states leads to peace
	- vision of a liberal world order where humans can live freely without war
		- achievement of this vision would let individuals fulfill their duty to fellow citizens and state and to humanity as a whole
	- believer in [[Idealism]], but not naive
		- rejects that transformation of world politics is imminent or easy to achieve
		- sovereign state is a reality
			- dealing with int'l [[anarchy]] must take states into account
		- creating a federation of states with a combined military force isn't realistic
			- would put states out of business
				- gov't leaders wouldn't allow this
- [[Kant]] parts from [[Grotius]], [[Samuel Pufendorf]], [[Vattel]]
	- believes none of them pay "the Stoic-Christian ideal of the unity of mankind the supreme compliment of taking its political consequences seriously" ([[Stoicism]], [[Christianity]])
	- comes up with proposal that balances aspiration and practicality
- Kant's "league of nations"
	- federation of states with its sole purpose being maintaining peace
	- compatible with freedom of several states
	- leaves separate sovereignties of league members intact
	- preferable idea of federative union, does not want "universal monarchy" (states under one superior power)
- Kant's view on human nature
	- like [[Rousseau]]: believes human state of nature exist in a condition of the [[self-help system]]
	- agrees with [[Hobbes]] (differing from [[Rousseau]]): natural state is state of war
		- peace must be established as a result of human action
	- [[Kant]]: "peoples, as states, like individuals, may be judged to injure one another merely by their coexistence in the state of nature" #quote 
		- negative effect of [[anarchy]] furthered
	- adversity and discord among humans will lead them to learn how to avoid future wars
		- part of nature's design: "Nature brings it to pass through selfish inclinations" #quote 
- [[Liberalism]] theorists emphasize [[Economics]]' imperatives
	- aids the process of moving toward peace
	- [[Kant]]: "The spirit of commerce, which is incompatible with war, sooner or later gains the upper hand in every state." #quote 
	- money is a critical source of state power
- Kant against conduct of statesmen creating domestic and international chaos, unapologetically pursuing state interests aggressively
	- Kant has faith that ultimate good in world politics will displace evil through slow progress
- Kant's link between morality and politics
	- [[Kant]]: "True politics can never take a step without rending homage to morality." #quote 
	- [[Kant]]: If "politics says, 'Be ye wise as serpents', then morality adds, as a limiting condition, 'and guileless as doves.'" #quote 
	- continuing tension between power and principle in politics ([[E. H. Carr]] discusses later between [[WWI]] and [[WWII]])
- [[Kant]] on progress of humanity over time
	- practicality must give way to moral principle: "Nature inexorably wills that the right should finally triumph." #quote 
- Kant: in human discord, the necessary element in nature's design by which progress is possible exists
	- [[War]] carries the seeds of later progress toward peace, as humans gradually realize how awful [[War]] is
	- [[Arendt]]: "It is by virtue of this idea of mankind, present in every single man, that men are human, and they can be called civilized or humane to the extent that this idea becomes the principle not only of their judgments but also of their actions." #quote 
- how do we make progress toward peace?
	- like [[Montesquieu]]: Kant prefers constitutional arrangements that check/balance competing interests
	- [[Woodrow Wilson]] ([[America]]) later argues: a federation of republics inclined toward peace and under [[jus gentium]] can be extended gradually to include add'l members
	- harmonization comes from structuring domestic and int'l environments in which we live
	- transformation of human condition won't be achieved in a short time, but it remains "our duty to work toward this end"
- Kant emphasizes:
	- ability of leaders to learn and realize that it's rational and in their individual AND collective self-interest to cooperate
	- belief in possibility of change
	- recognition of need to link aspiration and reality
#### 4.2.6 *The Federalist Papers*
- writers: [[Hamilton]], [[Madison]], [[John Jay]]
	- practical problem: sway [[America]]n public to support idea of replacing current confederation of states with a [[Republic]]
		- entail greater power at national level
	- conversant with works of predecessors
		- reliance on [[Montesquieu]], [[Plato]], [[Polybius]], [[Plutarch]], [[Grotius]]
		- familiarity with earlier works of [[Republicanism]]
	- reasons for interest in IR:
		1. [[Realism]] 
			- was suggested that [[Realism]] introduced into realm of [[America]]n foreign policy by [[Europe]] [[Post-WWII]]
			- [[The Federalist Papers]] proves there was already [[Realism]] perspective when discussing relations among states/factions
		2. [[Madison]]'s discussion on factions
			- influenced work on [[interest group theory]]
				- entered IR literature dealing with bureaucratic politics and domestic sources of foreign policy
	- nature of relations among states if each claimed [[sovereignty]] went their separate way
		- [[John Jay]] and [[Hamilton]]'s argument: result would be similar to relations among *any* state – **conflict**
		- [[John Jay]] (similar to power transition theorists): even if states are initially of equal strength, the condition will soon change
			- various circumstances will work to "increase power in one part and to impede its progress in another" so that "relative equality in strength and consideration would be destroyed" #quote 
		- [[Hamilton]] tries to find harmony between independent, unconnected sovereignties in the same neighborhood
			- presents possible conflict among states ranging in different levels of analysis
				- [[Hamilton]]: "safety from external danger is the most powerful director of national conduct" #quote 
- [[Hamilton]]'s state-societal level of analysis
	- "visionary or designing men" who advocate a "perpetual peace" among states and claim that [[Republic]]s are pacific and that "the spirit of commerce" dampens the possibilities of war
	- [[Hamilton]]: "Have [[Republic]]s in practice been less addicted to war than [[monarchy]]?" #quote 
	- commercial motives contributed to onset of wars
- [[The Federalist Papers]]: #10 by [[Madison]]
	- Madison outlines problems of factions and danger they present to unity of state (traditional republican concern)
		- passions, divergent interests, unequal distribution of property divide mankind
			- difficult to cooperate for common good
		- one virtue of [[Republic]]
			- [[Madison]]: representative government tends to "refine and enlarge the public views by passing them through the medium of chosen body of citizens, whose wisdom may best discern the true interest of their country" #quote 
- [[The Federalist Papers]]: #51 by [[Madison]] and [[Hamilton]]
	- factions can be turned into a virtue to overcome divisions
		- no need for a [[Leviathan]] (they didn't like [[Hobbes]])
	- because in a [[Republic]], "society itself will be broken into so many parts, interests and classes of citizens, that the rights of individuals, or of the minority, will be in little danger."
		- multiplicity, or plurality of interests, keeps permanent majority from forming
			- encourages creation of various coalitions depending on the issue
			- similar to balance-of-power theory predicting that it's natural for states to band together to prevent being dominated by a larger state/coalition
		- contribution to [[Liberalism]] image of domestic and int'l politics
			- emphasizes variety of actors/groups that make up a state and the competition among them
- thesis concerning virtues of [[Republic]]an forms of gov't
	- different from [[Machiavelli]]'s argument: conflict among classes ultimately serves to enhance external power and prowess of the [[Republic]]
	- [[Madison]] and [[Hamilton]]: effects of checks/balances are designed to keep too much power from being concentrated in any one domestic institution/group
		- foreign policy considerations are secondary
- [[The Federalist Papers]]'s central theme
	- security of a federal republic is to be found in unity
	- unification avoids armed conflicts among states in the union
		- provides a defense against threats/attacks by other states
		- same argument in [[The Prince by Niccolo Machiavelli]]: advocates unification among Italian states for domestic security AND defense against outsiders
#### 4.2.7 Hegel
- Georg W. F. [[Hegel]] (1770 – 1831)
	- often overlooked in IR
		- complexity of worldview
		- aversion to [[authoritarian]] orientation in relation to state power
		- later association with [[Marxism]]
			- emphasis on role of ideas in changing history in contrast to [[Marx]]'s [[materialism]]
- [[Hegel]]: the historical process is one in which humanity has continued to make spiritual and moral progress
	- NOT the random occurrence of unconnected events
	- individuals have the ability to reflect upon their circumstances and increase self-knowledge
	- reason and history are inseparable
	- historical process is a **rational, dialectical process** that unfolds the "Ideal of Reason"
		- apparent in historical development of state
	- function of philosophy: deal with the reality of the state in a particular epoch
		- NOT to dream up some ideal state
		- NOT to discern supposedly transhistorical natural law
- [[Hegel]]'s contributions
	- rise in [[historicism]]
		- highly critical of [[Individuality]] and [[universalism]] of [[rationalism]] associated with [[Stoicism]] and [[Enlightenment]]
		- rejects "uniformity of human nature and the supposed timelessness of moral principles"
- [[historicism]]
	- historicists argue moral beliefs and values vary in different cultures as proven throughout history
		- e.g. natural law teachings of [[Stoicism]] reflected a response to the historical dissolution of [[Greek]] city-states
	- no universal, supra-historical, transcendental set of values/natural laws
		- procedures to criticize beliefs and actions differ from different societies
	- reason is relative to culture; therefore, one should not utilize some universal standard of behavior to judge a state
- [[historicism]]'s further criticisms
	- historicists critical of [[social contract theory]] approaches to origins of state
	- historicists critical of emphasis on [[Individuality]]
		- argue values held by an individual are socially produced, not a reflection of universal laws of nature
	- fundamental fact of humanity is cultural diversity, resulting in division of world into states
	- Heinrich von [[Treitschke]]: "The idea of one universal empire is odious – the ideal of a state co-extensive with humanity is no ideal at all." #quote 
	- **a state has an absolute right to sovereignty, disputes among states are inevitable, and moral ties among individuals of various states as well as perpetual peace are illusory**
- [[Hegel]]'s themes in his writings
	- obsession with state
	- acceptance of [[sovereignty]] of individual states
	- acceptance of int'l [[anarchy]] that exists among states
	- [[The Philosophy of History by Hegel]]
		- "The State is the Divine Idea as it exists on Earth," the "embodiment of rational freedom, realizing and recognizing itself in an objective form." #quote 
		- "The nation state is mind in its substantive rationality and immediate actuality and is therefore the absolute power on earth. It follows that every state is sovereign and autonomous against its neighbors." #quote 
	- rejects idea that community of nations should, could, or does exists
		- this was a long tradition of political thought
- [[Hegel]] contrasts [[Rousseau]] and [[Kant]]
	- [[Hegel]] doesn't seek perpetual peace or anything approaching it
		- believes [[War]] has a good effect on societies by keeping them from decline associated with long-term peace
		- history's progression is the result of wars
		- [[War]] is not entirely an accident in [[Anarchic]] system
			- view found in [[Rousseau]] and [[Realism]]
#### 4.2.8 Clausewitz
- [[Carl von Clausewitz]] (1780 – 1831)
	- [[Prussia]]n military officer
	- [[War]] is "a continuation of political activity by other means." #quote 
	- writing took place in interwar period
		- 1815: [[Napoleon]]'s defeat
		- 1830: [[Carl von Clausewitz]] recalled to duty for East [[Prussia]]
	- 1831: [[Carl von Clausewitz]] dies while working on [[On War by Carl von Clausewitz]]
- [[Carl von Clausewitz]] on the military and [[War]]
	- policy makers, not the military, must decide the overall strategic objectives of the state
	- military develops plans for application of force, keeping political objectives in mind
	- [[War]] isn't fought for glory or an end in itself
	- [[War]] is used to enhance the security of the state ([[Realism]])
	- use of force in battle aims to destroy/weaken war-making capabilities of adversaries
		- undermines the will to continue fighting
	- leadership is important
		- commander crucial in rational enterprise
			- must adapt to changing circumstances
			- must employ principles of surprise, mass, and concentration of forces
	- attacks directed at enemy's "center of gravity" can cause enemy's capability to collapse
	- military forces are finite, don't be wasteful in its use
		- economy of force essential to sustaining military capabilities against adversary
- [[Carl von Clausewitz]]'s "[[fog of war]]"
	- uncertainty that attends decision-making in battlefield conditions
		- similar to [[Machiavelli]]'s [[Fortuna]]
		- similar to [[Thucydides]]'s reference to fate as blunting even the best-laid plans of the prince
	- rationally made plans run into obstacles/"friction" when actually implemented
	- warns that one shouldn't take the first step into war without realizing where the last step leads
- [[Carl von Clausewitz]]'s contributions
	- his observations found in present-day strategic literature and [[Realism]]
	- view of military being a political means
	- exposition of societal (social and economic) dimensions of national capabilities
	- focus on national security problems
#### 4.2.9 Weber
- [[Max Weber]] (1864 – 1930)
	- born in [[Germany]]
	- not generally associated with [[IR]], but impact on [[Realism]] and [[Liberalism]]
	- influence in:
		1. sociology of knowledge
		2. his conception of the state
		3. study of bureaucracies
		4. political leadership
- [[Max Weber]] assumes there are regularities in human behavior
	- through deductive and inductive reasoning, we can generate hypotheses and subject them to empirical testing
	- faith in the **scientific method**
		- a potential means to comprehend a complex reality
	- if we can understand human behavior, then as a result, we can understanding the functioning of societies
- [[Max Weber]] attempts to explain the development and functioning of societies
	- rejects mono-causal explanations
	- [[Marx]]'s insight on economic factors balanced with weighing cultural, political, military considerations
	- **understanding requires interpretation**
- [[Max Weber]]'s [[Realism]]
	- belief that modern state represents ultimate form of sovereign political authority
		- modern state "has been successful in seeking to monopolize the legitimate use of physical force" #quote 
	- like [[Hegel]]: a German nationalist ([[Germany]])
	- believes in [[Liberalism]], suspicious of state power interfering with autonomy of the individual
	- emphasis on state due to lifelong study of why empires and decentralized feudal authorities don't exist in modern world
	- observations on IR
		- "all political structures (states) use force," but "the attitude of political structures toward the outside may be more isolationist or more expansive" #quote 
			- case study: [[Switzerland]]
				- more towards [[isolationism]]
				- independence protected by a [[balance of power]] among larger states
		- political leaders of states are driven by power-oriented prestige
			- contrary to [[Marxism]]'s belief of imperialism being influenced by economic factors
				- Weber: economic factors at best codetermine the extent and manner of political expansion
	- political threats to peace often result in war due to desire for prestige
- [[Max Weber]]'s "rationalization"
	- [[Capitalism]] is the embodiment of [[rationalism]] in terms of productive use of resources
		- far from [[Marx]]'s characterization of [[Capitalism]] as "anarchy of production"
	- equates rise of bureaucratic administration (public AND private) with [[rationalism]]
	- rationalization is key component of his view of history
		- sees process interrupted by occasional emergence of charismatic movements that abhor routine
	- exemplified by how modern state manages to monopolize the legitimate use of physical force
		- ancient times, early medieval era, feudalism
			- individuals in armies were self-equipped
		- modern military states
			- provides equipment and provisions
		- "War in our time is a war of machines.... Only the bureaucratic army structure allowed for the development of professional standing armies that are necessary for the constant pacification of large states of the plains, as well as for warfare against far-distant enemies." #quote 
- [[Max Weber]]'s ambivalent attitude toward the historical process of "rationalization"
	- [[Liberalism]] in [[Max Weber]]
		- knows bureaucratic machine may be more efficient
			- but has limitations
		- but feels for "the individual bureaucrat who cannot squirm out of the apparatus in which he is harnessed" #quote 
			- "the professional bureaucratic is chained to his activity by his material and ideal existence" #quote 
- functioning and dysfunctioning of bureaucracies 
	- focus of attention for public administration discipline
	- indebted to works of [[Max Weber]]
		- influential to IR literature that examines the role of bureaucracies in foreign policy decision-making and crisis situations
- [[Max Weber]]'s affinity with [[Machiavelli]]
	- sympathy and respect for dilemmas and problems professional politicians face
	- [[Max Weber]] questions [[Enlightenment]]'s faith in power of reason to overcome political problems of governing
		- [[Machiavelli]] refuses to find comfort in laws of nature
	- Weber: states pursue conflicting values, so the statesman's primary duty is to promote national values
	- [[Machiavelli]]: political leaders have to deal with the unexpected ([[Fortuna]])
		- Weber: political actions result in unintended consequences
- [[Max Weber]] addresses issue of relation between ethics and politics
	- ethical conduct for politicians can be guided by two different maxims:
		1. an ethic of ultimate ends
			- an individual driven for religious or other purposes
			- associated with [[Enlightenment]] and 19th c. [[Liberalism]] writers
			- to follow: assume the goal being pursued is of such importance that how one achieves the goal are of secondary importance
				- a true believer in revolution may undertake actions that precipitate a harsh gov't reaction to the immediate detriment of the working class because the belief that such sacrifices are required in the name of the revolution
		2. an ethic of responsibility
			- when political leaders must anticipate the consequences of their decisions and actions
			- to follow: take into account possible results of one's actions
	- politicians must balance both ethics maxims
		- [[Max Weber]]'s sensitivity to the dilemma is part of [[Realism]] dating back to [[Thucydides]] and 20th c. writers (Hans J. [[Morgenthau]], Raymond [[Aron]])

#### 4.2.11 Adam Smith
- [[Adam Smith]] (1723 – 1790)
	- [[The Wealth of Nations by Adam Smith]] and [[Theory of Moral Sentiments by Adam Smith]]
	- [[Scotland]]'s [[Enlightenment]]
	- professor at Glasgow University
	- focuses on the workings of markets in world economy
- Smith's [[Realism]]
	- approach to finding ways and means of wealth of nations is state-centric
- Smith's [[Liberalism]]
	- states shouldn't place restrictions on the market
		- allows investors, producers, workers, consumers the freedom to make economic choices
	- opposed to [[mercantilism]]
- Smith on the marketplace
	- freedom in the marketplace should apply to all "social orders"
		- [[Marx]] and others later identify as "classes"
	- angry at owners of means of production who are prone to abridge [[Liberalism]] in relation to classes
		- e.g. using state powers to restrict right of workers to bargain for their wages
	- those working for wages don't always comprehend their interests
		- [[Marx]] later identifies as lack of "[[class consciousness]]"
	- landowners receiving rents and owners of capital receiving profits are aware of their interests
	- early ideas of [[class conflict]] and [[exploitation]] of one class by another
- [[The Wealth of Nations by Adam Smith]]
	- important concept of the "invisible hand" in a free market
		- allocates factors of production (land, labor, capital) in ways not purposefully done by those making individual economic decisions
	- idea of a system as a different level of analysis from individuals, forms, other groups/units of which it's composed of
		- individual level – each actor is expected to act rationally to maximize self-interest
		- market of freely competing individuals/firms, efficiency produced by competition results in greater aggregate output of goods/services
	- wealth of nations enhanced by allowing [[free-market]] principles to prevail domestically AND int'lly
- Smith on [[free-market]] principles
	- free markets composed of competitive, interacting units
		- results in equilibrium prices for quantities produced and supplied to markets
		- adapted by [[Realism]] regarding equilibrium/[[balance of power]] that occurs among competing states
- Smith's reservations about free trade's impact on society in terms of the ability of a nation to defend itself
	- the "bad effect of commerce is that it sinks the courage of humanity, and tends to extinguish the martial spirit" #quote 
	- result of division of labor in advanced economies
	- those engaged in commercial activities have no time/interest in performing military service
		- protection of country assigned to military professionals
		- results in "bulk of the people military courage diminshes"
	- [[David Ricardo]] (1772 – 1823), [[classical economic theory]]
		- intellectual champion of [[free trade]]
		- [[labor theory of value]]
		- builds on Smith's [[free-market]] [[Capitalism]]
			- develops theory of comparative advantage
				- [[free-market]] forces, unhampered by trade restrictions, leads to int'l specialization based on relative efficiency
					- maximizes overall production
		- critical of protectionist agricultural policies in [[UK]] which favored landholding aristocratic interests at the expense of capital-owning and labor/working-class interests
#### 4.2.12 Marx
- Karl [[Marx]] (1818 – 1883)
	- [[classical economic theory]], but takes [[Adam Smith]] and [[David Ricardo]]'s ideas 
		- far from classical economists's [[Liberalism]]
	- also influenced by [[Plato]], [[Aristotle]], [[Rousseau]], [[Hegel]], [[Kant]]
		- [[Plato]] and [[Aristotle]]'s influences
			- class analysis as method
			- slavery as form of political economy (mode of production in Marx's terms)
		- [[Rousseau]]'s influence
			- metaphor of the working man in "chains"
		- [[Hegel]]'s influence
			- Marx adapts his view of history as a dialectical clash of ideas to emphasize economic underpinnings of ideas
				- [[dialectical materialism]] in [[History]]
	- sees history in stages with progression from one form of political economy (mode of production) to another
		- rooted in [[Adam Smith]]
			- addresses rise of towns and their relation to the countryside
	- born  and educated in [[Germany]]
	- worked in [[UK]], London, British Museum library
	- [[labor theory of value]] central to his theoretical work
		- [[neoclassical economism]] critiques [[classical economic theory]]
			- Léon [[Walras]] (1834 – 1910) from [[France]]
			- [[Alfred Marshall]] (1842 – 1924) from [[UK]]
			- Thorstein [[Veblen]] (1857 – 1929) from [[America]] and [[Norway]]
			- value (price of good/service) becomes understood as a function of its supply and the demand for it in [[free-market]]s
				- [[classical economic theory]]: value is the hours of labor put into its production
- Smith's influences on Marx are usually overlooked since their conclusions are different
- Marx wasn't focused on the political economy of individual states
	- Smith and Marx recognize different levels of development and associated wealth of individual countries
	- Smith and Marx view countries as part of a larger whole (not isolated entities)
		- critical to [[Economic Structuralism]] on IR
- Smith's anti-mercantilist position: wealth of nations increases in [[free trade]] environment that allows for accumulation of capital
	- Marx is not just about class and class conflict within a given state – it's also an internationalist vision
- Marx sees the world divided by [[materialism]] based on [[class conflict]]
	- class divisions cut across state boundaries
		- prime source of conflict
			- analysis in contrast to [[Realism]]'s emphasis on conflict arising from interstate competition
	- growing [[class consciousness]] in [[proletariat]]/working class will reach its climax in a [[proletarian revolution]]
	- over an unspecified period, the state (instrument of control by dominant capitalist class) will fade away, as well as int'l/interstate relations
- Marx's work blends [[determinism]] and [[voluntarism]]
	- [[Marx]]: "Men make their own history, but they do not make it as they please; they do not make it under circumstances chosen by themselves, but under circumstances directly found, given, and transmitted from the past." #quote 
	- humans can understand the historical process
		- action can enhance the possibility of achieving greater individual freedom and dignity
	- [[Epistemology]] perspective (not just normative), different from positivist epistemology that traditionally informed IR
		- positivist epistemology usually seeks to discern ahistorical, universal laws/forces and assumes empirical/normative concerns can/should be separated
	- remember: [Epistemic](https://www.google.com/search?q=Epistemic&oq=epistemology+vs+norma&gs_lcrp=EgZjaHJvbWUqCAgBEAAYFhgeMgYIABBFGDkyCAgBEAAYFhgeMggIAhAAGBYYHjIICAMQABgWGB4yDQgEEAAYhgMYgAQYigUyDQgFEAAYhgMYgAQYigUyDQgGEAAYhgMYgAQYigUyBwgHEAAY7wUyCggIEAAYgAQYogQyBwgJEAAY7wXSAQg0MzIxajBqN6gCALACAA&sourceid=chrome&ie=UTF-8&mstk=AUtExfB-7ERD3afTOhJ1ChjRMrjQEV31ghW1FPl2WQVOeFJT6GqERhMx-pZuhscFpHEE1Euu5bYE6qNPgNtP-BcrCB0fy1yi-svI3_GVE1wpJRHCFRZiMt_Fa9DE_7dwFzlQ-Fej_uGmwDT63dAPgFPFi3Qe4Pi5WH7WmUz6i2hKPrTXAcntdNk8imUy1hw69EuRB3wxzAPyBJ3SbfZPiJGOF54YSw&csui=3&ved=2ahUKEwj778zMvNWTAxWAOzQIHfXqMlIQgK4QegQIARAC) concerns relate specifically to knowledge, truth, evidence, and justified belief ("what is true"), while [normative](https://www.google.com/search?q=normative&oq=epistemology+vs+norma&gs_lcrp=EgZjaHJvbWUqCAgBEAAYFhgeMgYIABBFGDkyCAgBEAAYFhgeMggIAhAAGBYYHjIICAMQABgWGB4yDQgEEAAYhgMYgAQYigUyDQgFEAAYhgMYgAQYigUyDQgGEAAYhgMYgAQYigUyBwgHEAAY7wUyCggIEAAYgAQYogQyBwgJEAAY7wXSAQg0MzIxajBqN6gCALACAA&sourceid=chrome&ie=UTF-8&mstk=AUtExfB-7ERD3afTOhJ1ChjRMrjQEV31ghW1FPl2WQVOeFJT6GqERhMx-pZuhscFpHEE1Euu5bYE6qNPgNtP-BcrCB0fy1yi-svI3_GVE1wpJRHCFRZiMt_Fa9DE_7dwFzlQ-Fej_uGmwDT63dAPgFPFi3Qe4Pi5WH7WmUz6i2hKPrTXAcntdNk8imUy1hw69EuRB3wxzAPyBJ3SbfZPiJGOF54YSw&csui=3&ved=2ahUKEwj778zMvNWTAxWAOzQIHfXqMlIQgK4QegQIARAD) concerns involve values, obligations, and "what ought to be" (ethics, morality)

#### 4.2.13 Cobden and Liberalism
- 19th c. [[Liberalism]] and [[Utilitarianism]]
	- assumption of potential for harmony among states and peoples in conjunction with the faith that unrestricted economic activity will enhance possibility of achieving that harmony
- Richard [[Cobden]] (1804 – 1865)
	- 1841: leader of Anti-Corn Law League in [[UK]] [[House of Commons]]
	- 1846: manages to repeal the [[Corn Laws]] act against wishes of protectionist landed interests
		- victory for [[free trade]] thinking of [[David Ricardo]]
	- argues [[free trade]] will enhance nation's prosperity
	- addresses issues of relation between [[free trade]] and [[War]]
	- addresses influence of military establishments on [[War]]
	- addresses the harmful effects of balance-of-power policies
	- 3 claims concerning impact of [[free trade]] on peace
		1. states fight most wars to achieve their [[mercantilism]] goals
			- [[free trade]] would show leaders a more effective and peaceful means to achieving nat'l wealth
		2. even in the case of wars not arising from commercial rivalry, those domestic interests that would suffer from the interruption of [[free trade]] due to [[War]] will restrain and oppose hostilities
		3. with an expansion of [[free trade]], contact and communication among people would expand
			- encourages int'l friendship and understanding
			- coalitions would favor peace over war
			- military orgs would be against this development because it would render militaries obsolete
	- int'l trade –> int'l peace
- [[Cobden]] and [[Liberalism]]
	- assumes "the great masses of mankind are disposed for peace between nations"
	- optimistic that humanity would negate the power of those who have vested interest in maintaining int'l conflict
		- through power of public opinion
	- as threat of [[War]] recedes, peace would become apparent since tax dollars would be diverted to domestic programs
- [[Cobden]] argues important obstacle to reduction in spending on arms is the policy of [[balance of power]]
	1. [[balance of power]] suffers from vague and multiple definitions
	2. as a policy for [[UK]], it's a little more than a [[SMOKESCREEN]] for British interests
		- word balance designed "to please the public ear; it implied something of equity; whilst [[England]], holding the balance of [[Europe]] in her hand, sounded like filling the office of Justice herself to one-half of the globe" #quote 
	- the cause of [[War]]s
		- "The [[balance of power]] is a chimera! It is not a fallacy, a mistake, an imposture – it is an undescribed, indescribable, incomprehensible nothing." #quote 
- summary of [[Cobden]]
	- emphasis on [[free trade]] as a means to mitigate aggressive tendencies of states
	- faith in ability of political leaders to learn alternative means to achieve nat'l interests
	- analysis of important role played by domestic constituencies in foreign affairs
	- epitomized 19th c. [[Liberalism]]'s thought on [[IR]]
	- contributed to development of [[Liberalism]]'s image of [[IR]]
#### 4.2.14 Hobson
- John A. [[Hobson]] (1858 – 1940)
	- works on [[imperialism]] that contributed to [[Economic Structuralism]]'s image of [[IR]]
- [[Imperialism – A Study (1902) by John A. Hobson]]
	- discusses "economic taproot" of [[imperialism]]
	- societies of [[Capitalism]] suffer from 3 interrelated problems:
		1. overproduction of goods due to efficiency of modern machinery
		2. underconsumption of those products by lower classes whose meager wages don't give them sufficient purchasing power to buy the excess goods
		3. over-savings by capitalists, helped by paying workers low wages
- problem of excess goods and capitals
	- solution for capitalists: find new markets
	- Western capitalist markets suffer from same issues so they can't be the outlet for another state's goods and capital
	- therefore, capitalists urge their gov'ts to lay claim to underdeveloped territories (in [[Asia]] and [[Africa]]) that represent untapped markets
		- result = [[imperialism]]: "the endeavor of the great controllers of industry to broaden the channel for the flow of their surplus wealth by seeking foreign markets and foreign investments to take off the goods and capital they cannot sell or use at home." #quote 
- [[Hobson]]: [[imperialism]] doesn't benefit the home country as a whole
	- selected groups (industrialists, financiers, individuals who staff the colonial empire) profit
	- large military expenditures required to protect imperialist system
	- drive for capitalist profits by securing overseas territories leads to competition and rivalry among [[Europe]]an powers
	- [[imperialism]] is a major cause of [[War]]
	- capitalists may profit from such conflicts
- more on [[imperialism]]
	- some analysts say [[imperialism]] is inevitable anywhere there are excessive powers of production
		- excessive capital in search of investment appears
	- Hobson disagrees
		- the maldistribution of consuming power prevents the absorption of goods/capitals within a particular country
		- solution: divert from capitalists their excess goods/capital –> workers (higher wages) or to community (via taxes)
			- either policy increases domestic consumption
			- result: no more need to fight for foreign markets/foreign areas of investment
		- it's in the best interest of possessing classes that the current use of surplus capitals/goods forces a policy of [[imperialism]]
#### 4.2.15 Lenin
- Vladimir Ilyich [[Lenin]] (1870 – 1924)
	- highly influential theory of int'l political change
- [[Imperialism – The Highest Stage of Capitalism (1916) by Vladimir Ilyich Lenin]]
	- [[Hobson]]'s analysis impacted this work
		- Lenin accepts critical importance of underconsumption and overproduction as stimuli for the search of foreign markets, leading to [[Colonialism]]
	- Rudolph [[Hilferding]] (1877 – 1941)
		- from [[Germany]], social democrat
		- Lenin adopts argument: critical feature of [[imperialism]] is finance capital
			- not so much industrial capital
	- wrote during [[WWI]]
	- explains the capitalist [[exploitation]] of lesser-developed countries
	- explains causes of [[War]] among advanced capitalist states
	- purpose: convince [[Marxism]] believers that the time is ripe for [[revolution]] ([[proletarian revolution]])
- Marx argues internal contradictions of [[Capitalism]] and its inherently exploitative nature will eventually lead to working-class/[[proletarian revolution]]
	- when predicted revolutions didn't occur, Marxists attempted to explain why
		- [[Lenin]]: because of [[imperialism]]
		- [[imperialism]] provided the European working class a taste of spoils, resulting of the [[exploitation]] of colonies, which dampened proletarian discontent
		- once the "whole world had been divided up, there was inevitably ushered in the era of monopoly possession of colonies."
			- then followed a "particularly intense struggle for the division and redivision of the world"
			- struggle among capitalist states was intensified due to continual, but uneven, growth of [[Capitalism]]
				- saw the rise of some states and relative decline of others
	- [[Lenin]] posits: [[power transition theory]] that relies on economic determinants at the expense of political factors
		- emphasis on exploitative nature of [[Capitalism]] and its universality
			- contributed to [[Economic Structuralism]] image of [[IR]]
#### 4.2.16 Reflections on Modern Thought Related to States and Capitalism
- writers observed a development of a new world order
	- marked politically by emergence of states
	- marked economically by [[Capitalism]]
- observed developments required a fundamental mindset change 
	- from [[medieval]] notions of essential unity of heavenly and earthly orders (sacred and secular)
- new concepts and ideas introduced
- [[Realism]] and [[Liberalism]] writers
	- states are sovereign entities with no authority above them
- attention paid to analyze the nature of int'l politics rather than viewing int'l conflict as stemming only from nature of humanity or nature of states/societies
	- new world: statecraft recognized as most important enterprise
	- [[Kant]] and other 19th c. [[Liberalism]] writers favored constraints:
		- [[Republicanism]] and public opinion placed on decision makers whose excess [[voluntarism]] was viewed as a major cause of [[War]]
- [[Realism]] writers
	- power and power politics result in balances of power among states
- [[Liberalism]] and [[Economic Structuralism]] writers
	- markets and commercial relations viewed as transcending nat'l borders with important political consequences
- [[Liberalism]] writers
	- economic [[Transnationalism]] can have an integrating and pacifying effect on relations among states
- [[Economic Structuralism]] writers
	- spread of [[Capitalism]] is a destructive and exploitative process
