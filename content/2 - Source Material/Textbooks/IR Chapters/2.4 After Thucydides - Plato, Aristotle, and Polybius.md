#### 2.4.2 Plato
- [[Plato]] (427 – 348 BCE)
	- distinguished family in politics
	- witnessed political leaders pander to the public and commit to the disastrous Sicilian expedition (412 BCE)
		- after expedition, oligarchic control was established (413 BCE)
	- power invested to [[Council of Four Hundred]]
	- succeeded by [[Government of Five Thousand]]
	- succeeded by opposition forces that initiated 6 years of *democratic terror*
	- political philosopher whose indirect effect on thinking about world politics has been profound
		- [[dialectical reasoning]] – dealing with opposites (Socratic)
			- **Hegel** and **Marx** used it in their development of theories
			- hypothetical opposites/conflicting analyses are the core of critical thinking and essential to theorizing
- 404 BCE: Athens defeated by Sparta
	- oligarchic revolution results in political and personal vendettas
	- regime only lasts 8 months
	- democracy restored
- 399 BCE: ordered the death of Socrates
	- grounds of impiety and corruption of the young
- 386 BCE: the Academy is established
- Plato and Aristotle
	- began systematic thinking about politics and the function and nature of the state
- Plato's questions
	- How does the state come into being?
	- How can the state's security be enhanced?
	- What's the primary cause of war among states?
	- Do different types of states exhibit different types of foreign policy behavior?
	- What's the relation between power and justice?
##### 2.4.2.1 The State and Security
- *The Republic* – the nature of justice
	- Glaucon: conventional wisdom assumes that it's "a good thing to inflict wrong or injury, and a bad thing to suffer it"
		- after experiencing both, people decide "they can't evade the one and achieve the other, so it will pay to make a compact with each other by which they forgo both."
		- "They accordingly... make laws and mutual agreements..."
		- human nature is selfish
			- people must be "forcibly restrained by the law and made to respect each other's claims"
- early forms of the social contract theory
	- **Thomas Hobbes:** social contract theorist, uses myth of the social contract to justify civil order within states
	- **Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau (17th-18th c.):** who should be the final authority and why the individual citizen should obey it
	- Glaucon: establish a morally justifiable and political basis of mutual obligation
		- "no man is just of his own free will, but only under compulsion... he will always do wrong when he gets the chance"
- Socrates: "society originates because the individual is not self-sufficient, but has many needs that he can't supply himself"
	- important: food, shelter, clothing
	- not mentioned: personal security
		- motivates social contract theorists to justify the state as final authority
	- security is only important after the state begins to expand
- *The Laws* – emphasizes man's innocence
	- there was enough food and clothing
	- "techniques of warfare peculiar to city-life"
	- Socrates: "the territory was enough to support us, now it will be too small"
		- need to gain neighbor's territory
		- if neighbor is growing as well, they will want to invade our territory
		- leads to war
	- Socrates: origin of war is evil greed (individual and social)
		- to defend property and possession of citizens, need an army
	- Glaucon: is a citizen army sufficient?
		- Socrates: greatest importance that the business of war should be efficiently run
			- those in the military require "complete freedom from other affairs and a correspondingly high degree of skill and practice"
	- Plato's *Guardian class* – rulers (philosopher-kings) and auxiliaries (soldiers)
		- using [[auxiliaries]] to defend the city and enforce the laws set by guardians
			- savage to foreigners
				- Aristotle disagrees: "harshness to strangers is wrong... fierceness is not a mark of natural greatness of mind except toward wrongdoers"
	- Plato: need soldiers to ensure safety and final victory in war
- Plato and Aristotle: *polis* is the supreme form of organization because it's created by human beings acting in accordance with their own nature
	- suspicious of the world beyond the city-state
- *The Laws* – utopian state to be insulated from the outside world
	- wary of foreign visitors to introduce "novel customs"
	- contact with foreigners to a minimum
	- "no young person under 40 is ever to be allowed to travel abroad under any circumstances"
	- "no one is allowed to \[travel abroad] for private reasons, but only on some public business"
	- those who do go abroad, when they return, are obligated to "tell the younger generation the social and political customs of the rest of the world don't measure up to their own"

##### 2.4.2.3 Power and Justice
- Plato: justice consists of individuals each doing the job for which one is naturally suited
	- begins class analysis
		- influenced Marx and economist-structuralists
- **Thrasymachus:** "I say that justice or right is simply what is in the interest of the stronger party."
- *Gorgias* 
	- Callicles: nature "demonstrates that it is right that the better man should prevail over the worse and the stronger over the weaker..."
		- "Right consists in the superior ruling over the inferior... are in accordance with natural law."
			- case study: Darius invading Scythia
			- case study: Xerxes invading Greece
	- Plato does not have the "might makes right" stance
		- "might makes right" is a straw man that gets knocked down in further discourse
- case study: Thucydides' Melian dialogue
	- Athens argues its necessary law of nature to rule whatever one can
	- Athens divorces the question of "right" from that of power
	- Athens tells Melians to submit to power of Athens to "leave justice out of account and confine ourselves to self-interest"
		- not because "we have a right to our empire because we defeated the Persians"
- Plato as an idealist
	- *The Republic* – emphasis on the philosopher king
	- *The Laws* – depiction of utopia
	- optimistic, voluntarist preference that assumes wise leadership ensure
		- security of the city-state
		- just and well-ordered society
	- two cautions
		- determinism – it's nature that wills a particular end, not the will of an individual
			- [[teleological view]] of state development
			- there are purposes apart from the will of individuals that drive construction of the state
		- pessimism
			- not many likely role models: no one "has been a good statesman in this country"
			- ruler should strive to achieve perfection in conduct and policies
				- Platonic ideal of state and constitution looks highly unlikely
			- Aristotle's work is more pragmatic and optimistic about leader achievement
#### 2.4.3 Aristotle
- [[Aristotle]] (384 BCE – 322 BCE)
	- born in Chalcidice in Macedonia
	- went to Athens, student of Plato's Academy
	- after Plato's death, traveled to Asia Minor and conducted scientific studies
- 343 BCE: returned to Macedonia and taught Alexander
- 338 BCE: Philip groups most Greek states into federation under Macedonian control
- 336 BCE: returned to Athens, Alexander leads armies eastward
- 335 BCE: Aristotle establishes Lyceum school
- 323 BCE: Alexander's death
	- anti-Macedonian feeling intensifies
	- Aristotle moves to Euboea (dies there at 62 yrs old) – always a foreigner in Athens
- Aristotle's influence
	- *Politics*
		- Polybius and Cicero found more interest in Plato's *Republic* than Aristotle's *Politics*
		- domestically oriented – a search for the ideal constitution
			- little to say about empires or general IR
		- IR theory influence:
			- conceptual and empirical approach to political phenomena
			- detailed examination of different types of state constitutions and the causes of instability
			- writings on economics
	- *Ethics* and *Politics*
		- impact not apparent until 12th c. when religious scholars ([[Thomas Aquinas]]) studies them closely
	- 14th c. Dante was influenced
	- Machiavelli, Jean Bodin, Hobbes
	- the monarchical ruler needs to "give the impression of dignity, not of harshness, of being the kind of person who inspires not fear but respect in those who meet him"
		- "this is not easy if he is readily despise"
		- when "he cannot manage to cultivate any other virtues, he should aim at least at valor in warfare, and establish for himself a military reputation"
##### 2.4.3.1 Epistemology
- Aristotle's analytic approach
- Plato: all objects have a perfect form or ideal essence, but that perfection can never be achieved, only approximated empirically in the world in which we live
- Aristotle: things are continually moving toward their full completeness and, through this natural process, perfection can be achieved
- origins of the state
	- Aristotle: not a social contract perspective (laws/contract does not bind people)
		- development of city-state is a natural follow-on from family to village
		- [[Aristotle]]: "While the state came about as a means of securing life itself, it continues in being to secure the *good* life. Therefore every state exists by nature, as the earlier associations too were natural. This association is the end of those others.... It follows that the state belongs to the class of objects that exist by nature, and that man is by nature a political animal." #quote 
	- hints of [[social contract theory]]
		- [[Aristotle]]: "The point is that if a constitution is to have a good prospect of stability, it must be such that all sections of the state accept it and want it to go on in the same way as before." #quote 
- perfection resides in individuals or political communities that have the potential to achieve completeness
	- Aristotle's fascination with rigorous empirical observation to make this idea credible
- Aristotle collected information on 168 constitutions
	- classified based on conceptions of the just distribution of political power and assessed causes of their relative stability
##### 2.4.3.2 Constitutions, Stability, and Security
- [[Politics by Aristotle]] – classifies constitutions
	- based on number and aim of rules
		- [[monarchy]] – rule by one
		- [[aristocracy]] – rule by few
		- polity – rule by many (mass of populace)
	- commonality of the "ideal forms": a concern for the common interest
	- "corresponding deviations"
		- tyranny – when monarchies become dictatorial
		- oligarchy – when the few use their position for self-aggrandizement
		- democracy – when the many run rampant over the few
	- key criterion for classification is *economic*
		- Aristotle's emphasis on *material factors*
			- [[Aristotle]]: "oligarchy occurs when the sovereign power of the constitution is in the hands of those with possessions" #quote 
			- [[Aristotle]]: "democracy occurs when it is in the hands of those who have no stock of possessions and are without means" #quote 
		- Marx incorporated insights to include his identification of slavery as an ancient mode of production (found in Aristotle)
	- another criterion is the division of political power based on what's viewed by a society as "just"
		- in Aristotle's examination of states and constitutions, their downfall almost always stems from internal factors
			- case study: aristocracy
				- five contributing factors listed
				- disparity of wealth between rich and poor – related to int'l environment
		- disparity of wealth
			- likely to happen in wartime
		- when revolutions happen
			- democracies – "the most potent causes of revolution is the unprincipled character of popular leaders"
			- oligarchies – "when the oligarchs wrong the multitude" or arise "out of the oligarchs' own rivalry"
- *The Laws* – Aristotle + Plato's belief: "in framing the laws a legislator ought to have regard both to the territory and to the population"
	- [[Aristotle]]'s criticism of [[Plato]]: "surely we should add that he should take note of the neighboring territories too" as a state "must provide itself with arms for warfare as are serviceable not only internally but also against territory beyond its border" #quote 
- [[Politics by Aristotle]] – Aristotle's ideal state
	- basic security matters
	- war should only be a means to defend the good life
	- security is essentially in the realm of defense
		- necessary to have "sufficient armed force to give the laws protection" and to "repel attempts at wrongdoing coming from outside"
		- Aristotle: need "to secure the greatest degree of protection that strong walls can afford"
			- best military measure when "the superiority of the attackers is too much for the valor" of the defenders
	- enemies will less likely attack well-defended city-states
	- military-strategic realist view: if you seek peace, prepare for war
		- basic element of present-day [[deterrence theory]]
- Aristotle: a city-state's power are affected directly by some other factor
	- example: location
- Aristotle: good government is the critical source of security
	- more than provide military protection against injustice and facilitate mutual acquaintance and exchange of good, a state must also promote civic virtue among citizenry and ensure government under good laws
- Aristotle: a good constitution is a mixed one or "polity" with a large middle class that blends elements of oligarchy and democracy and contributes to security and good living
- unity within the state under a system of good and effective laws is important to the security of any city-state
##### 2.4.3.3 Economy
- class analysis, approach with domestic and international applications
	- [[Aristotle]]: "a division of the state into classes is necessary" #quote 
	- defense of slavery and master-slave relationship integral to political economy of his time
- class formation due to
	- economic emergencies
	- security threats from possible slave revolts or foreign powers
- Aristotle's critical parts of the state
	- military
	- deliberative elements – supreme, authoritative part of the state responsible for deciding on matters of public policy (war, peace, treaties, law-making, appointment of officials)
- [[Aristotle]]: "the reason for plurality of constitution lies in the plurality of parts of every state" #quote 
	- "some must be wealthy, others poor, others in the middle" #quote 
	- "in addition to wealth there are other *differentiae* of family, virtue, and any other similar feature described as 'part' of a state" #quote 
- influence on **labor theory of value**
	- Adam Smith, David Ricardo, Karl Marx
	- Marx combines class analysis and political economy of ancients, changing their view through history to the 19th c. circumstances he observed
- Marx's *[[Communist Manifesto]]* 
	- all history is the history of class struggles beginning with the master-slave relationship
		- distant from Aristotle's "master over slave"
	- characterizes dominant mode of production of the ancients as slavery
		- successive historical and global modes of feudal and capitalist production
- Aristotle on property rights
	- should be held in common, but general principle should be of private ownership
	- does not like charging interest for the loan of money
		- interest earnings are not the outcome of productive labor, but "gain arises out of currency itself"
		- now seen as essential to capitalist mode of production
- Aristotle on trade
	- derives from "men having too much of this and not enough of that" (classical economists)
	- "exchange is carried on far enough to satisfy the needs of the parties"
	- "the import of necessities and export of surplus goods began to facilitate the satisfaction of needs beyond national frontiers"
	- traders "resorted to the use of coined money"
- ethnocentric rejection of foreign ways (Greek writers)
	- Aristotle shared this view
	- not a fan of foreign trade
	- concerned that foreigners and their ideas would undermine the unity of the state
- Aristotle on foreigners
	- argues for two judicial courts: "one for foreigners disputing with foreigners" and "the other for foreigners disputing with citizens"
	- not the dominant image in ancient Greece
		- suggestion that human beings constitute a human family that extends beyond the borders of any given tribe, city-state, or set of city-states
		- later found in the Stoics
- [[Alexander the Great]] spread Greek political ideas beyond Athens and Greek city-states
- new cosmopolitanism departed from ethnocentrism (Plato, Aristotle, other Greeks) in Alexander's policy of encouraging soldiers to marry within local populations conquered by force of arms
- Alexander's conquests spread Hellenic ideas throughout the world of his time
#### 2.4.4 Polybius
- "The work of the Greek historian Polybius (c. 200–120 BCE) is not often read or even cited by most present-day political theorists. This omission is a mistake. Such obscurity is unwarranted." #quote 
- [[Polybius]] (200 BCE – 120 BCE)
	- influence on political philosophers ([[Machiavelli]], [[Montesquieu]], US constitution writers)
	- rational approach, study of politics by relating observations to theory
	- uses primary sources and takes critical view of data collected
	- seeks causal explanation for his observed phenomena
	- theorist
	- Punic Wars (Rome v. Carthage), rise of the Roman empire
		- seeks explanatory variables for phenomenon at int'l, domestic, individual levels of analysis
	- born in Greece (Megalopolis)
	- son of a wealthy landowner
	- attracted to history and political theory
	- talents and family connections –> elected a cavalry leader in the Achaean League
	- Greeks caught between rival ambitions of Macedonians and Romans
- 168 BCE: Macedonian defeat at Pydna
	- Rome moved to purge any Greek notables they distrusted, included Polybius
- Polybius sent to Italy for cross-examination and remained there for 16 years without being tried or accused
	- housed in Rome, became friends with **Publius Scipio**
- Publius Scipio
	- son of the Roman general who had defeated Macedonians at Pydna
- Polybius gets acquainted with many of Rome's leading families
	- accompanies Scipio to Spain
	- visits North Africa and queried King of Numidia about Hannibal
	- returns to Italy through the Alps (Hannibal's crossing)
- Achaean detainees allowed to return to Greece
	- Polybius goes to Carthage at Scipio's request to advise the Roman's siege
- After capture and burning of Carthage, Publius goes beyond Gibraltar and down the coast of Africa
- Publius returns to Greece
	- spends 2 years as an intermediary between Romans and Achaeans
- Achaean League disbanded, Roman troops removed
	- Polybius tries to resolve disputes among the cities
- Polybius visits Alexandria, Egypt (and probably Spain) with Scipio
- Polybius's work influences [[Livy]], [[Plutarch]], [[Cicero]] in the Roman era and receptive audience at [[Constantinople]] (modern-day [[Turkey]])
- 15th c. Polybius's writings reappear in Italy
	- discussion of Roman constitution influences [[Machiavelli]] and [[Montesquieu]]
- [[The Rise of the Roman Empire by Polybius]]
	- [[Polybius]]: "There can surely be nobody so petty or so apathetic in his outlook that he has no desire to discover by what means and under what system of government the Romans succeeded in less than 53 years in bringing under their rule almost the whole of the inhabited world, an achievement which is without parallel in human history." #quote 
	- wanted to enlighten his fellow Greeks as to *why* and *how* the city-state system had come under Roman domination
- why is Polybius's work of interest
	- his epistemology/approach to knowing about the world sound quite modern to contemporary scholars
	- his explanation of the rise of Rome takes into account the relative importance of different causes (external and internal factors), a multilevel approach
	- addresses the role of fate in aiding/frustrating the political and military designs of leaders
	- present-day realist and liberal images of IR rest on Polybius's foundation stones
##### 2.4.4.1 Epistemology
- Thucydides provided Polybius model for historical research
- Polybius's purpose: "to write a history of actual events"
- opening statement: "Humankind possess no better guide to conduct than the knowledge of the past." #quote 
- history is more than a collection of facts – explanation is the goal
	- [[Polybius]]: "...the most important part of historical writing lies in the consideration of the consequences of events, their accompanying circumstances, and above all their causes." #quote 
	- a better prediction may also result
	- drawing analogies between parallel circumstances of the past and present can help make forecasts about the future
		- comparing and evaluating parallel occurrences provides a more reliable general picture than if everything is judged in isolation
- Polybius favors personal investigation and primary sources
- Polybius: one must be an impartial observer as much as humanly possible
- Polybius: accuracy and honesty
	- two kinds of falsehoods: one being the result of ignorance and the other intentional (deception)
	- present-day challenges of mass comms and social networks that facilitate efforts of manipulation/distortion of facts/propagation of lies that serve particular interests
		- people tend to accept distortions/falsehoods when they're repeated over and over again
		- truthful counternarratives become essential to knowledge in the public sphere
##### 2.4.4.2 Explanation
- Polybius: one should differentiate causes from beginnings and pretexts
	- causes: events which influence in advance our purposes and decisions, conceptions of things, state of mind, calculations, the whole process of reasoning where we arrive at decisions and undertakings
- war between Greek King [[Antiochus]] (241 BCE – 187 BCE) v. Romans
	- cause of war: "anger felt by the Aetolians"
	- pretext of war: "so-called liberation of Greece"
	- beginning of war "Antiochus's descent upon [[Demetrius]]"
- one must take a longer view of history that contains antecedent causes
	- to focus only on particular episodes at the expense of the longer view runs the risk of exaggerating the importance of trivial incidents and write at length on matters scarcely worth mentioning at all
- Polybius's levels of analysis problem
	- What unit should one study?
	- Does one focus on the whole or on the parts that make up the whole?
	- How does one relate the general to the specific?
	- "a grasp of the whole is of great service in enabling us to master the details while, at the same time, some previous acquaintance with the details helps us toward the comprehension of the whole" #quote 
		- dynamic relation between theory building and data gathering
- Polybius: the occurrences observed are a part of "an organic whole: the affairs of Italy and of Africa are connected with those of Asia and Greece" and are all part of a larger causal explanation
- Polybius: domestic factors are as central to his explanation as the external environment
	- "in all political situations... the principal factor.. for success or failure is the form of a state's constitution" #quote 
	- Rome's success vs. Carthage is related to "the perfection and strength of the Roman constitution"
		- position also with [[Livy]] (Roman historian)
		- contributed to Roman rule over Italians and Sicilians, acquisition of Spain, recovery of northern Italy, end of war with Carthage –> "idea of attaining dominion of the whole world"
- the cohesiveness of a political unit ([[Herodotus]], [[Thucydides]]) can be enhanced if the constitution correctly allocates political power
	- "...whenever some common external threat compels the \[senate, consuls, and the people] to unite and work together, the strength that the state then develops becomes quite extraordinary" #quote 
- Polybius: Greeks of a particular problem with lack of unity, given numerous threats from foreign forces
- [[Machiavelli]]: writings reflect Polybius on the contribution of unity to security
	- Polybius: "every state relies for its preservation on two fundamental qualities: bravery in the face of the enemy and harmony among its citizens" #quote 
	- Polybius: danger of placing the security of the state in the hands of mercenary troops
	- Polybius: danger of [[Religion]]'s role as a means of keeping a political unit together
		- Polybius and Machiavelli: the idea of an "integrating myth" (religion) is also found in [[The Republic by Plato]]
- when customs, laws, and constitution are good, internal and external security is enhanced
- Polybius: voluntarism that allows human will to be decisive, human agency matters
	- Euripides: "one wise head can outmatch a score of hands" #quote 
	- Machiavelli adopts same perspective
- Polybius praises Hannibal as an example
- Polybius denigrates King Philip of Macedon
	- criticizes changes in the character that resulted in his loss of goodwill among his allies and the confidence of all Greeks
##### 2.4.4.3 Fate
- "Polybius does not assume that everything is predetermined or the result of fortune or fate. He cautions us lest we view outcomes merely as acts of divine beings." #quote 
- we should engage in causal analysis of events
	- the "answer must remain in doubt" only "where it is impossible or difficult to establish a cause"
- Polybius admits some events are affected by chance
	- "we are no more than mortal human beings, and we should at times make due allowance for the unexpected, and especially in time of war"
- Polybius on Machiavelli's development of [[Fortuna]] (chance phenomena), a key concept in Machiavelli's work
- Polybius balances voluntarism and determinism
	- normative element: suggests as individuals we should control what can be controlled and accept what cannot
		- "Human nature is always fallible, and to meet with some unpredictable mishap is not the fault of the victim, but rather of ill-fortune, or of those who have inflicted it on him. But when we err with our eyes open and involve ourselves in great tribulations through sheer lack of judgment, then everyone agrees that we have nobody to blame but ourselves. It follows therefore that if a people’s failures are due to ill-fortune, they will be granted pity, pardon and assistance, but if to their own folly, then all men of sense will blame and reproach them." #quote 
- Polybius: "we must rather seek a cause, since no chain of events, whether expected or unexpected, can reach its conclusion without a cause" #quote 
- **[[David Hume]]:** 18th c. empiricist critique of causality as purely abstract or metaphysical ("after all, who has ever seen a cause?")
##### 2.4.4.4 Realism and Liberalism
- [[Realism]] in Polybius's critique of the Athenian constitution
	- ship as a metaphor for the state
	- importance of unitary and effective leadership to avoid instability and keep the ship on course
	- "always reckon friendship or hostility in terms of advantage"
		- though one may enhance security by seeking allies, security is established most reliably when city-states don't look to others for their safety, but to choose to defend their cities and their territory with their own hands
	- "[[self-help system]]" – states provide for their own defense
- realist concern for political stability
- liberal tendency: Polybius idealizes Roman things
	- portrayal of Rome's constitution as a balance among the executive (consul), legislature (senate), and the people may not be entirely accurate
- Polybius similar to Aristotle and Greek writers: advocacy of mixed constitution that blends kingship, aristocracy, and democracy
	- adverse forms of tyranny (one-man rule), oligarchy (minority rule), and mob rule
	- idea that there are cyclical shifts in constitutional type
- adopted by Montesquieu and others: ideas of [[checks and balances]] among branches of [[government]]
	- Polybius: "Whenever one of the three elements swells in importance, becomes overambitious and tends to encroach upon the others, it becomes apparent for the reasons given above that none of the three is completely independent, but that the designs of any one can be blocked or impeded by the rest, with the result that none will unduly dominate the others or treat them with contempt. Thus, the whole situation remains in equilibrium since any aggressive impulse is checked, and each estate is apprehensive from the outset of censure from the others." #quote 
- Polybius's checks and balances
	- framed purely in domestic context
	- his discussion of democratic ideas are an important part of the corpus of ancient Greek political thought
		- foundation for liberal formulations of domestic and world politics during the [[Enlightenment]] (17th-18th c.)
- Polybius sees humans as social by nature (similar to Aristotle)
	- influences later social contract theorists: [[Hobbes]], [[Locke]], [[Rousseau]] ([[social contract theory]])
		- explains politics within a state or among states internationally
- Polybius on "the origins of a political society"
	- first: state of nature composed of primitive humans
	- later stage of societal development: the choice of rulers is not due anymore to physical strength so much as to "the merits of their judgment and the power of their reasoning"
- democratic theory and liberal perspective
	- important point: choice of leaders is *made by the people* and legitimacy of rulers stem from that process
	- unless people become dissatisfied, they are the ones who "ensure that the supreme power remains in the hands not only of the original leaders but also of their descendants"
- Polybius is vague on how to perform the role, people are the ultimate check
	- influences [[Madison]] and [[Hamilton]]
- market-oriented values – normative economic orientation usually associated with liberal thought on domestic and int'l political economy
	- case study: Cretans
		- their laws permit citizens to acquire land without restriction
		- money held in high regard as not merely necessary, but most honorable
		- "avarice and greed are so much ingrained in the Cretan character that they are the only people in the world who consider no form of gain to be shameful" #quote 
	- Cretan's market-oriented values were not universal or widespread at the time
	- commerce was extensive among ancient civilizations
	- Rome-Carthage treaty (1st Punic War)
		- important provision: agreement to establish normal trade relations
	- influence on [[Max Weber]]'s [[Protestant work ethic]] 
		- important basis for accumulation of capital
		- work and commercial values would become universalized
	- influence on [[Adam Smith]]'s [[Capitalism]]
	- influence on [[David Ricardo]]'s international trade ([[Internationalism]])
	- influence on [[Marx]]'s corpus of political-economic theorizing
- commerce contributes in the long run to the development of broader conceptions of society beyond borders of a city-state/empire
- Polybius: such democratic values (re: commerce) as "equality and freedom of speech"
	- these values spread because of a common sense of humanity and of equality
- expansion of Roman empire
	- writers speculate what is now called [[Transnationalism]] – the necessity to develop a set of universal values, a rules-based order, that can unite disparate people
#### 2.4.5 Reflections on the Thought of Plato, Aristotle, and Polybius
- Plato and Aristotle's major impact
	1. their interest in thinking systematically about politics
	2. Plato's analysis of the relation between power and justice
	3. Aristotle's discussion of economics
	4. their inspiration to later theorists who have investigated the relation between the nature of a state and its foreign policy behavior
- uphill struggle against prevailing attitudes by the Sophists
	- [[Sophists]] separated politics from morality and viewed the state as a mere convention, not in accordance with nature
- Polybius's discussion of constitutions derived from Plato's classifications
- Polybius shares with Aristotle interest in empirical observation and understanding causation
- Polybius differs from Plato and Aristotle in his view of the purpose of the state
	- Plato and Aristotle: city-state's purpose is to make individuals virtuous and fulfill their potential as humans
	- Polybius: city-state's modest goal of political stability
- in Polybius's time, *polis* was in decline due to debilitating effects of the Peloponnesian war
	- then Macedonia's military and political [[hegemony]]
	- then Rome's military and political [[hegemony]]
- Polybius does not see the purpose of the state as molding good people and helping them lead a virtuous life
- Polybius more similar to Thucydides and Machiavelli than Plato and Aristotle
	- dispassionate study of power, balanced appraisal of fate and human volition, interest in relative importance of internal and external factors to explain a state's power and status in the world
