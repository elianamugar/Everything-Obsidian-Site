#### 13.1.0 Norms, Ethics, and Morality
- philosophers differ on what norms, morality, and ethics mean
	- IR theory's commonality: all prescribe what the world *should be* and what *ought to be* right conduct
		- norms might require people, states, or int'l and transnational actors to act in ways that may not promote the actor's narrow understanding of its self-interest
- issues central to an understanding of normative IR
	- when, if ever, is war just?
	- are there universally understood *human rights*?
	- on what grounds are armed or other forms of intervention legitimate?
	- on what moral bases should those in authority make foreign policy choices?
- normative IR theory building as a legitimate enterprise
	- differs fundamentally from empirical theory
	- deals with what *ought* to be
		- not subject to formal empirical tests of hypotheses about what *is* (empirical theory)
	- informed by human experience of [[War]], peace, human rights, etc.
	- arguments on normative theory are still subject to same canons of logic that also apply to empirical theory
		- critical thinking about any truth claim involves an assessment of 
			- whether the argument is logical
			- whether the facts brought to bear by the theorist hold up under scrutiny
- values at the core of normative theory
	- value orientations are also present among empirical theorists in [[Realism]], [[Liberalism]], [[Economic Structuralism]], [[The English School]]
		- what is studied and how it's studied are preferences that vary among theorists
			- empirical theorists as [[Positivism]] scholars
				- tries to minimize the effect of individual value bias through objective testing of hypotheses
				- personal values can't be filtered out completely
	- values are less problematic in [[social constructivism]], [[critical theory]], [[postmodernism]], [[feminism]], [[green theory]]
		- central to interpretive understandings
- normative IR is important in foreign/int'l policy
	- policy-making is all about making choices
	- choices among competing alternatives made by policy makers are informed by knowledge (what *is* or *could be* the possible outcome of a decision) and rationale for what *ought* to be
		- developing the bases for these choices is the domain of normative theory
