#### 7.7.0 Postcolonialism
- 1980s: [[postcolonialism]] literature emerges in [[America]] and [[UK]]
	- influenced by anti-colonial writings from [[Global South]]
	- emphasizes an interdisciplinary perspective that encompasses economic, political, social, cultural aspects of [[Colonialism]] and [[decolonization]]
	- highlights importance of race, gender, ethnicity in understanding anti-colonial struggles and lingering effects of colonial experience after the end of formal imperial rule
	- examines impact of [[decolonization]] on both *metropolitan* and *colonized* societies
	- [[Wallerstein]]'s [[capitalist world-system]]
	- influence of [[critical theory]] and [[postmodernism]] critiques
- 1958: [[Third World]] meet in [[Bandung Conference]] in [[Indonesia]]
	- established [[Non-Aligned Movement]]
- 1966: [[Tricontinental Conference]] in [[Cuba]]
	- identified the movement as spanning [[Latin American]], [[Africa]], [[Asia]]
- 1970s: activities provide foundation for call for a new int'l economic order
	- [[postcolonialism]] politics/grass roots movement to fight a system of injustice and gross material inequality sustained by powerful local interests and int'l power structures of banks, business, investment funds
- [[postcolonialism]] debate of the definition of key concepts
	- true of all theories and images previously examined
	- [[imperialism]], [[Colonialism]], [[postcolonialism]]
		- reflect differing theoretical positions and political values
		- can there be only "formal" [[imperialism]], meaning a country forfeits its sovereignty and is incorporated into an empire?
			- can there be an "informal" [[imperialism]]?
				- if so, is that basically [[neocolonialism]]?
		- what about cases where an indigenous elite work with the foreign imperial power to exploit their own people?
			- would this be an example of "internal [[Colonialism]]"?
		- can one speak of a [[postcolonialism]] "[[hegemonic power]]"?
			- case study: [[America]]
- how to characterize [[America]]
	- can and should [[America]] be viewed as an imperial power?
	- depends on how an author defines [[imperialism]] and associated terms
	- conventional historians reinforce narrative that [[America]] isn't an imperial power due to its own historical struggle for independence from [[UK]] [[Colonialism]] and the resultant anti-imperialist culture
		- 19th c. [[Spanish-American War]] (1898) brief imperial turn in [[America]]'s policy that continues into 20th c.
	- [[postcolonialism]] writers
		- views conventional historians' narrative as a myth, part of [[America]]'s [[exceptionalism]] tale
			- used by [[Woodrow Wilson]] to champion national [[Self-Determination]] for oppressed peoples [[post-WWI]]
		- [[America]]'s [[imperialism]] dates back to [[Monroe Doctrine]] (1823) when [[hegemony]] was proclaimed over [[Latin American]]
		- [[Vietnam War]] reinforces this narrative
		- some simply assign [[America]] as an imperial power as part of the West in general
		- [[America]]'s continental expansion and dealings with indigenous Native Americans are considered a separate phenomenon
			- may not be captured by concepts like [[Colonialism]] devised to explain [[Europe]]'s experience in [[Africa]], [[Asia]], [[Middle East]], but still part of the imperial project engaged by [[America]] in its own sphere
- [[Colonialism]] (physical occupation) of a state isn't a hallmark of the int'l system, but empire and [[imperialism]] are
	- argued by [[postcolonialism]] scholars
	- [[postcolonialism]] as a term presumes going beyond the era of [[Colonialism]]
		- not clear what constitutes the temporal dividing line
	- [[postcolonialism]] scholars question the utility of trying to pinpoint the divide between [[Colonialism]] and [[postcolonialism]] eras
		- views 15th c. – now as seamless web of relations between the West and [[Third World]] countries
- while formal empires disintegrated, strategies were developed to retain [[Western]] power and influence before and during [[decolonization]] process
	- still in existence today in [[postcolonialism]] world
	- earlier approaches to maintain power during direct colonial rule
		- exploiting ethnic and racial divisions among subject people
		- co-opting activists into colonial administrations
		- extending judicious concessions in trade
	- have been supplemented by more subtle mechanisms of domination
		- use of [[IMF]] and World Bank
		- manipulation of language designed to encourage "mental [[Colonialism]]"
- [[postcolonialism]] world still exhibits [[neocolonialism]] forms of cultural, economic, political-military dominance over former colonies
	- independence hasn't really brought liberation when former colonies are so linked to the metropole (seat of power in former colonial country)
	- to understand politics in any [[Africa]] country, important to identify former colonial powers
		- [[UK]], [[France]], [[Belgium]], [[Netherlands]], [[Spain]], [[Portugal]]
- [[neocolonialism]] patterns of dominance remain prevalent in [[postcolonialism]] period
	- observed in trade or other economic arrangements the [[EU]] has with former colonies in [[Africa]], the [[Caribbean]], and the [[Pacific]] (ACP countries)
		- keeps these states in relations that still work to the net advantage of the metropole
- increased focus on recipients of colonial policies
	- early work on [[imperialism]] and [[Colonialism]] had distinct [[Western]] focus
		- [[UK]] tended to object of analysis
			- historical work emphasized [[UK]]'s motives and mechanisms of [[imperialism]] and [[Colonialism]]
		- [[France]]'s experience in [[Africa]] and [[Southeast Asia]]
		- large number of scholars from [[UK]] and [[France]] universities
		- drew attention to perspectives and experiences of people on receiving end of [[UK]], [[France]], other colonial and imperial policies
		- highlighted what was obscured in more mainstream work on [[Colonialism]]
			- attempts to resist military, political, economic, cultural repression and oppression
		- "[[postcolonialism]] claims the right of all people on this earth to the same material and cultural well-being." #quote 
			- politics of the "subaltern" (subordinated classes and peoples)
- concepts and arguments of [[Marx]], [[Lenin]], [[Hobson]]
	- informed [[Post-WWII]] work on [[imperialism]] and [[dependency]]
	- interest in impact of [[Capitalism]] on [[Third World]] wasn't limited to [[Marxism]] scholars
		- some pursued detailed case studies of colonial policies and relations between [[UK]] and a single colonized people
		- others took more global approach
			- traced capitalist development and its global expansion over the centuries
			- emphasis on economic drivers continues to be primary concern of many [[postcolonialism]] theorists
			- e.g. [[Wallerstein]]
		- some argue that [[Capitalism]] shouldn't be conflated with [[imperialism]]
			- pre-capitalist empires have existed throughout recorded history, before rise of [[Capitalism]] as a mode of production
			- hallmark of all empires is subjugation of weaker people
				- [[Roman Empire]], [[Ottoman Empire]], [[UK]], [[France]]
			- why privilege the [[Capitalism]]-[[imperialism]] nexus as opposed to placing it under a broader perspective of [[imperialism]]?
		- some emphasize critical role of [[Capitalism]] in terms of development of modern world
			- rebuttal: while ancient empires came and went, those empires that emerged in conjunction with rise of [[Capitalism]] exhibit unique characteristics linked to particular intellectual, technological, scientific innovations
				- these characteristics often placed under *modernity*
				- most evident in "[[New Imperialism]]"
					- emerged in 19th c.
					- associated with [[Western Europe]] and [[America]]
				- weapons technology and firepower
					- success of imperialist policies
				- intellectual and ideological claims
					- "white man's burden", [[Social Darwinism]], virulent racism
					- justified and motivated imperial policies
- Eurocentric analytical perspective restricts [[Capitalism]] as an economic mode of production to [[Europe]]
	- equates capitalist expansion with [[imperialism]]
	- argument: [[Africa]]-[[Asia]] market and capitalist practices existed before developments in [[Europe]]
		- [[India]], [[China]], [[Japan]] were advanced as [[Europe]] before 18th c.
		- many [[Asia]] cities were larger than any 18th c. [[Europe]] city
	- with development of long-distance trading networks in late 15th c., advantage turned to [[Europe]]
		- not a function of [[Europe]]'s superiority, but rather the coalescing of political, economic, environmental, cultural, population changes that enabled [[Capitalism]] and modernity to emerge
		- facilitated the dominance of the global political economy
		- most advanced [[Europe]] empires were the most economically advanced or most influenced by modernity (ideas of [[Enlightenment]])
			- [[France]], Holland ([[Netherlands]]), [[UK]]
			- [[Spain]] stagnates in semi-[[feudalism]]
			- [[Russia]] declines to second-tier imperial status
- concept of [[Globalization]] and its relation to [[imperialism]] and the [[postcolonialism]] era
	- some say [[Globalization]] is simply a new stage in [[Western]] [[imperialism]]
		- deepened racial, class, gender hierarchies and inequalities
		- total rejection that [[Globalization]] could be of benefit to any people except those in dominant positions of power
	- others argument: while there might be an increase in [[economic interdependence]], politically the world is breaking into blocs characterized by different forms and mutations of [[Capitalism]]
		- global world-system was completed in 20th c., but moved into prolonged period of crisis that will bring the system to its end
