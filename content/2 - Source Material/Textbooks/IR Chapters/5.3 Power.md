#### 5.3.1 Definitions
- *Power* is the core concept for [[Realism]]
	- some realists: sum of military, economic, technological, diplomatic capabilities at disposal of state
	- some realists: not some absolute value determined for each state, but rather as capabilities relative to capabilities of other states
		- power of [[America]] is evaluated in terms of its capabilities compared to capabilities of other states
	- both definitions are termed a [[materialism]] view
		- both assume a static view of power:
			- an attribute of the state that's the sum of its capabilities whether considered alone or relative to other states
	- alternative, dynamic definition focuses on interactions of states
		- state's influence (capacity to influence/coerce) is determined by its capabilities and
			1. its willingness (and perceptions by other states of its willingness) to use these capabilities
			2. its control/influence over other states
		- *Power* can be inferred by observing behavior of states as they interact
		- relative power of states is revealed by outcomes of their interactions
	- other diverse views of power's definitions:
		- *power* as the capacity of an individual, group, nation "to influence behavior of others in accordance with one's own ends"
		- *power* as "the ability to prevail in conflict and overcome obstacles"
- Joseph [[Nye]] differentiates *[[hard power]]* and *[[soft power]]*
	- *[[hard power]]*: economic or military capabilities
	- *[[soft power]]*: cultural dimensions or values that define the identity and practices of a state
		- e.g. diplomatic capacity to influence other states bilaterally or multilaterally in int'l organizational contexts
		- attracting others to your agenda in world politics
			- not just getting others to want what you want
		- becomes manifest in int'l institutions (listening to others)
		- becomes manifest in foreign policy (promoting peace and human rights)
	- combining [[hard power]] and [[soft power]]
		- essential to attaining nat'l objectives
		- essential to affecting behavior of others
	- can power of states, like money, be *fungible* and readily transferred from one issue area to another?
	- an advocate of [[multilateralism]]
		- views sustaining [[America]]n power as dependent upon:
			- strategic restraint
			- reassuring partners
			- avoiding unilateral and arrogant foreign policy
	- consistent with [[classical realism]] and [[Liberalism]]
		- Nye addresses ideas, not just the material dimensions of power
		- focuses on limits of [[balance of power]] and [[hegemonic power]] theories as applied to [[America]]
- [[Stanley Hoffman]] (1928 – 2015)
	- Nye's mentor
	- also views powers as having different dimensions
	- student of [[France]] [[Realism]], [[Raymond Aaron]] (1905 – 1983)
		- economic, military, diplomatic, and other "chessboards" to capture different dimensions affecting the way different states applied their power
- [[Kenneth Waltz]] (1924 – 2013)
	- prefers not to dissect power and multi-dimensional
	- views power as an integral concept that states apply in different ways in pursuit of their objectives in [[IR]]
#### 5.3.2 Measurement
- attempts to measure *power* is as divergent as the definitional and conceptual disputes
	- hopes to apply scientific standards to their work
- if [[power]] = capabilities:
	- measurement: military, economic, other component elements
	- if measuring through indicators of defense expenditures, gross national product:
		- problem: aggregating/adding up diverse capabilities into a common measure of power
	- how can one combine different component capabilities that use different measures (e.g. dollars spent on defense expenditures vs. overall gross national product)
	- how does one measure geographic, technological, diplomatic factors with any degree of precision?
	- how does one measure unity and strength of society?
	- how does one measure type of political regime?
	- what's the metric?
	- if capabilities are hard to measure, are relative capabilities between and among states even more difficult to specify?
- [[Kenneth Waltz]]'s approach: use measures of economic size
	- gross national product (or GDP) as an indicator of a state's overall power
	- military capabilities are dependent on availability of economic resources, technologies, political decisions to allocate resources
	- what is left out of economic and military components: [[soft power]]
		- [[soft power]] elements: cultural, diplomatic, less tangible assets
- some argue that power as a unitary concept calculated by aggregating component/relative capabilities misses the key point
	- key point being: power of a state is dependent on the issue involved
	- e.g. some states like [[Japan]] have substantial economic power, but are militarily weak
		- in a particular area, [[Japan]] is powerful
	- [[Stanley Hoffman]]'s "chessboards" or [[Nye]]'s [[soft power]] and [[hard power]] fit the observation that se of power by states varies by issue area
- some disagree with breaking power down by its component/relative capabilities:
	- ignores how all of the different parts of power are connected and influence each other
	- economic capabilities of [[Japan]] as a global trader *would* be related to its military ties with [[America]]
		- assures [[Japan]]'s freedom to engage in commerce
	- whether addressing power of [[Japan]], [[China]], [[Europe]], etc.:
		- it's hard to understand economic, military, political, other component capabilities of power if they're factors independent of each other
	- military capabilities of states are boosted/weakened by strength/relative strength of their economies
	- military capabilities depend on underlying economic and technological capabilities
