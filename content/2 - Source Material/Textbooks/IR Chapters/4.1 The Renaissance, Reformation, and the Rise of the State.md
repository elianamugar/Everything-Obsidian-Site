#### 4.1.0 The Renaissance, Reformation, and the Rise of the State
- writings of Machiavelli, Bodin, Grotius, Hobbes
	- conceptually starts with int'l order
	- draws on writings of those who preceded them
	- developed in contemporary context ideas associated with
		- power and [[balance of power]]
		- sovereignty and sovereign authority
		- int'l law
	- concepts closely tied to development of state and system of states
		- contributed to understanding transformation and legitimation of newly emerging int'l order
#### 4.1.1 Historical Context
- [[feudalism]] –> modern state is a recent Western system transformation
- 13th c. [[Europe]]: representative assemblies created
	- enhanced power of commercial/middle classes at expense of kings wanting centralization of power
	- 1250: Castile and León
	- 1285: Catalonia
	- 1255: Rhenish towns ([[Germany]])
	- 1265: lower classes into parliament (becomes [[England]]'s [[House of Commons]])
- 1302: France's first representative assembly
	- [[Third Estate]] – new, non-aristocratic middle class ([[bourgeoisie]])
		- merchants
- parliament's functions
	- appropriate revenue that kings hoped would enhance their centralization of power
	- instead became rivals to the power of kings
- 14th c.: [[Black Death]]
	- popular insurrections
	- first concrete evidence of rise of national consciousness
	- [[Henry V]] counts on support of English wars versus France ([[Hundred Years War]])
- 15th c.: decay in parliamentary institutions
	- power of king and councils increased
	- [[Louis XI]] – [[France]]
	- [[Edward IV]], [[Henry VII]] – [[England]]
	- [[Ferdinand I]], [[Isabella I]] – [[Spain]]
	- parliamentary powers generally decreased, except [[England]]
- 16th c.: conflict and resistance to monarchical state-building 
	- ordinary people coerced into surrendering crops, labor, money, land to emerging states
	- rebellions in [[England]]
		- lesser nobles, members of local assembles also resisted
	- religious wars in [[France]]
		- contest between royal privileges and regional liberties
	- common thread of resistances to emerging state
		- taxes
			- increased taxation provided monarchs with revenues that supported larger armies
				- used to defend and expand frontiers
				- used to overcome internal resistance to centralizing states
- mid-15th c. [[Renaissance]] and [[Protestant]] [[Reformation]]
	- twin cradle of modernity
	- [[Renaissance]]
		- western Europe's cultural rebirth
		- ethical and humanistic overtones
		- glorified the individual
	- [[Reformation]]
		- [[Martin Luther]]
			- personal struggle for a right relationship with God
			- important in undercutting papal authority in [[Germany]]
			- important in undercutting hope for the idea of unity in [[Christianity]]
		- Luther's belief shared with [[John Calvin]] ([[France]])
			- state/secular authority should be separate from religious authority
	- growth of religious [[pluralism]]
	- national monarchies grew in strength
	- religious differences among ruling houses increased political problems
- 1618: conflict over religion and power of emperor of [[Holy Roman Empire]] leads to civil war in Bohemia (modern-day [[Czech Republic]])
	- expands throughout [[Europe]] ([[Thirty Years War]])
	- [[Thirty Years War]] – 3 wars
		1. imperial civil war ends in 1635 ([[Peace of Prague]])
		2. western war [[Spain]] vs. [[Netherlands]] and [[France]] ([[Eighty Years War]])
		3. Baltic war [[Germany]], [[Denmark]], [[Sweden]] vs. emperor and allies
	- underlying cause of war: shifting [[balance of power]] among major states
		- [[Thucydides]]' discussion of origins of the Peloponnesian war
- outcomes of the [[Thirty Years War]]
	- 1635: [[Peace of Prague]] settles religious problem in the empire
		- unlikely that religion would be primary cause of war
		- secular leaders of Catholic countries could ignore papacy's call for militant counter-reformation policy
	- growing power of some German princes, more appropriate to speak to [[Hapsburg]]s rather than [[Holy Roman Empire]]
		- [[Hapsburg]] dynasty (Vienna, [[Austria]])
			- interested in lands to east (weakness in the rest of the empire)
			- chance for secular empire and united Europe was distant
			- also distant: pope's hope for unity under [[Christianity]]
	- new [[balance of power]] emerged, more powerful states in [[Europe]]
		- rise of [[Brandenburg]]-[[Prussia]], [[Sweden]], [[France]]
			- their involvement in military struggles directly affect the character and organization of these states
- 1660: emergence of territorial state as primary political unit in [[Europe]]
	- 1648: [[Peace of Westphalia]] ends [[Thirty Years War]]
		- solidifies trend of increasing power to modern state
			- at expense of small political units headed by princes, dukes, lords (prominent during earlier [[feudalism]])
		- initiated new diplomatic practice
			- at the end of war, ambassadors gather to negotiate a peace settlement based on the sovereign equality of victor and vanquished
	- realignment of territorial borders
	- [[sovereignty]] concept emerges
		- becomes latest principle of newly developing, state-based political order
- elaboration on [[Peace of Westphalia]]
	- king, duke, other princes could establish state religion (between [[Protestant]] and [[Roman Catholicism]])
	- provided practical/concrete basis for establishing idea of [[sovereignty]]
		- part of intellectual discourse in previous century
		- ideas preceded facts in the social construction of [[sovereignty]] and the sovereign state
- rise of the state
	- political, diplomatic, military, and **economic** spheres
- economic realm
	- economic developments critical in victory of state system over other contenders
	- 17th c.: large trading companies
		- earlier years, trading companies associated with particiular families
		- now monarchs charter companies in the name of the state
			- [[East India Company]] (1600), [[Dutch East India Company]] (1602), [[Hamburg Company]] (1611)
		- state/monarch-backed firms increase in power
			- private city-based firms and trading associations decline
				- 1629: north [[Germany]] cities maintained importance as separate urban centers (Lübeck, Hamburg, Bremen – members of [[Hanseatic League]])
				- leaders of large territorial states ([[England]], [[France]]) worked to free themselves from dependence on foreign interests (Florentine and German banking houses)
					- family-based/small firms unable to provide amount of capital monarches required to carry out their wars
					- commercial and industrial firms preferred more secure, domestic capital sources
						- leads to rise of national banks
- development of [[mercantilism]]
	- development of state trading companies and banks
	- 17th c.: became the dominant economic doctrine 
	- [[mercantilism]] – the state should play a major role in the economy, seeking to accumulate domestic capital by running continual trade surpluses in relation to other states
	- no lofty moral aim or for benefit of private entrepreneurs
		- ultimate objective: provide resources to be used for [[war]], conquest, etc.
	- regulating and protecting commerce
		- [[authoritarian]] state bureaucracies emerge
		- contrasts with primary economic units of late [[Middle Ages]] (autonomous and self-regulating guilds)
	- national bureaucracies view competition in [[zero-sum]] terms
		- whatever one state gained came at the expense of another
- all prior developments led to rise of autonomous, belligerent state system
	- the view of [[Machiavelli]] and [[Hobbes]]
	- developments to counteract increasingly powerful states as core units
		- transnational character ([[Liberalism]])
			- motivation to commerce
				- discovery of [[America]] and new routes to the [[Indies]]
			- common intellectual background
				- [[Renaissance]]
			- sympathy of co-religionists in different states transcending national borders
			- common revulsion with armed conflicts
				- horrifying cost of religious wars
	- "the separate state could never be accepted as the final and perfect form of human association, and that in the modern world, as in the medieval world, it would be necessary to recognize the existence of a wider unity." (emergence of [[universalism]])
- emergence of [[universalism]]
	- contributes to development of int'l law
	- contributes to concept of external [[sovereignty]]
		- claimed right of all states to be independent or autonomous in their IR with other states
	- [[Grotius]] and others abandon [[medieval]] ideal of a world-state and accept existence of modern, secular, sovereign state
		- they deny absolute separateness of these states and the extreme version of int'l [[anarchy]]
			- identified in [[Leviathan by Thomas Hobbes]] ([[Hobbes]])
	- idea of "community"/int'l society increasingly becomes associated with [[Liberalism]] with the modern state system
- application of [[Grotius]]: there is an int'l "society" of states with commonly accepted rules/laws to guide their conduct
	- not just relations based on power and [[balance of power]] ([[Machiavelli]] and [[Hobbes]]'s belief)
	- core to [[The English School]] thinking
		- see int'l politics moving toward acceptance of universal moral norms/principles of right conduct ([[Kant]])
		- int'l society –> "world" society
		- these members admit we're not there yet
#### 4.1.2 Machiavelli
- Niccolò [[Machiavelli]] (1583 – 1645)
	- first "modern" political theorist
		- emphasis on "what is" as opposed to "what should be"
	- empirical, non-utopian view
		- reference to classical Greco-Roman and [[medieval]] philosophizing
			- [[Machiavelli]]: "Many have dreamed up republics and principalities which have never in truth been known to exist; the gulf between how one should live and how one does live is so wide that one who neglects what is actually done for what should be done learns the way to self-destruction rather than self-preservation."
	- transitional figure between [[medieval]] and modern world
		- affection for Italian city-state and admiration of ancient [[Roman Republic]] as forms of gov't
- [[Machiavelli]]'s impact
	- purpose of politics is not to make men virtuous
	- purpose of the state is not to pursue some ethical, religious, metaphysical end (ancient [[Greek]] and [[medieval]] writers)
	- [[Politics]] is the means to pursue and enhance the internal and external security of the state
- [[Machiavelli]]'s context
	- born in Florence, [[Italy]] to an old Florentine family
	- 1498: execution of Dominican priest, [[Savonarola]]
		- Machiavelli appointed to gov't administrative post in Florentine republic
	- engaged in diplomatic missions
		- opportunity to observe domestic politics closely
	- 1512: [[Medici]] family restored to power and republic ended
		- briefly put Machiavelli in prison and tortured him to identify republican connections
		- saw him as an enemy to newly restored principality
	- Machiavelli's release from imprisonment
		- Medici allows him to live in exile south of Florence
		- attempts to reenter politics
		- leisure time, writes and analyzes world around him
	- political events during the last 30 years of [[Machiavelli]]'s life
		- devastation of Italy by invasions from [[France]] and [[Spain]]
		- gov'ts collapsed
		- civil chaos spread
		- more powerful city-states/alliances devoured smaller ones
	- [[Machiavelli]] acknowledges Lorenzo de [[Medici]]'s reign as golden age of political stability
- [[The Prince by Niccolo Machiavelli]]
	- focuses on how the unification of the Italian city-states would restore civil order and security throughout Italy
	- unity among Italian city-states would end conflict among them AND keep foreign powers from intervening in Italian affairs
- 1520: Machiavelli readmitted to [[Medici]]s
	- commissioned by Cardinal Medici (Pope [[Clement VII]]) to write [[Florentine Histories by Niccolo Machiavelli]]
- 1525: Machiavelli finishes *Florentine Histories*
- 1527: Machiavelli dies
	- never regains political position he coveted
- Machiavelli addresses how to bring the state into existence and secure its independence
	- turns to history to answer these questions
	- humanist tradition of his age glorified classical past
		- Machiavelli turns to Roman history
	- tries to understand dismal condition of 16th c. [[Italy]]
		- interested to learn what about [[Rome]]'s [[Republic]] that allowed it to succeed for as long as it did
	- in-depth analysis and republican sentiments in [[The Discourses by Niccolo Machiavelli]]
		- directly reflects influence of [[Livy]], [[Polybius]], [[Plutarch]], [[Tacitus]]
#### 4.1.3 Republics and Security
- [[Machiavelli]] examines development of [[Rome]]'s constitution
	- following [[Aristotle]]: contrasts ideal forms [[principality]], [[aristocracy]], and [[democracy]] with the corresponding degenerate forms [[tyranny]], [[oligarchy]], [[anarchy]]
		- ideal forms: praiseworthy, but difficult to maintain and tend to slip into degenerative forms
	- adopts [[Polybius]]'s cyclical theory of gov't change
		- [[Machiavelli]]: as a result of domestic turmoil, the transition from one form of gov't to another may result in the state falling prey to a neighboring power
			- to avoid such dangers and defects of each ideal form of gov't, legislators should choose a form of gov't that combines the best aspects of [[principality]], [[aristocracy]], [[democracy]]
- [[Machiavelli]] argues a [[Republic]] (mixed form of gov't) is most stable
	- historical example ([[Plutarch]]): Lycurgus of [[Sparta]] assigned kings, aristocracy, and populace each its own function – gov't lasts more than 800 years
	- importance of free citizenry stems from ability to restrain abuse of power by governing class
	- ability of rulers to mobilize popular support makes republic better at defending state's security
	- key virtue of republic: **flexibility** ("The Need of Adaptation to Environment")
		- advantage of republican flexibility
			- times of crisis, dictatorial powers can be temporarily granted to authorities
				- [[Machiavelli]]: slow working of republican institutions poses a potential threat to security of state as it takes time to reconcile diverse views
				- such powers need to be granted by institutions of the republic, not unilaterally seized by dictator
	- [[Machiavelli]]: it's possible for dictators to work for common good
		- says a state can be radically transformed by one person
		- dictatorial powers may be required at certain times, so is active and engaged citizenry
	- kingdom or republic should be regulated by good laws, not the whim of prince or public
	- [[Machiavelli]]'s discussion of confederations of states
		- crises may require dictatorial powers as temporary measure
		- other times, republics being slower to act is beneficial for certain issues
			- whether or not to break a treaty
			- dissolve a confederation
		- claims republics abide by their agreements better than princes ([[Kant]] would agree)
			- princes tend to break treaties for small advantages
			- republics view longer-term security interests
	- [[Machiavelli]]'s emphasis on virtues of domestic political stability
		- state-societal level of analysis
		- echoes importance given to political unity ([[Aristotle]], [[Polybius]], [[Cicero]], [[Livy]])
		- dynamism to mixed government belief
			- argues that discord among elements of a republic can strengthen a state
	- advantages at certain times to conflict among classes
		- [[Machiavelli]]: republican institutions of Roman constitution and liberty arose from conflict
			- public tumult makes citizenry see it's an effective element of the political system
- [[The Discourses by Niccolo Machiavelli]]
	- it's not "the well-being of individuals that makes cities great, but the well-being of the community"
	- if [[tyranny]] replaces [[Republic]], at the minimum, the state stops growing power and wealth
	- theory of [[republican imperialism]] (case study [[Rome]])
	- important factors
		1. good laws and good arms
			- [[Machiavelli]]: aside from a good constitution, political stability, power, and independence of [[Rome]] derives from leadership, religion, citizen army, and arms (also in [[The Prince by Niccolo Machiavelli]])
			- Roman leaders were successful because of talent combining intelligence and will, thought and action
				- implementing political ideas ([[Machiavelli]]'s *[[virtù]]*) – enlightened boldness
			- if one weak princes follows another, the kingdom will not survive
				- in early [[Rome]], power vested in elected consuls, unending succession of competent rulers were assured
			- [[voluntarism]]: individual leaders can make a difference in politics (message in [[The Prince by Niccolo Machiavelli]] – practical handbook on politics dedicated to ruler of Florence)
		2. religion a critical factor binding people together
			- helps unify people, ruler doesn't have to rely on self-defeating policy of coercion against his own citizens
			- [[pagan]] religions with blood sacrifices and rituals help incite warriors to be fearless in war
				- [[Christianity]] "has glorified humble and contemplative men, rather than men of action"
				- decline of [[Rome]]'s pre-Christian [[pagan]] religion associated with decay of community and empire
					- attempted rebuttal by [[Augustine]] in [[City of God by Augustine]]
			- reinforces, not dominates, secular order
			- force for unity
			- [[Machiavelli]] believes Church of Rome did more harm than good ("[[Italy]] has lost all devotion and all religion", "the church has kept, and keeps, [[Italy]] divided")
			- Machiavelli wasn't anti-religious or anti-church
				- objected to religious authorities whose selfish/misguided policies resulted in undermining the possibilities of attaining Italian unity
		3. strong republic relied on patriotic citizen army
			- willingness of citizens to die for republic was sign of essential unity and strength
			- mercenary armies (prominent in [[Italy]] at his time) couldn't be trusted
			- rulers were afraid to arm poorer citizens
				- [[Machiavelli]] argues it's more dangerous to trust fate of gov't to mercenaries ("disunited, thirsty for power, undisciplined, disloyal")
			- state placing trust in troops of an ally is misguided
		4. necessary for state to be well armed to deter and defend itself from outside threats
			- good laws aid domestic stability
			- well-armed states provide defense against enemies AND expand range of foreign policy choices for leaders
##### 4.1.3.1 Republics and Political Decay
- [[Machiavelli]] discusses two topics regarding how the Roman polity and society declined, despite the virtues he discussed
	- [[Machiavelli]]'s view of human nature
		- accepts [[Christianity]]'s tradition of human beings being sinful and corrupt beings
		- acknowledges there can be moral improvement, but only achieved by a combination of good constitution, leadership, religion, citizen army, and arms
	- [[Machiavelli]]'s cyclical view of institutions
		- due to the evilness of humans, whatever human institutions exist, they are destined to decay
			- reflects classical heritage ([[Polybius]])
			- Machiavelli agrees [[Fortuna]] (chance) plays a major role in determining how long constructive political institutions last ([[determinism]])
				- Machiavelli's offset to determinism: prince needs to exercise *[[virtù]]* (enlightened boldness) when dealing with uncertainties
			- evidence of [[voluntarism]] (advocacy of human will to control as much as one can)
				- individuals can and do matter even if they cannot control everything
- [[Machiavelli]]: social and political good is defined by interests of the community/common good
	- the individual: one pursues good if one acts in the interest of the community
	- [[Christianity]]'s emphasis on serving general interests of community
		- [[Augustine]] and [[Thomas Aquinas]] emphasize a higher [[divine law]]
			- [[Augustine]]: two kingdoms, secular (City of Man), inferior to sacred (City of God)
				- Machiavelli rejects this: secular politics is central to his political thought
		- [[Machiavelli]] restricts his argument to level of secular community (departing from Christian thought)
- [[Machiavelli]]: due to cyclical nature of human institutions, there will be good and bad times for a political community
	- in bad times, effective leader needs to act in accordance with prevailing morality
		- a leader "should not deviate from what is good, if that is possible, but he should know how to take actions that cause harm, if that is necessary"
		- prince should focus on what's realistic and possible, not just what's desirable
		- effective leadership can contribute to public order and maintenance of city-state's independence or unification of [[Italy]]
- "[[Machiavellianism]]"'s negative connotations
	- notion of using as one's standards the prevailing morality of an age
	- last chapters of [[The Prince by Niccolo Machiavelli]] appeal to national sentiment of all Italians, hoping for unity from [[Medici]], end civil chaos, protecting Italy from foreign invasions
- [[Machiavelli]]'s contributions
	- emphasizing strong leader who can use common identity to strengthen bonds of a political community
	- justifies centralization of power in modern state
	- ideal community was the SMALL [[Roman Republic]]
	- realized the city-state system in [[Italy]] was becoming outdated when decisive political power in [[Europe]] was being wielded in [[France]], [[Spain]], etc. nearing modern state power
		- [[Italy]] needed to be unified
##### 4.1.3.2 Realism
- [[Machiavelli]]'s contribution to [[Realism]]
	1. leader's primary concern should be security and independence of state
		- [[The Prince by Niccolo Machiavelli]]'s definition of state: "All the states, all the dominions under whose authority men have lived in the past and live now, have been and are either republics or principalities"
			- 3 parts:
				1. dominion (territory)
				2. the right of authority to command
				3. people located on the territory who obey and view the commands (laws, orders, decrees)
		- prince should not "flinch from being blamed for vices that are necessary for safeguarding the state"
			- required to be alert to 2 dangers:
				1. internal subversion from his subjects
					- keep the people satisfied
				2. external aggression by foreign powers
					- defense lies in being well-armed and having good allies
		- independence: "princes should do their utmost to escape being at the mercy of others"
	2. leader blessed with [[virtù]]/enlightened boldness can rationally plan and institute policies that enhance security and independence of state
		- [[voluntarism]]
		- some [[determinism]] with [[Fortuna]]: "So as not to rule out free will, I believe that it is probably true that fortune is the arbiter of half the things we do, leaving the other half or so controlled by ourselves."
	3. conduct of relations among states
		- advice in [[The Art of War by Niccolo Machiavelli]] and [[The Discourses by Niccolo Machiavelli]]
		- topics:
			- 3 methods of expansion (leagues, alliances, hegemonies)
			- basic causes of war (provoked and unintended)
			- advantages and disadvantages of offensive and defensive strategies
			- dangers of following a neutrality policy
- Machiavelli believes permanent/perpetual peace is a dangerous illusion
	- any leader who operates under assumption risks country's loss of liberty or existence (worse than war)
	- speaks of "necessary" wars to keep what's worse than war at bay ([[just war doctrine]])
		- end of [[The Prince by Niccolo Machiavelli]], quotes [[Livy]]: "Because a necessary war is a just war and where there is hope only in arms, those arms are holy."
	- arms can be a deterrent, but is a critical role for defense
- Machiavelli a foremost contributor of [[Realism]] in IR
	- foremost observer/analyst of emerging modern state
	- similar to [[Thucydides]]: not just cynical power politics, wrestles with moral and ethical concerns
	- his recognition of need for leaders to cope continually and creatively with political change and flux
#### 4.1.4 Thomas More
- [[Thomas More]] (1478 – 1535)
	- political theorist, not often cited by IR scholars
	- wrote at the same time as [[Machiavelli]]
	- associated with [[socialist idealism]] ([[Utopia (1516) by Thomas More]])
- similarities between [[Thomas More]] and [[Machiavelli]]
	- agree on need for kings of more modern states to receive advice from experienced individuals to govern effectively and justly
		- [[Machiavelli]] served republic of Florence
		- [[Thomas More]] appointed privy councilor to [[Henry VIII]] –> lord chancellor
	- utilize contemporary events to illustrate major points
	- observe national state emerging as dominant political force in [[Europe]]
		- state power increasingly used arbitrarily, unchecked by moral understandings in philosophical/religious teachings
	- share ideal of a smaller social, economic, political unit
		- [[Machiavelli]]: [[Roman Republic]]
		- [[Thomas More]]: critic of enclosure movement destroying pastoral communities in [[England]] through building of fences to contain livestock
			- concerned growing power of state was at expense of [[pluralism]] of [[medieval]] life
			- agrees with [[Plato]] and [[Aristotle]]'s city-state
			- concerned with growing power of state at the expense of more localized lifestyle that prevailed in the [[Middle Ages]]
- [[Utopia (1516) by Thomas More]]
	- Book 1 aware of problems in providing advice to princes
		- Raphael returns to Europe from "Utopia" and is asked by More to enter public service
		- Raphael believes his unconventional views and straight talking wouldn't be appreciated by kings and cause jealousy among royal advisers
			- royal advisers follow the maxim: "We'll never get human behavior in line with Christian ethics, so let's adapt Christian ethics to human behavior."
			- Raphael doesn't accept this cynical approach: "I can't see what good they've done. They've merely enabled people to sin with a clear conscience."
			- More: an advisor "must go to work indirectly. You must handle everything as tactfully as you can."
		- More's ambivalence towards public service
			- addresses pitfalls and problems of advising prince
			- raises issue of relation between politics and morality
- [[Thomas More]] did not accept the [[Protestant]] [[Reformation]]
	- sent to death rather than acceded to absolutist claims to power by [[Henry VIII]]
- Machiavelli's reluctant acceptance of need for strong national Italian state to repel foreign aggressor helped lay intellectual groundwork for later justifications of modern state system
#### 4.1.5 Botero and "Reason of State"
- [[raison d'état]] – reason of state, development of legitimating theory of statecraft
	- justification for states to invoke for conduct
	- [[Friedrich Meineckie]] (historian): [[raison d'état]] is the fundamental principle of national conduct, the State's first Law of Motion. It tells the statesman what he must do to preserve the health and strength of the State.... For each State at each particular moment there exists one ideal course of action, one ideal *raison d'état*. The statesman in power tries hard to discern this course, and so too does the historian surveying the past in retrospect.
	- [[Realism]] – state as key actor with policy makers rationally calculating the best course of action for the state to preserve and strengthen security of state
	- can justify more secular pursuits
		- over time, power of state became justified
		- state is a rational tool for achievement of expressly political as opposed to political-religious/moral ends
	- centralized state acquires supreme authority and power to defend realm and carry out other duties
		- instead of simply being one political actor among various types of political units claiming autonomy
	- Cardinal [[Richelieu]] in [[France]]: cult of state reaches its climax
		- *État* (with capital e) wraps the state in a cloak of mystery
- [[Giovanni Botero]] (1540 – 1617)
	- counter-reformation Jesuit
	- tries to find way to aid papacy in pursuit of its goals
		- requires the church to seek secular support
	- hoped to mobilize resources of the state in pursuit of papal goals
	- [[Della Ragione di Stato (1589) by Giovanni Botero]]
		- [[raison d'état]] – *ragione di stato*
- writers opposed justification for increased state power
	- makes the case for maintenance of powers of king, nobility, and clergy (separate "estates" in [[medieval]] order)
		- king was one estate among several
		- sense of unity among estates was based on adopting [[Christianity]] as a faith
	- [[medieval]] times: greater fluidity of relations compared to rise of states
		- separate estates carry on negotiations with foreign princes/estates
- [[Thirty Years War]] (1618 – 1648)
	- turning point, victory of king's "state" over estates 
		- estates were a hindrance to king who needed to marshal resources quickly to prosecute a war effectively
- degree of king's power and authority varied from state to state
	- less in [[England]] due to parliament's assertion of supremacy (after beheading of King [[Charles I]] in 1649)
	- more in [[France]] due to legislature ([[Estates-General]]) not meeting (1614 – 1789)
#### 4.1.6 Hobbes
- Thomas [[Hobbes]] (1588 – 1679)
	- 17th c. writer most closely associated with [[raison d'état]] thinking
		- also associated with elevation of secular state to supreme status
	- similar to [[Thucydides]] and [[Machiavelli]], contributes to political theory and [[Realism]] perspective on IR
	- translated [[Thucydides]]'s work into English
	- placed political philosophy on a scientific and secular basis
	- became one of the foremost theorists on the state and nature of power
	- associated with Cavendish family, prominent aristocratic family
	- met with [[Francis Bacon]], René [[Descartes]], and [[Galileo]] Galilei
	- spent most time in Paris, [[France]]
		- exposed to diverse philosophical and scientific developments
- 1640: Hobbes flees to Paris due to association with aristocracy and royalist leanings
- 1646 – 1648: Hobbes tutors young King [[Charles II]] (during princehood)
- 1649: King [[Charles I]] is beheaded during the [[English Civil War]] between [[Stuart]] monarchy and [[Puritan]] forces under [[Oliver Cromwell]]
	-  [[England]]'s parliament asserts authority
- 1651: Hobbes returns to [[England]]
	- pledges allegiance to newly established commonwealth
		- established after [[Puritan]]s defeat king's forces
	- remains in England
- 1679: Hobbes dies
- [[Leviathan by Thomas Hobbes]]
	- published before his return to [[England]]
	- like [[Thucydides]] and [[Machiavelli]]
		- [[Thucydides]]: experienced Peloponnesian wars
		- [[Machiavelli]]: experienced Italian wars
		- [[Hobbes]]: experienced [[English Civil War]]
	- first modern work on political theory published in [[England]]
- Hobbes concerned with domestic rule; relationship between ruler and citizens
	- [[Leviathan by Thomas Hobbes]] devoted to this topic
- Hobbes' impact to [[Realism]] in IR
	- the "state of nature"
		- comparable to [[Anarchic]] int'l system: world without central authority as if it were like the state of nature that Hobbes identifies as a state of war
	- natural law ([[jus naturale]])
		- does not emphasize restraints placed upon rulers
		- emphasizes how [[jus naturale]] enables absolute power residing in hands of ruler (early forms of [[Divine Right of Kings]])
		- results in IR view: [[sovereignty]] mainly in terms of internal aspects, much less so in relation to other sovereign states
- Hobbes believes security and order *in* state are of highest importance (like [[Machiavelli]])
	- leaves little hope for int'l cooperation or mitigation of the effects of the int'l system's [[Anarchic]] structure
##### 4.1.6.1 State of Nature
- [[Hobbes]]'s state of nature is his attempt to imagine what the world would be like without governmental authority
	- imagine a society without any governmental structure
	- critical starting point: question of human nature
		- how one answers this question dramatically affects one's prescriptions concerning the most appropriate type of political system
	- humans are ruled by their passions
		- some are physically stronger than others
		- "the weakest has strength enough to kill the strongest, either by secret machination, or by confederacy with others"
	- basic equality between weak an strong
		- hope of attaining desired ends
		- two individuals cannot enjoy the same thing equally
		- conflict results
			- [[Hobbes]]: "During the time men live without a common power to keep them all in awe, they are in that condition which is called war; and such a war, as is of every man, against every man." #quote 
				- does NOT suggest such a state of nature has constant fighting
				- war still represents a constant inclination
	- devastating consequences of constant war as state of nature
		- uncertainty over one's security means no industry, culture, trading, cumulative knowledge, arts, letters, society, and
			- "continual fear, and danger of violent death; and the life of man, solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short"
		- no right or wrong
			- [[Hobbes]]: "where there is no common power, there is now law: where no law, no injustice. Force, and fraud, are in war the two cardinal virtues" #quote 
				- civil society with supreme authority to regulate disputes and enforce contracts only leads to the existence of justice
- Hobbes's state of nature comparable to int'l system
	- humans stand alone –> states strive to maintain independence
	- individuals predisposed toward war –> int'l system marked by constant tension and possibility of conflict
	- without social structure, there would be no culture, governmental/religious institutions, family, and other human associations we take for granted
- Hobbes's conclusion
	- negative "dog-eat-dog" perspective
		- state of war – everyone against everyone, life of one person is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short"
	- domestic remedy: create a sovereign (monarch or parliament)
		- sovereign has the authority to provide security and order required for humans
	- if governments don't exist, we have to create them
- int'l system reflects condition like Hobbes's fabricated "state of nature"
	- there's no common power/centralized authority to enforce order
		- condition of [[anarchy]]: a system whose structure encourages suspicion and distrust among sovereigns
			- suspicion and distrust because they reflect human nature unconstrained by any common power
##### 4.1.6.2 Natural Law
- earlier writers encourage greater civility relations among humans and secular authorities by providing common and restricting standards of behavior
	- civil/manmade law is subordinate to higher natural/divine laws
		- Hobbes rejects this view
			- sees revolutionary implications that would allow citizens to use [[jus naturale]] as justification to overthrow monarch
			- Hobbes prefers [[monarchy]]
			- crux of his analysis doesn't rely on religious justification
- Hobbes key unit of analysis **individual**
	- laws of nature are designed to help individuals survive
	- not based on religion
	- individual's most basic right is **self-preservation**
		- "preserving his life against his enemies"
- [[Hobbes]]: since anyone can kill anyone in the state of nature, most basic human passion is fear of death
	- laws of nature
		1. individuals should seek peace to avoid constantly being afraid of death
		2. individuals should seek peace through agreement to escape the state of nature
		3. justice can't reach full fruition outside an organized commonwealth transcended into the state of nature
			- justice in terms of individuals fulfilling their covenants
			- if each person fulfills their covenants, chances of escaping violent death are enhanced
			- [[Hobbes]]: "Therefore before the names of just, and unjust can have place, there must be some coercive power, to compel men equally to the performance of their covenants, by the terror of some punishment, greater than the benefit they expect by the breach of their covenant." #quote 
- Hobbes argues [[Leviathan]] (supreme sovereign power) is required to assure that individuals will fulfill their obligations
	- [[Leviathan]] is a scriptural reference to a fearsome, powerful entity of monstrous proportions
		- charged with making sure the parties fulfill all aspects of the agreement
- Hobbes's unitary conception of the state
	- all power may be conferred "upon one man, or upon one assembly of men, that may reduce all their wills, by plurality of voices, unto one will"
	- preference for monarchy, but state can be republic or monarchy
- Hobbes's focus on disorder and lack of security
	- due to absence of sovereign
	- people (coming out of "state of nature") covenant with a sovereign (monarch or parliamentary assembly) to maintain order and provide security
		- democratic remedy (revolutionary idea at the time)
		- sovereign's authority established by the people, not God's will (aka not [[Divine Right of Kings]] that English kings relied on)
		- only the people have the right to remove the sovereign's right to rule
			- if sovereign fails to provide secure order in which to live, that is a breach of covenant and calls into question legitimacy of sovereign's authority
- Hobbes's views on factions
	- unjust: "contrary to the peace and safety of the people, and a taking of the sword out of the hand of the sovereign" #quote 
	- traditional conception of natural law
		- laws of nature don't result in restraints placed upon ruler
		- laws of nature devolve absolute power on ruler so individuals who take part in the covenant can enjoy benefits (security) that come from escaping the "state of nature"
		- "Covenants, without the sword, are but words." #quote 
- [[medieval]] vision of community –> major justification for centralizing power in modern state
	- Hobbes: [[Leviathan]] provides citizens internal peace AND peace against enemies abroad
		- commonwealths (like individuals) have the right to do *anything* to enhance their security
		- alliances *may* happen, but Hobbes doesn't trust alliances to last long
		- Hobbes doesn't suggest ethical or moral considerations should play a role in conducting relations
- Hobbes never mentions int'l law
	- also doesn't suggest [[anarchy]] of interstate relations can be overcome through a worldwide [[Leviathan]] to provide peace and security
	- doesn't propose world government as a means to end int'l [[anarchy]]
	- suggests state of nature for individuals differs from conditions faced by states
		- individuals sees logic in needing to transcend [[anarchy]]
		- states do not
- Hobbes's provides basic elements of [[Realism]] image (in most extreme form)
	- state is supreme political organization
	- unity enforced by [[Leviathan]] and should not allow factions
		- charged with dealing with world beyond kingdom's borders
	- rational for individuals to escape "state of nature"
	- rational for individual [[Leviathan]]s (states) to guard against one another
	- [[self-help system]] of states
		- not one which sovereign maintains int'l peace and security
#### 4.1.7 Sovereignty
- critical concepts that helped legitimize idea of an int'l system of states
	- [[raison d'état]]
	- [[sovereignty]]
		- social construction developed philosophically over time in response to states needing to establish their authority and exercise power
		- right of state to exercise exclusive jurisdiction over its territory (domestic)
		- right of state to be autonomous in the conduct of its foreign affairs (int'l)
- sovereign states when coerced/invaded by others
	- not able to exercise those rights as a matter of fact (*de facto*)
	- does not alter their right to exercise their rights as a matter of law (*de jure*), perhaps later when invaders have withdrawn
- case study: 1940, [[USSR]] invades [[Estonia]], [[Latvia]], [[Lithuania]] ([[Eastern Europe]], the [[Baltic]]s)
	- governments representing sovereignty of invaded states operated abroad in exile
		- waiting to reassume political control of homelands
	- *de facto* control with [[USSR]] until end of [[Cold War]]
	- after Cold War, control returned to gov'ts with *de jure* right to rule
- sovereignty as a basis of state authority
	- constitutional justification for state's exercise of power
	- [[Europe]]'s history: where should supreme power reside in the political community?
	- [[F. H. Hinsley]]: concept of internal [[sovereignty]]
		- "an enforced compromise between those who claimed it lay with the ruler and those who claimed it lay with the ruled. It is the justification of absolute authority that can arise and exist only when a final power is considered necessary in a body politic, and only when the body politic and its government are considered necessary to each other." #quote 
			- conditions are unmet until rise of territorially based state where *ruler* and *ruled*, *state* and *society* are differentiated
			- [[Ancient World]] [[Greek]]s did not conceptualize [[sovereignty]] because of the *polis*, which didn't distinguish between community and state
				- [[Greek]] city-states were communities without distinctive state forms
				- [[Ancient World]] empires were lords of many communities and varied people
					- impact of their forms of gov't upon communities under their rule were negligible
			- [[Rome]] and [[Byzantine]] successors had the idea of internal [[sovereignty]]
				- final authority resting with emperor
				- desire to overcome social and political chaos
				- results in [[tyranny]]
				- decay of [[Roman Empire]] leads to fading of Roman idea of supreme central authority
			- [[medieval]] [[Europe]] had many overlapping authorities by many actors/institutions
				- final authority wielded by pope or emperor was not universally acknowledged, but a pervasive influence
				- idea that [[Europe]] was a single community united under [[Christianity]]
					- this is why idea of internal [[sovereignty]] didn't take hold until 16th c.
				- universal community, in reality, was an inadequate basis for rule by a sacred or secular authority
- at regional level, single rulership needed to be combined with a true sense of community for the idea of internal [[sovereignty]] to take root
	- social disorder provided the requirement to move away from [[pluralism]] of political authority in [[medieval]] [[Europe]]
#### 4.1.8 Bodin
- [[Jean Bodin]] (1530 – 1596)
	- born in [[France]], middle class
	- studies law in Toulouse
		- [[Roman Catholicism]]-[[Protestant]] conflict threatens France's progress toward unity
			- [[fanaticism]] on both sides made reconciliation difficult
		- associated with *Politiques*, group dedicated to halting fanaticism
			- placed state and nation (secular) above the Church (sacred)
			- recognized political virtues of religious tolerance
	- 1576: publishes [[Six Books on the State (1576) by Jean Bodin]]
	- [[sovereignty]] concept picks up in France
		- development of judicial, administrative, legislative institutions
		- sentiment of [[nationalism]] evident
			- 13th c.: struggle between King [[Philip IV]] and Pope [[Boniface VIII]]
- [[Six Books on the State (1576) by Jean Bodin]]
	- attempts to find a basis where harmony can be restored in [[France]]'s political community
	- agrees with [[Machiavelli]] and [[Hobbes]]: some centralized authority wielding unlimited power is required to achieve harmony
	- rejects [[Machiavelli]]'s argument that the solution to harmony requires freeing the ruler from all religious limitations and restraints based on custom
		- Bodin: body politic should compromise *ruler* and *ruled* 
			- similar to [[Machiavelli]]'s definition of "state" in first line of [[The Prince by Niccolo Machiavelli]]
		- Bodin: declares ruler must respect moral and legal rules
	- desire of political community to escape chaos is not enough
		- power has to be vested in a sovereign (the state)
- [[Bodin]]'s definition of [[sovereignty]]
	- "the absolute and perpetual power of the state, that is, the greatest power to command" #quote 
	- important distinction between "gov't" and "state"
		- gov't exercises sovereign functions for a period of time
			-  gov'ts headed by diverse individuals and diff types of gov'ts come and go
		- [[sovereignty]] is **unlimited and perpetual**
			- continues as long as state exists
- "absolute" aspect of [[sovereignty]]
	- there is no legal authority above the state
	- [[Bodin]]: "Only he is absolutely sovereign who, after God, acknowledges no one greater than himself."
		- mention of God: Bodin still holds some [[medieval]] sentiment that sovereign king shouldn't violate [[divine law]] of God and nature
			- a king must keep whatever agreements he has made with his subjects
- Bodin primarily focuses on internal sovereignty, but begins discussion on implications of external [[sovereignty]]
	- external threats to the sovereignty of state
		1. empire
		2. claims of universal church
	- Bodin argues against external threats to interfere in ecclesiastical and secular affairs of state
	- relations to other states, Bodin views sovereignty as equal legal status of states
	- political power varies in different states
		- some states are *de facto* politically and militarily dependent on others
		- NO state is legally subject to *de jure* authority of any other state
- internal sovereignty
	- final and absolute authority within state and society
	- one supreme authority exists in the domestic realm
	- aids domestic unity
- external sovereignty
	- rejects idea of one final, absolute int'l authority
	- each state *de jure*is equally sovereign with no other state having the right to tell it what to do int'lly or how to handle domestic affairs
	- reinforces state independence/autonomy in relation to other states
- sovereignty
	- applied to relations among states
	- no supreme authority exists over and above a collection of states
	- undermines [[medieval]] and universalistic claims of the papacy and empire
#### 4.1.9 External Sovereignty
- "If it took hundreds of years for the idea of internal sovereignty to take root, the external application of sovereignty involved an even longer and more tortuous process." #quote 
- [[constructivism]] views sovereignty as a concept that emerged and evolved over centuries
	- Roman law didn't help since it reflected Rome's rapid transformation from tribal city –> [[Roman Republic]] –> [[Roman Empire]]
	- authority of Roman emperor viewed in terms of internal manifestations within empire (not int'l/inter-empire relations)
- until Bodin, kings might've claimed they were *de jure* independent of pope/emperor in terms of right to govern their communities as they saw fit without external interference
	- reason: despite differences, these states and leaders are still part of a single united community – [[Christianity]]
- a century after Bodin: successful application of [[sovereignty]] to interstate relations
	- problem of developing int'l sovereignty: similar to earlier problem of developing internal sovereignty
		- requires balance between ruler's desire to be superior to manmade laws and ruler's willingness to be subjected to ethical premises and political limits demanded by the *ruled*
	- int'l application of sovereignty couldn't be applied "until the notion of the sovereign power of the individual state had been reconciled in some way with the ethical premises and the practical needs of an international community of states." #quote 
		- personal note: is the International Court of Justice the only accountability for international sovereignty? All I can think about is the UN and ICJ at the moment...
- end of 17th c.: four basic schools of thought on IR
	- all fail to recognize the need for reconciliation of the above quote, EXCEPT ONE
	1. some conservative writers were part of a pluralist-unity-despite-diversity school of thought
		- individuals who cling to [[medieval]] notion of [[Europe]]'s int'l system as a single society
			- divine and natural law imposed common rights and duties on all states ([[Thomas More]], [[Thomas Aquinas]])
		- [[Francisco de Vitoria]] (1480 – 1546)
			- Spaniard, Dominican professor of theology
			- utilizes theological and [[jus naturale]] reasoning in discussions of [[just war theory]]
			- clerical writers argue for power of pope over emperor and kings
				- emperor should maintain power over local rulers in the name of unity under [[Christianity]]
			- secular-minded writers supported unified European order
				- fear of growing [[anarchy]] of interstate relations
				- distrust of [[Machiavellianism]] and [[raison d'état]] that encouraged [[anarchy]]
	2. legal positivists
		- emphasized autonomy of state
		- argue int'l law could and should exist
		- [[Francisco Suarez]] (1548 – 1617), Jesuit from [[Spain]]
		- [[Alberico Gentili]] (1552 – 1608), lawyer from [[Italy]]
		- diplomatic practices of sovereign states were customary basis for int'l law
		- extent of community of sovereign states' existence
			- found in states being tied together by a mutually agreed upon body of law
	3. naturalists
		- argue int'l political society can't be created through introduction of [[Positivism]] (human-constructed) int'l law
		- [[Samuel Pufendorf]] (1632 – 1694) from [[Germany]]
			- optimistic: through experience and reason, laws of nature could be known and established as legally binding principles
			- had no illusions concerning likelihood of a universal society
				- at best, a society of sovereign states could be established
		- restraints involving relations among states would derive from laws of nature that predate the historical state
		- other writers were more pessimistic
			- [[Benedict Spinoza]] (1632 –1677) from [[Netherlands]] and [[Jewish]]
				- "the big fishes devour the little fishes by natural right" #quote 
			- [[Hobbes]]: int'l state of nature is really a state of war
				- neither int'l law nor ethical restraints are mentioned in his works relating to int'l politics
	4. [[Bodin]]-[[Grotius]] perspective
		- sees the need to reconcile internal sovereignty of the autonomous state with the notion of an int'l community
		- paved the way for modern conception of external sovereignty
#### 4.1.10 Grotius
- Hugo [[Grotius]] (1583 – 1645)
	- [[Netherlands]], [[Protestant]]
	- "father of international law"
	- intellectual precursor of [[Realism]]
		- accepts the state as the key political unit
		- competition and war are inescapable aspects of int'l politics
	- contributes to [[Liberalism]]
		- argues there's a basis upon which one can view the state system as a community that exhibits something less than a "war of all against all"
	- rejected direct/indirect authority of [[Holy Roman Empire]] and the papacy
	- combines two thoughts: manmade AND natural law can both contribute to weakening conflict among states
		- conceived [[jus gentium]] as manmade laws resulting from human volition ([[Positivism]])
		- follows moral theology tradition of [[Thomas Aquinas]] and [[Francisco de Vitoria]], emphasizes laws derived from laws of nature
- natural law: humans in the state of nature are equal and free with no superior authority over them
	- natural law for states: also equal and free
	- individuals can't live in isolation, they aren't self-sufficient
	- individuals are social creatures, must associate with one another to survive
	- states must also associate with one another to survive
	- requires augmenting basic laws of nature through creation of the law of nations based on custom, consent, or contract
- Grotius discusses [[jus naturale]] and [[jus gentium]]
	- law of nature and of nations/peoples
	- no single term for "international law"
	- contributes to emancipation of int'l law from theology
		- states laws of nations still "have a degree of validity even if we should concede that which cannot be conceded without utmost wickedness, \[that] there is no God, or that the affairs of men are of no concern to Him." #quote 
- desire to liberate natural law from theology
	- [[Grotius]] was a committed [[Protestant]], but extremely tolerant compared to earlier [[Roman Catholicism]] and [[Protestant]] writers
		- the latter viewed different religions as heresy
- [[Grotius]]'s context
	- wrote during bitter religious wars of the first part of 17th c. and [[Thirty Years War]]
		- realized growing corpus of int'l law had to be truly secular
			- else, it would never be acceptable to [[Roman Catholicism]] AND [[Protestant]]s
- [[The Law of War and Peace (1625) by Hugo Grotius]]
	- discusses all types of laws including laws among nations
	- discusses [[just war doctrine]]
		- quotes [[Augustine]], [[Thomas Aquinas]], [[Cicero]], [[Livy]], [[Thucydides]]
		- argues 3 justifiable reasons for going to war
			1. "defense"
			2. "recovery of property"
			3. "punishment"
		- before war begins, state that's accused of causing injury has the right to submit the matter to [[arbitration]]
			- Grotius recognizes that in most cases, this is unlikely
			- that's why upholding the law of nations ([[jus gentium]]) just be undertaken by the aggrieved party through use of force
			- war is a means to enforce a state's legal rights
				- similar to judicial remedies in domestic polity
	- war shouldn't be undertaken "except for the enforcement of rights"
	- "once \[war is] undertaken, it should be carried on only within the bounds of law and good faith" #quote 
	- Grotius's rules of warfare
		- discusses treatment of civilians, prisoners of war, pillaging, duty of the victor toward those who offer unconditional surrender
		- key idea in his discussion of law of war: "moderation"
	- book was an immediate success
	- 1648: gains importance after [[Peace of Westphalia]]
		- [[Thirty Years War]] among [[Germany]] states and its settlement
			- completes *de facto* transformation of an int'l system based on tenuous unity provided by papacy and [[Holy Roman Empire]] to a system of sovereign state
		- to regulate relations among these new entities, a system of law was required
- Grotius differs from Hobbes
	- Grotius believes states are subject to [[jus gentium]] and observance of int'l law is in self-interest of states
		- writing during [[Thirty Years War]]: "such a work is all the more necessary because in our day, as in former times, there is no lack of men who view this branch of law with contempt as having no meaning outside of an empty name." #quote 
- Grotius provides intellectual foundation for evolving interstate system
	- inspired Emmerich de [[Vattel]] (1714 – 1767)
		- argues recognition of moral obligations among states could coexist with balance-of-power policies designed to assure stability and independence of states
	- influenced literature on regional integration and int'l regimes
	- inspires [[The English School]]'s core idea of an int'l society driven not just by power considerations, but also int'l rules of conduct accepted by states (for their enlightened self-interest)
		- many of these rules legally binding, constitutes a larger corpus of int'l law
#### 4.1.11 Reflections on the Thought of Writers Related to the Rise of States
- authors grappled with emerging int'l order in [[Western Europe]] following breakup of unity provided by [[Christianity]] and empire during [[Middle Ages]]
- 1648: settlement of [[Thirty Years War]] with [[Peace of Westphalia]]
	- explicit recognition of authority: kings, dukes, princes OVER people living on territories under their jurisdiction
- in previous century, [[Machiavelli]] used classical works ([[Greek]] and [[Roman]]) to understand why Rome became a world power in a short period
	- hoped to find lessons, guidelines, PATTERNS for rulers of emerging national states
	- key: state-societal level of analysis
		- Rome's [[Republic]] institutions
	- dynamic nature of [[Republic]] allows leaders to make most opportunities provided by [[Fortuna]] (fate)
	- previous moral justifications for state conduct were replaced by pragmatic considerations based on how states *really* act
	- focus on how states *do* act versus how they ought to behave
- [[Giovanni Botero]]'s summary
	- practical concerns how a leader can get power and keep it
	- "reason of state": justifies further expansion of state power
- [[Thomas More]]
	- [[Realism]]
	- recognized increasing importance of modern national state
	- recognized need for statesmen to be provided with sound advice on how to use their power
- [[Hobbes]]
	- political philosophy on more scientific basis
	- used traditional ideas as "state of nature" and "natural law" ([[jus naturale]])
		- concepts used to justify a powerful centralized state (preferably [[monarchy]]) to end domestic [[anarchy]]
	- elevates concept to an int'l system
		- warlike posture of states in a world without a supreme authority to enforce convenants
	- concept of the [[Leviathan]]
		- contributed to [[Realism]] idea of the unitary, rational actor dedicated to maintaining the security and independence of the state
- [[Realism]] emphasis on state as principal actor in IR
	- unified and calculating how best to preserve state's physical integrity
	- emerging concept of [[sovereignty]]
		- reinforced state autonomy
		- undermined universal claims of papacy and empire
- [[Bodin]]'s contribution
	- internal sovereignty
	- other scholars built on his concept –> external sovereignty
		- implications for relations among states
- schools of thought held different views on nature of rights and duties among states
	- Grotius realized it was necessary to develop some basis upon which states could relate to one another in an [[Anarchic]] realm
		- argued to a certain extant an int'l community existed
		- contributed to [[Realism]], [[Liberalism]], [[The English School]] images of int'l politics
	- writers after Grotius
		- interstate/IR were a given in political life
		- political thought turned to analyzing the conduct of states in int'l politics
