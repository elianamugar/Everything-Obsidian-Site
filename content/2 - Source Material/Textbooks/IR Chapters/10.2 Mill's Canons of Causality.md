#### 10.2.0 Mill's Canons of Causality
- [[John Stuart Mill]]'s understandings of causality underlie the application of the "scientific" method to testing of hypotheses of the social sciences in general
	- canons of [[induction]]
		- infers a causal relation between *independent variable* (X) and *dependent variable* (Y)
			1. Inductive Canon No. 1
				- method of agreement
					- (X) is always present whenever (Y) is always present
						- e.g. when in our research we always see an arms race (X) having begun before the outbreak of war (Y)
			2. Inductive Canon No. 2
				- method of difference
					- (X) is always absent whenever (Y) is also absent
						- e.g. when in our research we observe no arms races in prolonged periods of peace marked by absence of war
			3. Inductive Canon No. 3
				- joint method of agreement and difference
					- when (X) and (Y) are both present in one set but not in another (when (X) is put in one experiment to see if (Y) appears), but (X) is omitted from the other experiment in the expectation that (Y) will not appear
						- e.g. when in two separately controlled experimental simulations, an arms race is introduced in one simulation, but not in the other, the former results in the outbreak of war, but that latter simulation remains without war
			4. Inductive Canon No. 4
				- method of concomitant variation
					- when (X) and (Y) both vary in relation to one another either in the same or opposite directions
					- AKA as (X) increases/decreases, (Y) also increases/decreases
						- e.g. when in our research we assemble all the cases we identify of arms races and all the cases of the outbreak of war, we find a direct correlation between the two
							- the more arms racing occurring, the more wars tend to breakout
							- inverse correlation would be if there are more arms racing occurring, the lesser frequency of war breaking out
			5. Inductive Canon No. 5
				- method of residues
					- when a certain independent variable (X) or certain independent variables (X1, X2, etc.) have been identified as accounting for some of the variations in (Y), the remaining variation can be accounted for as due to other independent variables present, even if they haven't been identified as such
						- in a statistical analysis of the percentage of explained and unexplained variance
						- e.g. when in our research arms races account for much (or all) cases in which wars break out, we conclude that other factors we may or may not be able to identify and account for the rest of the explanation
- positivists identify one or another of these causal sequences in the hypotheses/causal models they test empirically
	- e.g. if (X) is present, then one tends to find (Y) directly or inversely follows variations in (X)
	- we find different combos of cause-effect sequences in complex causal models
	- some factors that need to be present to effect a certain outcome are referred to as necessary, but may not be sufficient to have this effect
	- theorists in the positivist [[Epistemology]] try to identify conditions or factors (variables or constants) that are necessary/sufficient to produce expected effects/outcomes
