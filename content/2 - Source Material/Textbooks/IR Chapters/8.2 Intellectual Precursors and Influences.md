#### 8.2.0 Intellectual Precursors and Influences
- Hugo [[Grotius]] (1583 – 1645)
	- looked beyond power and [[balance of power]] echoed in [[Machiavelli]] or [[Hobbes]]
	- states observe the norms of rules because they produce some degree of *order* or [[security]] that is in their enlightened self-interest
		- int'l law and "rational" bases of int'l/world politics
	- this interest-driven, rule-following dimension leads [[Martin Wight]] to adopt the term "[[rationalism]]"
		- [[Hedley Bull]] (1932 – 1985) later places in context of a still [[Anarchic]] [[international society]] of states
- [[Grotius]] doesn't ignore power and power politics among states
	- conflict among states include use of force
		- central to [[The Law of War and Peace (1625) by Hugo Grotius]]
	- treatment of economic activities (freedom of navigation on high seas) provides intellectual basis for int'l [[commercial law]]
	- it's in the interest of independent [[sovereignty]] states to follow rules that set parameters of [[IR]] in peacetime and provides criteria for resorting to [[War]]
		- result: make the use of force less barbarous than it otherwise would be
- [[Grotius]] focuses on commercial issues and matters of [[War]] and peace
	- gov'ts, trading companies, businesses of newly formed states
- [[Grotius]]'s context: [[Thirty Years War]]
	- offers formulations of law
	- influenced by [[Philosophy]] and [[History]] of [[Rome]]'s [[jus gentium]] (law to govern relations among diverse peoples in [[Ancient World]], [[Roman Empire]])
	- natural law ([[jus naturale]]) – philosophical view that claims are laws inherent in nature that transcend any laws made by mere mortals
		- [[Augustine]], [[Thomas Aquinas]], other [[Christianity]] writers of late-Roman Empire and [[Middle Ages]]
- [[Grotius]] makes general principles and customary practice central to his constructions of legal rules-of-the-road for states in a newly emerging, state-based [[Europe]] society
	- later colonial and imperial extension in 18th, 19th, 20th c.
	- results in [[Grotius]] understandings of int'l law becoming global in scope of their application
- territorial states become principal actors in new [[international society]] order
	- int'l law develops rapidly in [[diplomacy]]
		- e.g. territorial sea defined as a three-mile limit from the shoreline of the coastal state
			- 3 miles was practical: artillery technology of the time limited the range of a cannonball to about 3 miles
				- practical distance any country could expect to defend from the shore without going to sea
		- principles of [[just war theory]]: limits on, resort to, conduct in [[War]]
			- developed by [[Cicero]], [[Augustine]], [[Thomas Aquinas]], [[Alberico Gentili]], [[Francisco de Vitoria]], [[Francisco Suarez]] over 1500 yrs of [[Western]] civilization
			- matters of int'l law, not just moral preachings
		- ideas concerning mutual respect for welfare of foreign diplomats and their embassies and consulates
			- legal obligations based on customary practice of states
- contrasting [[Grotius]]: [[The English School]]
	- not as persuaded by writings of [[Kant]] (1724 – 1804)
	- [[Martin Wight]] views Kant scholars as revolutionists who try to substitute moral principles for the realities of politics
	- [[E. H. Carr]] (1892 – 1982) rejects any Kantian invocation of morality as attempts to transform [[IR]] as if they were/would become independent of power and interest
- [[Kant]]'s major influence
	- concerns about [[justice]]
	- follow-on generation of [[The English School]] scholars interest in establishing global, cosmopolitan norms in a transformed [[international society]] ([[world society]])
- 1950s: [[E. H. Carr]] widely acknowledged as an intellectual precursor to emergence of [[The English School]]
	- usually categorized as [[classical realism]]
	- "inner meaning of the modern int'l crises" experienced in the interwar period (1919 – 1939) is "the collapse of the whole structure of [[utopian]]ism based on the concept of the harmony of interests" #quote 
	- int'l politics involves continuing tension between power and interest, and moral considerations
		- sets intellectual stage for development within [[The English School]] for a [[rationalism]] middle path
		- traced back to [[Grotius]]
			- falls between [[Realism]] of [[Machiavelli]] or [[Hobbes]] and [[Idealism]]/[[revolutionism]] in [[Kant]]
	- [[E. H. Carr]] rejects as [[utopian]] the pure [[Idealism]] that focuses only on moral values while attempting to exclude power and self-interest
	- [[E. H. Carr]] finds any [[Realism]] that pretends values somehow can be dropped from the political equation as unrealistic
