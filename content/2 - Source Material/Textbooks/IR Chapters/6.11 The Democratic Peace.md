#### 6.11.0 The Democratic Peace
- likelihood of war reduced
	- expansion of free trade
	- [[democracy]]
- [[post-Cold War]], 1991: scholars attempt to answer "Are democracies more peaceful in their foreign relations?"
	- collapse of [[Communism]]
	- rise of fledgling [[democracy]] to replace [[authoritarian]] regimes
		- test bed for [[democratic peace theory]]
			- [[democratic]] backsliding begins in 2010s
	- following [[Kant]]
		- [[Michael Doyle]], [[Rudolph Rummel]], [[Bruce Russett]]
			- argument: [[Liberalism]] [[democracy]] are unique, can establish peaceful relations among themselves based on shared values and common approach to establishing legitimate domestic political orders
- [[democracy]] tends to not go to war with each other
	- [[Michael Doyle]] renews [[Kant]]'s idea of [[democratic peace theory]]
		- reexamines traditional [[Liberalism]] that gov'ts founded on respect for individual liberty exercise restraint and peaceful intentions in foreign policy
	- contradiction between [[Liberalism]]'s *pacifism* and [[imperialism]]
		- [[Liberalism]] nevertheless are different and more peaceful, yet prone to use force when they see it in their interest to do so
			- *not in wars with each other*
- [[democracy]] fights [[authoritarian]] regimes, sometimes as "arsenals of democracy"
	- e.g. [[America]] vs. [[fascism]] in [[WWII]]
	- [[democracy]] historically engaged in violent [[imperialism]] [[expansionism]] which provided opportunities to spread their own [[Liberalism]], [[democracy]] ideologies
	- empirical work suggests newly emerging [[democratic]] states may be more prone to start wars than long-established [[democratic]]/[[authoritarian]] regimes
- [[democratic peace theory]]'s literature
	- seeks to identify and explain patterns of behavior shown by democracies around the world
	- excitement generates around 1990s
		- collapse of [[USSR]] and breakup of [[Warsaw Pact]] produces wave of new democracies and promises new empirical possibilities to test theory
			- undermines [[neorealism]] argument that it's the structure of the int'l system (not the nature of gov'ts/societies) that explains a state's policies
				- good for [[Liberalism]]
			- [[democratic peace theory]] scholars: state and society do matter as do the people who compose them
- [[democratic peace theory]] used/misused by politicians [[post-Cold War]]
	- optimistic predictions concerning future of int'l politics
	- virtues of the theory were used by [[neoconservatism]]
		- added *democratization* as a reason to justify the [[America]] invasion of [[Iraq]] in 2003 ([[Iraq War]]/[[Second Gulf War]])
			- bad for [[Liberalism]]
		- supposedly, a [[democratic]] [[Iraq]] would benefit the Iraqis AND provide a model for [[Arab]] world
			- a "positive domino effect" on the rest of the troubled region
	- [[Liberalism]] theory embraced by [[neoconservatism]] by policy elites for their own purposes is **not good**
- complications in research and scholarship of [[democratic peace theory]]
	- complementary and contradictory research paths
	- complicated basic question: how does one define [[democracy]]?
		- critics: [[democratic peace theory]] scholars have too many definitions for their independent variable
		- [[democracy]] explains tendency for peaceful relations with other democracies, but what exactly is a democracy?
			- not entirely clear
	- lack of consensus on defining [[democracy]]
		- makes testing [[democratic peace theory]] hypotheses problematic
		- inclusive definitions compete with the definitions excluding large number of cases
		- more than a requirement of having elections
		- forms of democratic practice doesn't count, substance does
			- [[dictatorship]]s can use elections to mask [[authoritarian]] designs
		- fully developed, enduring democracies are supported by social structures, economies, cultures of shared values beneficial to democratic practice that are often lacking in emerging regimes loosely referred to as [[democratic]]
	- if agreed on what is [[democracy]], how do we define the dependent variable ([[war]]/peace)?
		- how is it operationalized (measured/counted)?
		- if [[War]], does that include armed interventions with marines landing on beach or only major and long, drawn-out conflicts with widespread death and destruction ([[WWI]] and [[WWII]])?
		- if peace, how is that defined?
			- simply the absence of [[War]]?
			- if so, just left with [[Hobbes]]'s view that int'l [[anarchy]] is inherently a *state of war*?
				- only remedy is to eliminate [[anarchy]] by establishing [[Leviathan]]/world gov't as the means to maintaining peace?
			- how far must [[global governance]] go?
			- can we rely more on peace-oriented norms of behavior (values shared by democracies)?
	- what is the time needed to examine to test [[democratic peace theory]] hypotheses?
		- different historical periods result in different findings
			- some periods, certain cases are included and others aren't
	- is the attribute of [[democracy]] the only explanatory factor as to why democratic states don't find each other?
		- is [[democracy]] necessary *and* sufficient to provide an explanation for peace?
			- are other factors of equal or greater importance?
- states in [[Western Europe]] haven't engaged in war/exhibit warlike behaviors among themselves since 1945
	- due to logic of [[democratic peace theory]]?
	- due to [[USSR]] threat from 1945-1991 that binds these states together under an [[America]] security umbrella that provides a comfortable deterrent effect against would-be invaders?
	- historical memories of [[WWI]] and [[WWII]] on the continent enough to discourage war?
	- pacifying effects of [[economic interdependence]] and [[integration]] key to peace in [[Europe]]an context?
- [[Kant]]'s argument for [[Republic]], more peaceful [[IR]]
	- doesn't necessarily argue for [[democracy]]
	- [[Republicanism]]: [[representative democracy]] supported by rule of law and respect for basic freedoms/rights in civil society
		- not a direct [[democracy]] (popular will/majority prevails)
		- people might not be active participants in everyday politics
			- leaves matters to representatives with varying degrees of accountability
