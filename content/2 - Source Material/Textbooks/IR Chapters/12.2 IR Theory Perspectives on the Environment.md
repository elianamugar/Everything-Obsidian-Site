#### 12.2.0 IR Theory Perspectives on the Environment
- IR theory mostly focuses on conflict/[[War]]
	- states as actors
	- climate change doesn't discriminate among states and borders, effects felt globally
		- sovereign political borders mismatch global ecological system
		- due to differential impact, dealing with climate change is an example of collective action (global governance problem)
- 1968: Garrett Hardin's [[tragedy of the commons]]
	- individuals (herdsmen) have access to a shared resource
		- e.g. pasture land in a village ("the commons")
	- rational from short-term self-interest perspective to allow one's sheep to graze on the commons
	- but if everyone does the same thing at the same time, overgrazing occurs, depleting the resource
		- everyone loses in the long term
- [[Rousseau]]'s stag hunt in [[5.4 System]] ([[Realism]])
	- by following one's narrow self-interest, everyone eventually suffers
- solution to a collective-action problem is to design a policy that leads people/states to agree to upgrade the common interest to the benefit of all over the long haul
	- [[Realism]] and [[Liberalism]]
		- states/int'l orgs they're a part of are starting point for finding answers and solutions
	- others see states as major block and cause of ecological problems
- [[Realism]]
	- doesn't focus on climate change
	- threats come from states, not nature
	- resource scarcity viewed from prism of state competition
		- important when it subtracts from/adds to a state's material power
		- relative gains matter, so it's rational for a state to secure as much energy as possible (oil, gas, coal pollutants)
			- striving for independent energy [[security]] trumps concern for global environment
			- e.g. [[Russia-Ukraine War]] 2022
				- energy dependence on [[Russia]] was a source of security vulnerability
			- if a state rival suffers from drought/catastrophic climate event, can enhance relative power of a competitor state
	- climate change
		- if it's slowed and not halted, states will take lead in int'l orgs they play a major role in
		- states will have to find a way to upgrade their common interest and solve the collective-action problem
		- int'l orgs are controlled by member states, reflecting state interests
			- NGOs can lobby for green policies
			- private-sector companies contribute to technological breakthroughs that reduce a state's dependence on foreign sources of energy
	- power-based [[regime theory (realism)]]
		- [[hegemonic stability]]
			- regimes (e.g. agreed rules that constitute a global climate regime) are established and maintained when a single state holds a preponderance of power resources
			- once a dominant state's [[hegemonic power]] declines in relative power as other states rise, it may not have the same capacity to lead
				- the regimes in which it's played a dominant role may decline and the collective efforts on the part of states
		- [[post-Cold War]] [[unipolar]] "moment" of [[America]]
			- destined to devolve into [[multipolar]] world
				- lack of such leadership/hegemony undermines prospects for a strong global climate regime
- [[Liberalism]], [[liberal institutionalism]]
	- optimistic about prospects of int'l efforts and collaboration for tackling global environment problems as climate change
	- literature on [[global civil society]], [[Transnationalism]], [[interdependence]], int'l regimes, [[neoliberal institutionalism]], [[global governance]]
		- useful concepts and hypotheses to analyze collective-action problem
	- assumes that states, while major actors in world politics, aren't the only ones
		- non-state, transnational actors
		- int'l orgs
- 1980s: "sustainable development" a conceptual framework for dealing with the environment
	- resulted from a series of compromises between [[First World]] and [[Third World]]
		- [[Third World]] claimed [[First World]] polluted and exploited the environment and now wanted the Third World to forgo economic policies that harmed the global environment
	- idea behind sustainable development
		- to legitimize economic development in the south in the context of environmental protection
	- liberal interest in economic and environmental regimes
		- sustainable development a popular concept in [[Liberalism]]
		- challenge of achieving cooperation in global commons and the complex interactions among int'l regimes
		- [[neoliberal institutionalism]] uses rigorous methodologies to address basic research questions concerning environment
			- what conditions do states tend to support int'l regimes and why do some states defect?
- [[Realism]], [[Liberalism]], [[The English School]] share [[rationalism]] approach to understanding IR
	- [[The English School]] latecomer to environmentalism, but recently incorporates topic into its framework of int'l/[[world society]]
		- what impact has global environmentalism had on world society?
		- how can one reconcile the tension between global environmental responsibility and justice vs. [[sovereignty]], int'l law, and global market?
	- development of guardianship norm in int'l society
		- rise of environmental ethics and values within society of states promoted by environmentalists
		- state leaders are responsible stewards of the planet
			- states have come to accept environmental [[multilateralism]] as a procedural norm within int'l orgs, but barriers to deep-seated change exists
			- pressure from [[Third World]] led [[First World]] to agree to establish a fund to compensate them for some of the costs/losses sustained due to global climate change
				- important details on size of fund, amounts to be contributed by particular states, and how the money will be distributed remain undecided
- [[Economic Structuralism]] on the environment
	- another example of [[Capitalism]]'s [[exploitation]]
		- the environment is a source of material power and wealth,  not a concern for the welfare of humans
	- [[Europe]]'s global expansion
		- economic development uneven
		- environmental degradation uneven
	- [[postcolonialism]] literature developed concept of [[postcolonial ecocritcism]]
		- an umbrella term for several topics including gender, justice, and differential impact of [[Globalization]]
- [[social constructivism]]
	- focus on development of norms
		- major impact on scholars working on global environmental issues
			- [[Liberalism]], [[Economic Structuralism]]
	- challenge to [[Positivism]] and argument that scientific knowledge is constructed
		- prospects for cooperation among states are defined by material power *and* ideational elements *and* intersubjective agreement among actors
			- if state interests and identity are constructed due to assumption of human agency, it's logically possible that policies can be designed to enhance the global public good in the environmental realm, expanding [[global governance]]
	- problem: while there might be a growing consensus among scientists and policy experts re: climate change, domestic politics can be barrier to significant change at home and int'lly
		- many countries (e.g. [[America]]) position on climate change is associated with political affiliation
			- scientific evidence and policy expertise have difficulty overcoming anti-environmental political ideology, given allegiance to political affiliation
- 1970s and 1980s: modern [[feminism]] movement in West, around same time as environmental movement
	- "ecofeminism" concept brings the two concepts together
		- adds assumption of [[Capitalism]] [[exploitation]] involving [[Third World]]
		- hierarchical economic order under capitalism is combined with a patriarchal understanding of the world resulting in series of binaries
			- man/women, humans/nature, exploiters/exploited
			- white, male, "human" subject dominates and is in opposition to the rest who are "other/s"
		- "The climate crisis is not gender neutral. Climate change is a powerful 'threat multiplier,' making existing vulnerabilities and injustices worse. Especially under conditions of poverty, women and girls face greater risk of displacement or death from extreme weather disasters.... There is growing proof of the link between climate change and gender-based violence, including sexual assault, domestic abuse, and forced prostitution. Tasks core to survival, such as collecting water and wood or growing food, fall on female shoulders in many cultures. These are already challenging and time-consuming activities; climate change can deepen the burden, and with it struggles for health, education, and financial security." #quote 
	- requirement to empirically document these impacts and disparities
	- moral clarity, empath, action also a requirement
	- strong [[normative]], policy-oriented focus and agenda
