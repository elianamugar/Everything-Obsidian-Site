# Thoughts
This oil painting (I can't even find the original title in Hungarian!) is by István Réti, a famous Hungarian painter known for founding the Nagybánya artists' school and colony, which was very influential in Hungarian and Romanian art. 

There's not too much information out there on this painting, but essentially, this is a depiction of bohemians. Réti spent his Christmas in Munich while studying under Simon Hollósy (another Hungarian painter) and was inspired by Hungarians (these bohemians) feeling homesick at Christmas. The website that I could get information from explained the painting best:

*The silence of the humble fasting and a deep solemnity are present. He depicts the scene in soft reddish-brown shades and brushwork recalling the sound of the violoncello.*

Réti, as founder of the Nagybánya school, gave a quote about the urge to reform art, which captures the spirit of this specific painting:
>"In the ecstasy of the Millennium celebrations we believed we were occupiers of new art in Hungary. During the self-celebrations of the past, we turned our faces to future. We were standing there alone, but we felt strong. Our souls were filled with what was to come, what we were going to do."

It was hard to find any reasoning behind using *Bohemians' Christmas Abroad* (1893) online as the cover art for the specific edition I was reading for *The Brothers Karamazov* (1880), so much so that Google AI says "it seems there might be some confusion." Here is my evidence that it was used:

Generally, book covers reflect the themes, tone, and setting of the book they represent (not all the time, but that's for a whole other topic). I can only guess the publishers thought "hey, these three guys could be the three brothers in the book!" RIP Smerdyakov. That's what I see, anyway. I reckon the guy standing behind the lamp that sort of looks like a priest is Alyosha, the one right of the lamp is Dmitri, and the one in the forefront is Ivan. All I know is Dostoevsky would've been repulsed by this painting because it's much too bourgeois of a depiction of anything Dostoevsky has written in his literary canon.
