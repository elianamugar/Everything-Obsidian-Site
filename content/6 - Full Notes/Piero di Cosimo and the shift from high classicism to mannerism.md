# Piero di Cosimo and the shift from high classicism to mannerism

In 1510, Piero di Cosimo began painting “Perseus frees Andromeda” in Florence. He finished it in 1515, during the time of the Renaissance. The painting is 70 x 120cm and the technique used is tempera grassa on wood. In the scene of the painting, it depicts the mythological hero Perseus killing the monster who trapped Andromeda, the goddess of dreams.

Based on the piece, many artistic and trends fit between the shift from high classicism to mannerism. One main feature in the piece is the off-centered monster with Perseus on its back. This is highly attributed to mannerist paintings, where the compositions tend to be far less balanced and symmetrical. To add to the asymmetry, there are more people crowding the bottom-right of the painting than the bottom-left. This feature of composition is similar to that of Michelangelo’s “The Creation of Adam,” where the hands of Adam and God are off-centered.

Another feature is the space of the composition. In mannerism, spaces tend to be more ambiguous and less perfectly designed. The perspective of the painting is from an odd angle and captures all landscape features in an imperfect way. Logically, from a realistic perspective of the viewer, one would not be able to see all the features that Cosimo included in the painting, but it is in a mannerist one. This adds to another aspect of mannerism, which acknowledged that viewers were looking at a painting as opposed to a perfect recreation of reality. So, in warping the image to show an abnormal view from the viewer’s perspective, it emphasizes the artificial and the unrealistic. These features are much different to that of Leonardo da Vinci’s “The Last Supper,” where there is a strict and formal composition and all the perspective lines were realistic from the viewer’s point of view.

Because Cosimo was a Florentine painter, his work seemed to be heavily influenced by Michelangelo. Besides “The Creation of Adam,” there are some similarities between Cosimo’s “Perseus frees Andromeda” and Michelangelo’s “The Last Judgement.” Both paintings subvert expectations of the subject matter; Michelangelo depicts God to look like Apollo and Cosimo depicts the demigod Perseus in two places at once in the same painting.

Cosimo, as a painter, strove to depict clean narrations with credible eastern influences, as seen in the costumes of the subjects. His aim for this painting was not to evoke the drama of Perseus freeing Andromeda, but rather focused on the story as it is, at face-value, with accuracies in its musical instruments. Cosimo does this by painting minimal expressions on the subjects’ faces or not showing their faces at all. This is most apparent in both Perseuses; the flying Perseus has no facial expression, while the Perseus on the monster has his face concealed. In looking at the painting, I appreciated the fullness of the painting and how the structure is atypical of the flat perspectives from the Renaissance era, adding to the story-telling. In general, I think any painting that portrays myths is fascinating because there is always a bit of the artist’s touch and flair to the story. In a way, we are able to see how the artist views the story, depending on their artistic liberties or lack thereof.
