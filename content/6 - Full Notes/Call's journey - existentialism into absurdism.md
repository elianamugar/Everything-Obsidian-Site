# Call's journey - existentialism into absurdism

Woodrow Call in *Lonesome Dove* (1985) hears from a fellow ex-Ranger, Jake, about the beauty of Montana. Jake claims Montana and the north in general is a cowboy's paradise, far different from the Texas they know, especially being right on the border. Call's ears perk up at this idea and convinces his lifelong friend and also fellow ex-Ranger, Augustus, to take up the Hat Creek Cattle Company with them at set up the first ranch in Montana. This becomes Call's purpose.

Call found his purpose. This is the pure epithet of [[Existentialism]], which claims the universe has no meaning, but insists that individuals have the radical freedom and responsibility to create their own purpose through actions, choice, and authenticity. But this all changes when Gus dies in Montana and requests Call to bury him at the beginning of their journey: Lonesome Dove in Texas, right on the border of Mexico. Call promises to do so, yet, Call delays. He hovers around getting the ranch started in Montana, hoping to find purpose in working. He doesn't. He finds he doesn't have a penchant for it anymore. And worst of all, his promise to his best friend hangs over his head. Larry McMurtry writes this poignant line:

>Gus’s death, and the ones before it, had caused \[Call] to lose his sense of purpose to such an extent that he scarcely cared from one day to the next what he was doing.

Call gets thrown into a state of [[Absurdism]], which claims humans have an innate desire for meaning, but live in a world that offers none – the "Absurd". Even though Call feels purposeless, he picks himself up and carries Gus's coffin right down to the south border of Texas. Rather than creating meaning (existentialism) or giving up ([[Nihilism]]), absurdism suggests embracing this conflict with defiance and living life to the fullest anyway. Call does this by going through with a new purpose, a temporary state of existentialism, then just *lives*. He arrives to the start of Hat Creek Cattle Company, then lingers around town, staring at was has and hasn't changed. And that's where Larry McMurtry ends the book, which reminds me of a line of his from the final chapter:

>Something wasn't there that had been.
