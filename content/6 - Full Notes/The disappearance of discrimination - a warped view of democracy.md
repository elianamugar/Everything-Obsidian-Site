# The disappearance of discrimination - a warped view of democracy

In the pamphlet of the Massachusetts State House, there is a note from William Francis Galvin, the Secretary of the Commonwealth, saying, “The State House is a precious landmark full of historical significance to both our state and country…. It is a symbol of the values our founding fathers envisioned. It is the location of democracy....” The history of the State House has layers—after all, “Massachusetts” is an Algonquian Indian word meaning “at the great hill.”There is a sort of irony in seeing that Massachusetts is “a symbol of the values our founding fathers envisioned” and “the location of democracy” when the origin of the state begins with the Algonquin Indian natives. The Massachusetts State House emphasizes how past marginalized groups of people, including the Algonquian Indians, blacks, and women, made Massachusetts the state it is today, but disregards their struggles, glorifying colonial and male dominion. 

A central piece of the Massachusetts State House is its stained-glass window placed on the ceiling, present in the Memorial Hall. On the stained glass are unique depictions of thirteen different seals, each representing the thirteen original colonies. Twelve of the thirteen seals surround the 19th century Massachusetts state seal, displaying the circle of the unity of the states. Putting the Massachusetts seal in the center highlights the state of Massachusetts and its role in politics. When taking a closer look at the 19th century Massachusetts state seal, a blue field with an Algonquian Native American man holding a bow and arrow in both hands can be seen. The blue field on the shield represents the blue hills of Massachusetts which represents the people who are part of the Commonwealth, including the Algonquian people who live on the hill. One of the early forms of the seal was a white Algonquian Native American wearing improper clothing, showing the seal creator’s ignorance of the Algonquian Indian culture. The original seal “featured an Indian holding an arrow pointed down in a gesture of peace, with the words ‘Come over and help us” and is still on the present-day seal. The original seal shows the intentions of the colonists, depicting the Native Americans’ need of help to assimilate to the colonists’ culture. Symbolically, the arrow pointing down represents the peacefulness of the Algonquian people, but there is a deeper meaning. This establishes the white settler’s supremacy by making the Algonquian Indians subservient people, displaying their passiveness and welcoming of the colonists. It was later changed to an Englishman and Indian kneeling in front of King James II.This changed the depiction of the Native American seems to show that the Native Americans accepted the rulings of the King and are like the colonists. Now, the modern-day seal is an “attempt to provide a more accurate representation of the appearance of the Indian.”

 The use of the Native Americans in the present-day seal may seem to represent the natives of Massachusetts, but there may be a factor that is overlooked. The seal has a Latin motto which translates to “by the sword we seek peace, but peace only under liberty” and “was written about 1659, attributed to the famous English patriot, Algernon Sydney.” Although the formation of the Massachusetts state was in major part due to Algonquian Indian history, the Latinization of the state motto whitewashes the Native American history. Also, the irony is American “patriots” are allowed to use “the sword,” or warfare, to “seek peace” for “liberty,” yet the constant battle for Algonquian Indians to retain their land has been seen as an encroachment on the white man’s settlement. In other words, the Algonquian Indians’ right to use “the sword” to “seek peace” for “liberty” is not reserved for the Algonquian’s liberty to keep their land, only for American liberty to use Algonquian land. 

Along with the whitewashing of Algonquian Indian history, the State House glosses over the black history. The particular flag representing the 54th Massachusetts Volunteer Infantry commemorates the survival of the flag, but that is all it seems to do. The caption covers many factual points, including the flag’s history in battle, but only a brief mention of “the first black regiment to be raised in the North.” Although the point was raised, it does not cover the depth of the value of having a black regiment during the Civil War. It may seem minor, but this displays a lack of glorification in the black history behind the flag and its regiment. Rather, it focuses on praising the battle history of the flag, only hyping up the Civil War. Because the caption glazes of the black history, when talking about Sgt. William Carney’s heroic actions, Carney’s race is vague; the Massachusetts State House’s caption assumes visitors understand Carney’s race, never mentioning this point. An interpretation of the caption could be that Sgt. William Carney is a white man leading the black regiment. Only after further research can it be realized that Sgt. William Carney was a black man leading a black regiment. Both points of the representation of the first black regiment, the makeup of its history, and the significance of having a black man lead his regiment have been masked by the glory of war and Massachusetts’ participation in the Civil War.

In 1995, the Massachusetts State Senate wanted to “make art on display in the state’s most important public building more inclusive and representative of the people of the Commonwealth” attributed to the women of Massachusetts. Through a committee, the Senate chose to show six female individuals because “through [their] actions, has made a major contribution to the government of our commonwealth or who, through [their] actions, has represented a group of women who has made such a contribution.” One of the individuals was Dorothea Dix. She was interested in the treatment of criminals, the poor, and the insane and helped those who were considered “outcasts of society” by visiting each city and town in Massachusetts. She wrote a report called Memorial to the Legislature of Massachusetts and sent it to the legislature to persuade them to have a bill written to increase the funding of the overcrowded Worcester State Asylum. Through her arduous work, an act was passed, helping these individuals. Having these outstanding women who contributed to the commonwealth be celebrated is a very important concept that the state of Massachusetts strives for. This allows the state to be more inclusive of its citizens and care for its people. Her work not only impacted Massachusetts but she also brought her work and helped other states to construct mental hospitals.

Despite the Massachusetts State House’s recognition of impactful women like Dorothea Dix, the exhibit is placed in an unfrequented area. Initially, in 1999, the exhibit would be placed near the entrance so people would pass by it on their way in. After remodeling, the women’s exhibit was placed in an empty hall where visitors only attend when taken on a tour. Even then, the exhibit is the last talked about on the tour, permitting people to dismiss these great women since they are not talked about in the beginning. The argument could be made that since these women are talked about last, that it will be the most impressionable part of the tour, leaving visitors pondering after they finish the tour. But from experience, no detail was gone into each of the six women, only particular ones. Many were left unnamed and their presence throughout the whole Massachusetts State House is minimal. Subtle details as such minimize the significance of these women, even when their importance makes up a big part of Massachusetts’ history. Again, like the rest of their marginalized counterparts, women’s history is passed over.

In its grandeur, lining the halls of the State House and Chamber rooms, portraits of past governors hang. The portraits are hung in the order of when the governors served, allowing visitors to see the changes in artistic trends of the governors’ portraits. There is no diversity of the governors because, up until the 2000s, all the governors were white males. Governors paid commission for their own portraits so they could decide how to present themselves and would later be donated to the State House. Some governors chose to wear informal clothing and others held cigars, while ex-Governor Mitt Romney was the first portrait to be painted with a picture of his wife next to him. These portraits display the natures of the governors as well as the tradition to paint portraits of governors, like that of Presidents. These portraits honor the governors that served Massachusetts. Displaying these portraits helps to show how Massachusetts has been more inclusive in the people who govern them. Although it took until the 2000s to have a female governor and a black governor, it shows the change over time from Massachusetts’ traditional choice in governors. These changes in traditional governorship helps to display the changes in diversity in our political system.

In the House Chamber there is a cod fish hanging from the ceiling, above the chamber doors. The cod fish plays an important role in the fishing industry of Massachusetts and for its citizens. Presented to the Massachusetts House of Representatives by Jonathan Rowe in honor of the importance of the cod fishing industry, the Sacred Cod was placed in the House of Chambers for the “survival and prosperity of the Commonwealth.” The House of Chambers represents its citizens, ensuring the voices of citizens to be heard in political matters. With the cod fish in the House Chambers, it symbolizes the representation of fishermen, showing how Massachusetts honors past traditions of the prosperous fishing industry. When the cod fish was stolen as a prank by Harvard students the House of Representatives didn’t want to discuss legislatures until it was returned, displaying the role of quirks and tradition in the Massachusetts government. 

After a visit to the State House, there is a realization that it displays a skewed version of democracy. They show the complex history of the previously alienated; the State House claims their struggles as their own, creating the modern state of Massachusetts. Although the inclusion of marginalized people is thoughtful, the State House neglects in telling the story of America’s selective liberty. The possible consequences of the continuation of this form of storytelling is the complete ignorance of the past, eventually forgetting the sacrifices of the Algonquian Indians, blacks, and women.
