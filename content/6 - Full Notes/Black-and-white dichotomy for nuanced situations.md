# Black-and-white dichotomy for nuanced situations

This is probably one of my more subjective mini-essays, but something I've noticed is people viewing nuanced situations in a black-and-white, or in other words, a binary view. Something is either *this* or *that*, and no in-between. No gray. And I can't accept that, personally, even if that makes my life a little easier. I get why people do it – it's simply because two options makes life easier rather than the infinite amount of options that can happen between 0 and 1! I say this because in *The Drama* (2016), one of the topics it brings to the forefront of my mind *is* this modern view of a black-and-white dichotomy.

Now, here I should rant a little longer about why I *absolutely despise* black-and-white dichotomies. I grew up as a POC woman at the intersection of many identities. I've despised those checkmarks that determine my identity and would much prefer people accept the whole person of who I am, which is this nuanced amalgamation of a mess. I'm not defined just by my looks, but *so much more*. At a pragmatic level, it just doesn't make sense to view situations as such. Hell, even in the [[IR]] theory book I'm reading, if the world were so black and white, there would only be two views! But, in fact, it discusses eight primary ones! Don't even get me started on the plethora of sub-views. 

For the sake of this discussion, there are **two** ways I describe this black-and-white dichotomy: **metaphorically** (a binary world, with no gray in-between), and **literally** (black and white race, male and female gender). In terms of the *first* definition, I bring up Charlie, Misha, and Rachel as case studies. I actually do believe Charlie views the world in a gray area, that is before he caves into the pressures of his immediate circle of people who see the world as more black and white. It doesn't help his case that he is too close (literally he's going to marry her) to the person-in-question, Emma. The people's views he succumbs to is primarily Rachel and Misha. Rachel is the first to lash out at the wine tasting, right when Emma drops the fact that she *almost* committed a school mass shooting. Immediately, Rachel categorizes Emma as *bad*, even though she didn't do it (mentioned more in-depth [here](obsidian://open?vault=Everything&file=6%20-%20Full%20Notes%2FDo%20your%20thoughts%20or%20actions%20define%20you%3F) and [here](obsidian://open?vault=Everything&file=6%20-%20Full%20Notes%2FDoes%20the%20purpose%20of%20your%20actions%20define%20how%20legitimate%20your%20actions%20are%3F)). Charlie offers a slightly more creative version of Emma's story, asking Misha's opinions on what she would do if she were to marry someone like Emma. Misha also immediately categorizes Emma as *bad*, and in fact, doubles down. This increases Charlie's doubts about going through with the marriage.

Now, onto the *second* definition. Quite literally, this meta-subject is brought up in the flashback scene where Emma decides to not commit the mass shooting. A classmate brings up that the issue is with men, they're always the ones doing the shooting. Emma rebuts that point, especially because she (and us as the audience) know that she would've changed that supposed fact. Emma brings up that there have been other women that have done mass shootings, then the conversation patters out. The other subtle part that Borgli forces the audience to contend with in a subtle way is the fact that Emma is a **black woman**. That changes this discourse on its axis, since most of the conversation regarding America's mass shooting and gun violence issues are usually in regards to **white men**. It should be noted, however, that at the time the movie was being made, there was less shootings made by transgender/transgender-affiliated shooters. Now they exist and it's turning the heads of ultra-conservatives here in America. 

But back to the conversation of Emma being a **black woman**. No one in the film seems to acknowledge this intersectionality which plays *a* role in leading her to her *almost*-committed actions. Charlie tries to pin the thoughts of a mass shooting on Emma's potential trauma of seeing a car accident. Emma adamantly ad vehemently denies that. I believe her. My first thought to the reason why Emma *almost* committed a mass shooting was due to society. After all, Charlie is also subjected to this societal bullying surrounding Emma's *almost*-committed actions. We see flashbacks of Emma being bullied, not necessarily because of skin color, but because of *her*. But in those flashbacks it's clear that she's the only **black person**, let alone **black woman** amongst her peers. Now *that* would fuel the flames. She claims she did it for the aesthetics, because it was prevalent online and looked cool, and I think that has to dow ith the fact that she didn't feel like she fit in. She felt like an outsider. And it's hard to be an outsider if you fit in with the other outsiders, right? A group of outsiders negates that *outsider*-ness. So to her, maybe it was more than just an aesthetic, it was in fact a safe haven. More flashbacks reveal that her family life was decent, but her parents were *busy* with work. They weren't necessarily absent or neglectful, but they weren't around to solve her alienation from society. And so I think the deep root of the issue is *feeling* alienation. After all, she stopped proceeding with the mass shooting when she fit into the anti-gun club at school. She wasn't feeling alienated anymore, but rather championed.

Here, I feel Borgli fell short of the discourse and, as I've mentioned in my Letterboxd review, was severely limited in the film format. Again, I wished this was explored in a book wher eyou have more words and space (and I would argue, time) to further delve into this topic (amongst several others I've written about regarding *The Drama*).

I think a lot of this discourse regarding black-and-white dichotomies can give us insights into mass shooters and why the choose to do it. Their identity plays a part in it, but there are deeper roots than identity. A shooter is not created in a vacuum. It takes a village, unfortunately.

I would like to add a disclaimer. Understanding **why** something occurs (such as a mass shooting) doesn't justify the actions taken, but rather adds to the whole picture of the causes that can lead to certain effects, which can in turn serve as case studies on how to treat individuals, society, etc. to potentially reach a certain outcome (preferably positive, whatever that means).
