# The United States must run this show - the contradiction of self-determination

In 1898, Spain ceded the Philippines, Puerto Rico, and Guam to the United States after the Spanish-American War. Referring to the Filipinos, William Howard Taft, civilian governor of the Philippines, informed President William McKinley that “our little brown brothers” would need “fifty or one hundred years” of close supervision “to develop anything resembling Anglo-Saxon political principles and skills.”[^1] In order to “save” the Philippines, the McKinley administration believed they needed to control those lands to set them “free.” Soon after McKinley, President Theodore Roosevelt “wanted to increase the influence and prestige of the United States on the world stage and make the country a global power,” believing that American values and ideals should be exported to rescue the world.[^2] This was an early instance where colonization, imperialism, nationalism, and self-determination were ambiguous. Especially in regards to self-determination, this root of American ideals complicated America’s foreign policy and involvement on the international stage. Although self-determination began as an ideal that drove the American mission to support self-governance, it made America a dominating global superpower that took advantage of fragile nations to fit the heroic American narrative, ultimately using globalization to impede different nations’ ways of life**.**

While wars waged on the European continent, the United States felt geographically separated from the Great War. Yet in 1917, President Woodrow Wilson believed America should enter the war and promoted “self-determination,” meaning that a nation can seek to create its own independent government or state.[^3] When convincing Congress, Wilson established America’s objective to “...vindicate the principles of peace… against selfish and autocratic power and to set up… the really free and self-governed peoples of the world….”[^4] Wilson paved the way for the heroic American narrative and introduced America’s role as a global superpower by signifying America as the guardian of peace and liberty. Though it was not apparent at the time, Wilson constructed the foundation upon which America would be the valiant savior. This Wilsonian ideal was more deep-rooted at the end of the Great War.

After the Great War, the world was fragile and needed to be fixed; Wilson believed America was the country to repair the broken world. Even though the other victors, such as France, Britain, and Italy, put their faith in Wilson, they were also skeptical of his “[[League of Nations]],” the ultimate American creation for peace.[^5] Wilson had to persuade with a proper case for this idealistic international body. In doing so, Wilson outlined his fourteen points which dictated the American motivation for spearheading the creation of the League of Nations. Although the word “self-determination” was never explicitly used, it was alluded in the fifth point, promising “\[a] free, open-minded, and absolutely impartial adjustment of all colonial claims, based upon a strict observance of the principle that in determining all such questions of sovereignty, the interests of the populations concerned must have equal weight with the equitable claims of the government whose title is to be determined.”[^6] For Wilson, he wanted all nations to be independent, but it didn’t happen. Many nations were neglected during the Paris Peace Accords, even when they bargained for territory, reparations, and self-governance. 

The problem with the [[Paris Peace Conference]] and the [[Treaty of Versailles]] was the victorious powers wanted unreasonable demands from Germany. As a nation, Germany did not believe they lost the war since it was never fought on German soil. So, they rightly believed these demands could be negotiated. But when Germany realized this would not be the case, the nation was outraged.[^7] Even though Wilson advocated for “ultimate peace of the world” and “liberation of its peoples, the German people included,” by denying Germany the chance to argue their case against unreasonable reparation and territorial demands, this contradicted the idea that America was fighting “for the rights of nations great and small and privilege of men everywhere to choose their way of life and of obedience.”[^8] Count Ulrich von Brockdorff-Rantzau, the German foreign minister who attended the Paris Peace Conference, pleaded to the council, “Even in internal affairs we are to give up the right to self-determination,”[^9] and counter-proposed that “Germany demands that the right of self-determination shall also be respected where the interests of the Germans in Austria and Bohemia are concerned.”[^10] Unfortunately for Germany, Wilson contradicted American ideals and stripped the German right to self-determination in order to create the League of Nations. The recognition of the loss of German self-determination simply because Germany was not seen as a victor showed the unfairness of the Paris Peace Talks. Though Wilson believed America would be the one to make the world safe for democracy “planted upon the trusted foundations of political liberty,” the outbreak of World War II would break down those ideals.[^11]

During Adolf Hitler’s rise to power and the establishment of the Third Reich, German people were mixed into different countries. Many of those countries treated the Germans as third-rate citizens. No one during the Paris Peace Conference considered the consequences of splitting up the German people and German nationalism, as well as Germany’s inability to pay reparations. As a result, German people starved. Hitler used the contradiction of the American self-determination and the shame Germany held over its head after they had been denied self-determination to explain how Germany would restore its greatness: “I hereby declare that… the part of the Treaty of Versailles which deprived our Volk of equality of rights and degraded it to an inferior Volk has now been settled in the natural course of things.”[^12] Hitler ultimately blamed the inevitable and incoming Second World War on the Treaty of Versailles. Based on Hitler’s sentiments, the United States and other victorious powers after World War I faced the repercussions for their brutal demands. Their ultimate demise was another world war and another period of uncertainty. Although this was a small crack in the idea of self-determination that America promoted, America tried to fix the world again.

Towards the end of World War II, President Franklin Roosevelt desired to create a more successful international body—the [[United Nations]]. Roosevelt’s purpose was to prevent the mistakes made during the Paris Peace Accords, but unfortunately, Roosevelt died shortly before he could see it come to fruition. As a result, President Harry S. Truman oversaw its creation and gave a speech to the United Nations, focusing on America’s intentions of global collaboration to bring world peace: “Our own industrial accomplishments have nominated us as the Nation that must assume a position to guide others in the path way of peaceful production….”[^13] This was another stepping stone in America’s global superpower narrative—the U.S. was going to spearhead the mission for international peace, spreading democracy, and self-determination. In these efforts, U.S. Secretary of State George Marshall proposed his “Marshall Plan,” saying it was the American people’s responsibility “to save Europe from starvation and chaos…” while preserving “the glorious heritage of a free America.”[^14] On top of this proposed idea, Marshall emphasized the “self-sufficiency” of Europe and a “self-supporting Germany.”[^15] The Truman and Roosevelt administration did not want an outbreak of another world war, so to do so, they believed America had the economic power to stimulate the global economy. This gave America the upper-hand in foreign policy, establishing a moral obligation from other nations to respect America’s dominance and “one-world” order.[^16] Around this point in time, America’s mission to spread democracy and restore world order became twisted. After the Marshall Plan’s implementation and the restoration of the European economy, the push for globalization began to threaten other countries’ independence—the ultimate contradiction of self-determination.

After the French decolonization of Indochina in 1954, Vietnam became a country of American interest. Due to the [[Geneva Accords]], Vietnam was split in two: North Vietnam had communist ideals while the South was anti-communist. In order to push against the communist Viet Congs and North Vietnamese Army, the United States supported Ngo Dinh Diem’s installation in South Vietnam because of fears of a power vacuum and political instability.[^17] But this led to a lot of conflict in Vietnam, a country where the North Vietnamese Prime Minister Ho Chi Minh believed Vietnam should be more independent and restore their way of life.[^18] During the Vietnamese conflict, a South Vietnamese peasant girl was taught to refer to the Viet Congs as “terrorists.” But later in the account, the girl became a Viet Cong supporter, believing the Viet Congs were freedom fighters rather than “terrorists.” The Viet Congs, in turn, indoctrinated young children like the peasant girl to believe the Americans were the terrorists.[^19] Although America had been vying for self-determination and self-sufficiency for nations, America seemed to contradict these ideals with the situation in Vietnam. If the U.S. believed in self-determination and self-sufficiency, idealistically, America would have trusted the Vietnaemse to install their own government. But in this case, America did not. As a result, North Vietnam felt trapped and threatened by the American way of life clashing with the Vietnamese way of life. And with this complication of self-determination, independence, and democracy, another conflict arose during the 9/11 era, when the term “terror” and “terrorism” became more prevalent in American society.

After American involvement and intervention in the Middle-East, Osama bin Laden, the leader of al-Qaeda, struck the World Trade Center and the Twin Towers on September 11, 2001. Bin Laden believed that America was overstaying their welcome in the two holiest sites of Islam: Mecca and Medina. Similar to the situation in Vietnam, there was an underlying message that the American way of life was threatening the Arabic and Islamic way of life. As a result, bin Laden declared “the duty of Jihad against the Enemy” to “expel the infidels from the Arab Peninsula,” referring to the American military occupation.[^20] Former Deputy National Security Advisor Ben Rhodes commented on the complication of declaring a “war on terror” under the Bush administration: “\[Bush] further defined the nature of the conflict by saying,... ‘They hate… a democratically elected government.’ To have this unfathomable event framed in a way that fit neatly into the American narrative… was reassuring. America had a new national purpose on par with the Cold War—another generational effort to make the world safe for democracy.”[^21] Rhodes realized that bin Laden did not ultimately hate America’s “democratically elected government,” but rather American foreign policy. Rhodes concluded, “Democracy was being debased, not promoted.”[^22] What followed after these events was the U.S. militarization in Middle-Eastern countries like Afghanistan and Iraq. This displayed the ultimate contradiction of the United States. America was supposed to be the valiant savior to bring peace and freedom to the people, but America could now be seen as the “terrorists” inflicting more chaos and oppression upon Middle-Eastern civilians. This was a peak moment when the distortion of self-determination, which was used as a motivation to protect the world from disorder, seemed to be in America’s self-interest. Etched into American ideals was the idea that America was the altruistic superpower, but now it seemed America was also a terrorist superpower.

Democracy seemed to be a guise for the decisions and policy making behind the American political curtain. While [[modernization]] enabled many to live in a more materially comfortable world, it has also played a major role in injecting an element of terror in modern lives. The effects of America’s domineering power can be seen in the consequences of globalization. The efforts to “export America” established soft power throughout nations. People around the world consumed American material and cultural products, which translated into an appreciation for America and Americans, but not necessarily one for the U.S. government. That seemed difficult for American officials to understand.[^23] It can be difficult to explain to a colonizer why colonization hurts, and it can be difficult to explain to a “freedom-fighter” why they can be seen as a “terrorist.” But what needs to be understood is that imposing the American way of life onto another nation’s way of life strips away a nation’s self-determination and nationalism. America had hoped for these ideas of self-determination and nationalism, at least in the beginning, for a world of peace and order. Nowadays, it seems that America has strayed from their mission for liberty and democracy, and it has created turmoil throughout the world.
