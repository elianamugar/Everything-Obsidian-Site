# Killing the Leviathan

After the chaos of the [[English Civil War]] and the [[Thirty Years War]] in the mid- to late-1600s, Thomas Hobbes wrote his _Leviathan_, a body of work which described the reasons for strong governmental control. Considering the conflicts he witnessed, Hobbes lost faith in human “goodness” because he believed that individuals acted upon their own accord. Hobbes was convinced that strong government would lead to stability and peace, the ultimate goal for society. Yet, after the [[Glorious Revolution]] in 1688, John Locke challenged Hobbes’ views and defined citizens’ inherent “natural rights.” Locke set up the basis for the [[Enlightenment]], when thinkers prioritized individualism for the betterment of society over institutional control. In turn, the idea that the government should rule on behalf of the people based on the principles of individual rights was introduced. From this point in history, thinkers debated the issue of how much control the government should have and how much control people have over their own lives. In the late 18th century, the shift of power from government to the people influenced political rule for the sake of human progress due to the newly discovered idea of individual rights, ultimately toppling conservatism in favor of different extremes of liberalism.

In the beginning of the [[French Revolution]] in 1789, the newly established [[National Assembly]] documented individual rights in _The Declaration of the Rights of Man and Citizen_, including “liberty, property, security, and resistance to oppression.” The National Assembly defined “liberty” as “consist\[ing] in the freedom to do everything which injures no one…” so people were free to exercise their rights under the limitations of law. This was one of the earliest works that explored the balance between the freedom of individual rights and laws established by the government to protect the people. Essentially, the National Assembly created a government that attempted to rule on behalf of the people rather than for a governmental agenda. This made the declaration a very liberal document, since it described liberty as a political value, individualism, skepticism of power, belief in rule of law, and limited government—the essential principles of liberalism. As a result, this opposed Hobbes’ conservative support for a strong central government because of the differing time periods and circumstances. Hobbes lived during a chaotic period hoping for peace and stability, while people overthrew the government during the French Revolution because of internal dissatisfaction rather than external wars. Although the National Assembly was short-lived, it was the beginning of political progress, weaving human rights into the governmental process.

Between 1791-1792, politician and philosopher Thomas Paine used the circumstance of the French Revolution to logically explore the question of rights and progress and wrote his _Rights of Man_ to counter British conservative Edmund [[Burke]]’s disapproval of the French Revolution. Paine covered individual rights within the scope of nature rather than politics and condemned conservative institutions, such as aristocracy and hereditary powers. Paine noted, “Government, on the old system, is an assumption of power, for the aggrandizement of itself; on the new a delegation of power for the common benefit of society. The former supports itself by keeping up a system of war; the latter promotes a system of peace, as the true means of enriching a nation.” Paine compared that the old way simply wanted power and war, rather than the new prospect for peace. Although conservatives like Burke and Hobbes trusted government control, Paine advocated for a liberal government that ruled for and by the people, rather than the upper-class, because the people held the ability to determine peace on their terms. This progression of political rule to accommodate for people’s inherent rights conveyed an optimistic outlook for humanity’s potential to keep improving society.

Then, during the [[Jacobin]] rule between 1793-1794, the [[Marquis de Condorcet]], a progressive Enlightenment thinker, wrote the _Sketch for a Historical Picture of the Progress of the Human Mind_, which was published posthumously. In the “Tenth Epoch” of his text, Condorcet hypothesized that the “perfection of the human species might be capable of indefinite progress.” During this period of ideological changes, thinkers built upon the idea of “indefinite progress” to improve society for the good of humanity. Condorcet implied that future generations will hold the responsibility for continuing to improve society—to do so, old ideals must be left in the past and newly established individual rights must be exercised to create a new liberal government for the sake of human progress. However, this idealistic goal for improving society led to havoc because of the forced means to reach the ironic goal of peace.

At the height of the French Revolution’s radical period, authority granted Maximilien [[Robespierre]], the leader of the Committee of Public Safety under the governing [[National Convention]], extensive executive power over France’s social and political developments. In his 1794 “Justification of the Use of Terror” speech, Robespierre declared, “...in order to lay the foundations of democracy… at the peaceful reign of constitutional laws, we must finish the war of liberty against tyranny and safely cross through the storms of the revolution.” Ironically, the “constitutional laws” were spearheaded by Robespierre himself, and the outcome of his actions was far from a “peaceful reign.” Robespierre justified his use of terror with the ultimate goal of liberty and democracy, calling it a “war against tyranny,” or the war against conventional ideals, for the sake of revolution. The use of terror to dissolve the old “religious veil” and other conservative obstacles standing in the way of human progress epitomized toppling conservatism in favor of radical liberalism—Robespierre essentially argued that the ends justify the means. Yet, this unruly destruction through a forced revolution was dangerous to society and stifled human progress by leading to chaos and instability rather than peace, which was against Condorcet’s expectations for the future. Robespierre’s [[Reign of Terror]] established the prominent rift between the past and the present—the great schism of conservatism and liberalism.

The basis of conservative ideals for a central government stemmed from a place of uncertainty and mayhem, while the basis of liberalism and democracy stemmed from the newfound idea of human rights. Although the goal of human progress was to improve society, achieving peace ultimately backfired, causing more pandemonium and disorder. The French Revolution was bloody because of its rapid and forced revolution, which demonstrated the need for a balance of how much human rights should influence political rule. The ideal “indefinite human progress” was a possibility until the Reign of Terror regressed that progress. A perfect, [[utopian]]-like society was an imagination during a time of great optimism and revolution, but today, our modern perspectives have made us realize that such a society cannot exist due to inherent human flaws. Consequently, in the present day, contemporary ideals come from a place of pessimism and anti-Enlightenment thinking. We don’t see history as a gradual march towards progress, but rather as an endeavor where some people win and some people lose—everything is a constant battle. As a result, we’ve lost faith in the idea of human progress, which could lead to a problematic and nihilistic future.
