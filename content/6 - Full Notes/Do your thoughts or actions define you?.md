# Do your thoughts or actions define you?

If the thoughts you have are *bad*, does that make you a *bad person*? Does that stain your entire reputation, even if those thoughts were in the past? In *The Drama* (2026) directed by Kristoffer Borgli, Zendaya and Robert Pattinson play a couple (Emma and Charlie) on the week leading up to their wedding. During a wine tasting with their friends, Emma's friend in particular (Rachel) asks Emma and Charlie a question that kickstarts the inciting action: *What's the worst thing you've ever done?*

Everyone responds. Mike (Rachel's husband) used his ex-girlfriend as a human shield against a dog. Rachel locked a "slow" kid in a closet for over a day and didn't tell anyone. Charlie cyberbullied someone. And Emma? She almost committed a school mass shooting. The important part is *almost*. She never did it. No action occurred. But the fact that she thought to do so and *almost* did it sparked outrage. Who it sparked outrage in is particularly interesting: Rachel.

Now here's my opinion. Fact: Everyone BUT Emma actually *did* their "worst thing." Opinion: Rachel's was the worst one! She even laughed about it while telling the story and she lied to the father of the kid who was looking for his son! There was a whole search party for him and they eventually found him, thank god, but the truth of the matter is, Rachel *did* lock a kid in a closet over a day. She actually took action. Even though the thought to lock the kid wasn't pre-meditated or the purpose behind that thought didn't really exist, she did a *visceral action*.

This can even stretch to Charlie's case. He *almost* cheated. But he didn't really. Is a little kiss cheating? Maybe. It's a bit gray there. But he didn't fully go through with it. *That* action is the most important. But yes, it's a bit questionable as to *why* and what the purpose behind that was (to me it seemed a mixture of purposeless and not being a great state of mind that put him in autopilot), but more on that [here](/6---Full-Notes/Does-the-purpose-of-your-actions-define-how-legitimate-your-actions-are?).

I think my conclusion is pretty clear here. I don't think having *bad thoughts* makes you a *bad person*. A lot of that is said in OCD forums, as many people have OCD thoughts and they believe they're a bad person because maybe they thought of wanting to kill someone. But these people don't take the actual action to do so. This is a concept that's also discussed in [[1984 (1949) by George Orwell]], this idea of *thoughtcrime*, where having *bad thoughts* is treated as supreme treason and, ultimately, being considered a *bad person* in society. It's a satirical idea, where obviously, simply having a thought isn't a crime. It adds to the whole interesting execution of *The Drama*, where literally everyone but Emma *is* the antagonist, but *within* the film, it's Emma who's the antagonist.

Another piece of literature *The Drama* reminded me of was [[Crime and Punishment (1866) by Fyodor Dostoevsky]], which explores the ideological, psychological, and moral divide between harboring evil thoughts and committing violent acts (this idea between abstract theory versus visceral action). The protagonist, Raskolnikov, discovers that mental justification for a crime cannot override the unbearable guilt and conscience resulting from its action. In this book, while a person isn't legally punished for thinking about a crime, Dostoevsky argues that harboring such ideologies is a profound moral failure and the source of ultimate corruption. This book executes its ideas well in word format, and that was one of my critiques of *The Drama*, is that it's severely limited by the film format. We don't know the thoughts of these characters, so we simply have to see what they say (and they don't say all their thoughts out loud) and how they react. Rachel very much takes Dostoevsky's stance of Emma having a moral failure and she cannot seek an retribution to fix this. Mike honestly does not care. And Charlie? Initially, he has the stance that I have where the past is in the past. It doesn't define you, nor do your past thoughts define you. The fact that Emma didn't take action is the best outcome for everyone... and at the end of the day, *nothing happened*. But he wavers under societal opinions, that being of moral superiority, crumbles under the weight of that. The anguish of his belief versus Rachel's (and Dostoevsky's) causes him to have a breakdown on his wedding day.

And now we've left out Emma in the discussion. More than just a catalyst, she also suffers from societal judgment. Through *Crime and Punishment*, Dostoevsky claims *bad thoughts* can create their own *punishment* through guilt, mental anguish, and alienation from humanity. And in every scene where Charlie pesters Emma about her past, it's apparent she feels this way. She feels guilty, she feels the mental anguish, and she feels the alienation she once felt as a child and it surfaces to the present. She doesn't *become* that child again, but there are moments where she's reliving her childhood and being viewed as that child which no one dared to pay attention to *during* her childhood.

So, I'll say it again. Your past doesn't define you, nor should it. Your past thoughts don't define you. People can change. There is a separation between *thoughts* and *beliefs*, in which beliefs are more rooted to your core being and guide your decision-making on how to be a human, but even that can change. But *doing* good things and taking actions *do* define you. If Emma *did* do the mass shooting, that would be a different story. But at the end of the day, she didn't – and the world is much better off for it, no matter how that happened.
