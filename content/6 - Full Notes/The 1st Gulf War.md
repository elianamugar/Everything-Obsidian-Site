# The 1st Gulf War

The Iran-Iraq war had been going on for 8 years, from 1980-1988. Saddam Hussein, the Sunni Muslim military dictator of Iraq, had complications in Iraq. Iraq’s majority Muslim group are the Shi’ites and Sunnis are the minority--Hussein is a part of the minority. Most of the Shi’ite tribes are in southern Iraq and Hussein doesn’t trust them and treats them abysmally. Meanwhile, in northern Iraq, the Kurds (their own ethnic group) make up another large percentage of the Iraqi population. 

After Shah Pahlavi's radically progressive changes in Iran, he had to flee. Taking his place was Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini, a very religious and conservative leader. In 1979-1980, Khomeini’s mission is to restore Islam and tradition in Iran, calling Iran an Islamic state. Khomeini then calls for an exporting of the Islamic revolution. This worried Hussein because Khomeini is a Shi’ite--this means that the exporting of Islamic resolution had the potential to strike accord with the majority Shi’ites in southern Iraq. On top of this, most Persian Gulf countries were majority Sunni with Shi’ite majorities and they all feared that Khomeini’s exporting of the revolution would cause revolt against the Sunnis. Hussein, aware of broken US-Iran relations during the Carter presidency, remembered that Iran relied on US weapons. But now that Iran didn’t have US weapons, they were vulnerable--Iraq could attack. Khomeini had too many enemies: the US, the USSR, and the Arab world (Khomeini and majority of Iran was Persian). In September 1980, Hussein decided to attack Iran. The small rationale was to expand Iraq’s borders for oil, but the larger rationale was to get rid of Khomeini. The war was brutal, and even included chemical weapons--it ultimately ended in a stalemate with both sides’ economies wrecked. In August 1988, the war ended.

During the Iran-Iraq war, Hussein was backed by Kuwait and Saudi Arabia but it was established that these were loans to Hussein. When the Iran-Iraq war ended in August 1988, Kuwait and Saudi Arabia demanded repayment from Hussein. Hussein was shocked by the demands because the Iraqi economy was absolutely devastated. But Hussein was reasonable and suggested some conditions: if you want me to be able to repay you, you must reduce your oil production so that the oil prices will rise, then I can sell my oil at a higher price to gain capital to pay. Though this was logical, Saudi Arabia and Kuwait refused. As a result, Hussein reached out to George H.W. Bush since the US is an ally at this point.

In 1990, Hussin asked a US ambassador about how to solve the Kuwait and Saudi Arabia issue. The US ambassador, by H.W. Bush’s order vaguely tells Hussein “to do what you need to do” with the Kuwait and Saudi Arabia issue. Hussein perceives this message as a go-ahead to do whatever he wants. In August 1990, Hussein sent troops to Kuwait. Kuwait is defenseless, so Iraq quickly takes it over. H.W. Bush is angry and worried--Iraq now controls 20% of the world’s oil. The next target for oil on the Persian Gulf for Iraq was Saudi Arabia, which currently controlled 15% of the world’s oil. H.W. Bush announced that Saudi Arabia contacted the US for protection and H.W. Bush sent troops to Saudi Arabia. During the process, H.W. Bush was angry because this was not the way to solve issues in the post-Cold War world. War, destruction, and the military are not the answers. If there were issues, the first response should be to use diplomacy and peace to solve it. Ironically, in August 1990, H.W. Bush aggressively sends in a coalition of NATO, Egypt, Syria, Saudi Arabia, Japan, and the USSR into Saudi Arabia to protect it, marking the 1st Gulf War (Operation Desert Shield).

In January 1991, H.W. Bush and the coalition launched a massive bombing campaign against Iraq. Then, in February 1991, H.W. Bush and the coalition continued their bombing campaigns and launched attacks into Kuwait to drive out Iraqi forces (Operation Desert Storm). After 100 hours, the war ends and the Iraqi troops lose--a big victory for the coalition and a big loss for Iraq. After Kuwait’s freedom from Iraq, the coalition forces head to southern Iraq, but H.W. Bush decided to stop the coalition from advancing further. Bush did not want to overthrow Hussein because of 4 reasons.

Reason 1: Hussein has Arab allies and Bush’s coalition has Arab allies. The overthrowing of Hussein would threaten the Arab world, setting a bad precedence of overthrowing dictators. The coalition Arab allies clarify to Bush that they don’t want to get rid of Hussein because allied coalition dictators don’t want to be overthrown themselves. Reason 2: The US would be responsible for Iraq after overthrowing Hussein. Bush was well aware of the chaos with the Vietnam War after the killing of Ngo Dinh Diem and the US’s responsibility for Vietnam. Reason 3: after the overthrow of Hussein, Iraq could fracture and that might benefit Iran (US’s enemy). If Hussein is overthrown, southern Iraq in which the Shi’ite Muslim majority lives, will look toward Iran for support and try to secede. Although this was realistically unlikely due to the Arab and Persian differences between Iran and Iraq, this was still a valid US fear. Reason 4: The Kurds situation. Based on reason 3, if Iraq starts to fracture, then the Kurds in northern Iraq will want independence. The worry was focused on the large number of Kurds based in Turkey, a significant NATO ally due to its location and its air bases, missiles, etc. If the Kurds flow into Turkey, there might be a revolution which might have chaotic repercussions. As a result, the US troops stay in Saudi Arabia in order to defend it from Iraq/Hussein’s grab for oil. The stationing of US troops in Saudi Arabia angers many people, leading to the creation of al-Qaeda and forming the basis of Osama bin Laden’s angry rhetoric--the US doesn’t deserve to be on the Islamic holy lands of Mecca and Medina.
