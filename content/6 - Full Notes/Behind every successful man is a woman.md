# Behind every successful man is a woman

In many parts of the play, [[Aristophanes]] confirms how men of Greece view women, but Aristophanes presents his overall message that women can be equal to men in some instances. Ultimately, Aristophanes undermines the Greek men’s view of women by depicting both genders collaborating, making the central part of the text about brains and brawn working together rather than one over the other.

The gender stereotypes that the men hold are both reaffirmed and contradicted by the women in the play. For the majority of the play, the women acknowledge their stereotypes. When Lysistrata is laying the groundwork for stopping the Peloponnesian war and explaining her plan to Calonice, Calonice asks, “But how can women achieve anything so grand or noble? What do we ever do but sit at home looking pretty, wearing saffron gowns and make-up… and giant slippers?” (43-46). In a way, Calonice confesses the flaws of women, claiming that women are essentially useless and makes it sound as if meddling in the situation is an impossible feat. In another instance, Lysistrata puts down a slave of their own kind: “Scythaena! What are you staring at? Put that shield face down in front of us” (182-185). Lysistrata’s tone displays a lack of respect for slave women. If Aristophanes truly wanted to represent women empowerment, hypothetically, he would write this part differently, maybe demonstrating that they treat each other fairly and equally despite social status. Yet, by introducing these flaws, Aristophanes makes the passive sexism in ancient Greek society apparent to the audience. These are the very flaws that Aristophanes turns into instruments for the women to stop the Peloponnesian wars.

Despite the women confirming their stereotypes, the women take advantage of the men’s views of them. In rallying the women to take a stand against the men to stop the war by refusing to have sex, Lysistrata replies to Myrrhine’s concern of being beaten by saying, “Just make yourself frigid…. Make life a misery for them, and they’ll give up trying soon. No man is ever going to get satisfaction if the woman doesn’t choose that he should” (163-166). Lysistrata has found the weak point of men: their appetite for sex. In essence, it is their Achilles heel; the women’s collective decision to practice abstinence, for the time being, is what will make “life a misery for them.” This plays into the logistics of stopping the war since the men will be too distracted to satisfy their appetite for sex and focus on trying to please the women. After the women take their oath of chastity, Myrrhine proclaims, “We must show that it’s not for nothing that people say ‘Damn and blast, but there’s no getting the better of a woman!’” (252-254). Interestingly, Myrrhine mentions that the general people say that “there’s no getting the better of a woman,” which shows that ancient Greek society somewhat believes that it’s hard to take advantage of a woman. If this is the case, then Aristophanes presents the case that women are capable of stepping up and taking responsibility to protect the greater good from the Peloponnesian wars.

The views of the men about women change somewhat over the course of the play. The change occurs simply because the men are lustful, only desiring sex. To fulfill their wishes, they have to comply with women’s orders. This shows that Aristophanes presents this as merely a temporary necessity to end the war. Towards the end of the work, when a resolution has been reached between the Spartan and Athenian males, Lysistrata says, “Spartans, here are your wives back” (1274). The men got what they wanted. When Lysistrata says “here are your wives back” it sounds like the wives are being returned. If it is implied that the wives are being returned, then that means the women were initially taken away, then put back in their place. Therefore, it’s implied that Aristophanes writes the men’s change of view of women as a short-term and temporary change in order to have a drive for the turning point of the plot. It was just a convenient part of the plot structure, nothing more, nothing less. In the end, men still view women as a “plaything” that they desire, only following the women’s commands so that they can have them. Underlyingly, the men don’t think of the women any differently than before, even if the ending has the illusion of teamwork and unity between the two genders.

Although it seems that Aristophanes confirms the idea that Greek women are submissive, the end’s ambiguous ending still demonstrates that women can be a part of a bigger solution, which is progressive from how they are usually depicted. Aristophanes uses the stereotypes of women and turns those into their tool, so in a way, it’s progressive because it is one of the earliest works that depict women as asserting their wit among the table of men at such a crucial time. At the end of the play, an actor playing Athena would come out, representing the statue of the goddess. This may show a display of female dominance and empowerment at the end, but that is an undermined message because a male actor would be portraying the statue of Athena. The assumed underlying message is that it takes both brains and brawn as well as men and women to solve societal problems, which explains the combination of a man representing Athena. And for the time of ancient Greece, this was a liberal societal view; Aristophanes elevated the views of women up to the level of men.
