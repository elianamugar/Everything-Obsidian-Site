# Laissez-faire capitalism - critiques from Mill and Marx

Laissez-faire capitalism translates to letting the economy run on its own, free of government interference. The laws of supply and demand would occur naturally, and as a result, the greatest happiness would occur for the greatest number of people (in theory). This was an idea inspired by Adam Smith’s critique of [[mercantilism]], the prevailing economic theory at the time. Although Smith wanted to improve upon the unfavorable conditions of mercantilism, there were issues with the pragmatics of the theory. Smith warned against monopolies and corruptness occurring because of the notion that people are inherently selfish. In short, some people will take advantage of the system. Marx and Mill attacked these issues of laissez-faire capitalism.

John Stuart Mill offered an improvement upon laissez-faire capitalism. He did not believe all of capitalism was “bad,” he just wanted to implement an idea of “good” capitalism through some form of “restriction.” Ultimately, to reform the current system, Mill asked the utilitarian question, “Does the action ensure happiness for the greatest number of people?” Mill proposes a solution to the faults of laissez-faire capitalism—sanctions. External sanctions, such as laws, would help keep corrupt capitalism in check but Mill’s emphasis was on internal sanctions. If you change from within, you can change on the outside. This was Mill’s prevailing idea because of his belief that human nature was inherently selfish, and that we would need to change from the inside in order to affect the outside world with good intentions. That is, to contribute to the general social welfare instead of private welfare.

Karl Marx almost opposed capitalism altogether. Marx admits that in the early stages of small capitalism, private property is good because the capital equates to the labor. But in late capitalism, private property becomes bad because the gap between workers and capitalists becomes too big to maintain. The labor is no longer personal and the capital is greed. The wealth accumulates in fewer hands, which means more misery is shared among more people, and the longer this goes on, the more inevitable a revolution is. As a result, inequalities and inequities will occur in distribution of wealth and the economy as a whole. So, to fix this whole problem, Marx proposes that if you get rid of capitalism (the condition of living), then greed and inequalities will disappear as a whole.
