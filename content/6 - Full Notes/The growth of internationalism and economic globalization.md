# The Growth of Internationalism and Economic Globalization

During both world wars, there were enemies and victors. Unfortunately, [[Germany]] always seemed to be on the enemy side. In order to bring resolve to these conflicts, there was a complication of how to deal with Germany. The historical events that followed the world wars were dependent on the treatment of Germany. Only in hindsight would historians be able to assess when the faults and mistakes occurred. But amidst those faults and mistakes, there were also improvements. In the aftermath of the two world wars, there was a growing sense of internationalism, the advocacy of cooperation and understanding between nations. After World War I, there was a limited sense of international collaboration while after World War 2, there was a more inclusive internationalism that mostly focused on defense pacts. Due to America’s role as a global superpower, their obligation to gain support from Western powers and fragile nations ultimately led to the natural progression of globalization, which impeded many nations’ ways of life.

After World War I, many nations discussed the consequences and reparations of the chaos in Paris. At the Paris Peace Conference, it was apparent that high expectations were placed on the U.S. President [[Woodrow Wilson]] to bring peace and order. Wilson formed a coalition with French Prime Minister [[Georges Clemenceau]], British Prime Minister [[David Lloyd George]], and Italian Prime Minister [[Vittorio Emanuele Orlando]].[^1] Named the “Big Four,” they held a lot of responsibility for Articles written in the [[Treaty of Versailles]], which also meant they had control over the desired outcomes for each of their nations. One of Wilson’s desires was to see the creation of the “[[League of Nations]],” an international body that was supposed to “promote international cooperation… by the acceptance of… honourable relations between nations by the firm establishment of the understandings of international law… and by the maintenance of justice and a scrupulous respect for all treaty obligations in the dealings of organised peoples with one....”[^2] Wilson’s intentions were to create an international body that could civilly solve disputes before another world war occurred as well as collaborate on certain world issues. But what unfolded was many nations’ needs were neglected. Italy wanted ports, [[Japan]] wanted a ban on racial discrimination, and [[China]] wanted their territory protected from the encroaching Japan. None of these nations got what they wanted.[^3] To make matters worse, the creation of Middle Eastern countries, such as [[Iraq]], [[Yugoslavia]], and [[Palestine]], led to many current international problems.[^4] In this sense, there was a limited scope of internationalism. The “Big Four” held a lot of power and collaborated with each other, but they limited the collaboration and interaction with other influential nations. The Big Four isolated themselves from the rest of the other nations, especially with regard to Germany. Even when Germany gave counter-proposals, which Clemenceau was inclined to accept, the Big Four was not willing to budge.[^5] Wilson was a proponent of leniency towards Germany, but Clemenceau and David Lloyd George were committed to brutally punishing Germany for their own personal and national reasons.

In contrast to the aftermath of World War I, after World War II, U.S. President Franklin Deleanor Roosevelt ([[Franklin D. Roosevelt]]) set out to create a more successful international body—the United Nations. To prevent the shortcomings and pitfalls of the League of Nations, Roosevelt and other collaborating countries decided to establish this international body in 1945 before World War II came to an official end.[^6] Unfortunately, Roosevelt died before seeing the [[United Nations]] come to fruition. As a result, U.S. President [[Harry S. Truman]] delivered the speech to the San Francisco Conference of the United Nations where he emphasized the importance of international collaboration: “If we seek to use \[the United Nations] selfishly—for the advantage of any one nation or any small group of nations—we shall be equally guilty of that betrayal…. we must deny ourselves the license to do always as we please.  No one nation, no regional group, can or should expect, any special privilege which harms any other nation.”[^7] Truman’s speech extended the Wilsonian ideals established during World War I and the creation of the League of Nations. Much like the League of Nations, the United Nations was established to promote international collaboration and civil dispute before another large-scale war broke out. Unlike Wilson, Roosevelt and Truman were not pushovers during this creation period—they heavily asserted that no one nation would hold an unreasonable amount of power over other nations. Yet, there were still imperfections with the United Nations; Roosevelt and Truman did not foresee the consequences and abuses of special veto powers within the Security Council. 

Still, after World War II, the territorial split-up of Germany caused tensions between the Western powers and the Soviet Union. As a result, the North Atlantic Treaty Organization in 1949 was created as a defense pact. If one nation was attacked, then all nations within [[NATO]] were considered under attack.[^8] In response to NATO, the Soviet Union ([[USSR]]) created the [[Warsaw Pact]], the Soviet version of NATO. The main difference between the two organizations was that NATO membership was voluntary while the Warsaw Pact was mandatory within the Soviet Union and other Eastern Bloc allies.[^9] In terms of internationalism, this was a shift in collaboration—there were two different sides, both on the defense against one another, using internationalism. More nations were involved, but in a way, it was still a limited sense of internationalism. Democratic governments voluntarily cooperated with similar nations, while the communist nations were forced to cooperate with each other. Essentially, internationalism was more inclusive after World War II and was more used for defense measures, but it did fulfill the goal of safety and peace for the most part—nuclear warfare and atomic bombs did not kill people in mass numbers.

Transitioning into the [[Cold War]], the United States took the opportunity to win over nations with economic influence and “soft power.” It began when U.S. Secretary of State [[George Marshall]] proposed his “[[Marshall Plan]],” saying it was the American people’s responsibility “to save Europe from starvation and chaos…” while preserving “the glorious heritage of a free America.”[^10] This gave America the upper-hand in foreign policy, establishing a moral obligation from other nations to respect America’s dominance and “one-world” order. America’s quest for international influence was just beginning and soon led to America’s international operation of economic cooperation. During the existence of the Berlin Wall, the United States Air Force, along with the Royal Air Force, delivered 2.3 million tons of supplies in East Berlin from between 1948 and 1949.[^11] The sentiments from the Berlin Airlift and the Marshall Plan seemed to translate into the idea of “exporting America” where America’s efforts to provide global aid became a way for America to hold onto their economic power and gain more leverage on the international stage with their influence. The Soviet Union was threatened by this idea. People around the world began to consume American products and the Soviet Union could not compete.[^12] Ultimately, America was able to secure their dominance as a global superpower in the fight to counter communism.

The growth of internationalism led to the natural progression to economic globalization. Because internationalism was to strive for global peace and cooperation, Western powers, including the United States, began a movement of economic cooperation. In this global effort to support other nations’ economies and prevent a global depression, American products started making their way into countries around the world. Though this was a promotion of American culture, this impeded on many nations’ ways of life. Nations whose economies depended on local businesses, local foods, and local products, were soon touched by American corporations. Now, there’s a promotion of capitalism in many nations, including third-world countries. Coca-Cola stands, cheap American toys, and American fast food chains have been ridden with the American way of life.
