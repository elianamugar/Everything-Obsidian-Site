# Automation armageddon - do we have a mind of our own?

With smart technology integrating into our everyday lives by replacing simple and mundane tasks, some people speculate that this can replace their jobs. In _Black Mirror_, the episode “White Christmas” depicts technological advancements that can deter or improve home life, relationships, and police interrogations by extracting the human mind. In this world, Matthew, a charismatic man, makes his living through jobs that require people skills. Matthew tries to coax Joe, a murderer, to admit his crimes in a demonstration for the police. In Sue Halpern’s article, “How Robots & Algorithms Are Taking Over,” she explains how automation subtracts humans from the job market because technology will be more cost-effective and efficient, opposing _Black Mirror_’s message. Both the article and episode discuss the issue of technological advancements, criticizing the government and economic systems that control technology, with different intentions. Although Halpern argues that robots and algorithms will take over the job market, “White Christmas” implies that there will be jobs that technology cannot replace through Matthew’s character, strongly suggesting that human jobs will not go extinct.

In Halpern’s article, she believes technology creates competition for humans because technology’s improvements will out-do human flaws. When explaining the tragic middle-class job loss during the economic collapse of 2008, Halpern quoted a 2013 article by the Associated Press: “Most of the jobs will never return, and millions are more likely to vanish as well… They’re being obliterated by technology.”[^1] People’s fears make sense because we’re blindly trusting ambitious entrepreneurs of the technology industry with the responsibility of how much robots and algorithms will replace human jobs. Because producing better robots and algorithms for efficiency makes more money, companies are willing to sacrifice human jobs for the sake of profit. With the work ethic of humans in these tech companies, Halpern says that “we would be architects of our own obsolescence, replaced by our machines.”[^2] If our idea of success is to discover and push the boundaries of what is previously known, then humanity will create the technology to replace themselves because that is considered improvement. One creation that humans have already created are “social robots.” Halpern cites John Markoff of _The New York Times_ who said social robots are “robot\[s] with a little humanity.”[^3] If these robots have a little humanity, then people’s fears that robots can not only replace jobs, but also replace humanity. A robot with a little humanity can evolve to a robot full of humanity without errors, which means automation would terrifyingly dominate the world. Halpern argues that humans should fear the impending doom of robots and other unimaginable advanced technology. In the unforeseeable future, does that mean there will be no more jobs left for humans?

In contrast to Halpern’s argument, “White Christmas” establishes that humans will still be needed for certain jobs requiring emotional intelligence, which technology cannot replace. Throughout the episode, technology facilitates different ways of communication and jobs, which Matthew takes advantage of. Matthew’s first job consists of being a hook-up technical support guru who assists Harry, a young man, to find a partner using advanced eye technology that allows Matthew to see from Harry’s perspective.[^4] Then, Matthew becomes a liaison, explaining to the “cookie” —a device that is run by a copy of a person’s mind to control smart home settings—what is happening to them; Matthew is tech support for advanced technology.[^5] Matthew’s final job is a sort of salesman, demonstrating how his services and the use of technology can be beneficial for interrogations.[^6] Matthew’s existence shows that there needs to be people that understand people—technology cannot do everything. People will still be needed for certain jobs. Instead of fearing the depletion of jobs, Matthew uses technology as a tool to promote his career and takes advantage of the capitalistic society that rewards such behavior. 

Yet, there are downsides to his career due to human error. As a hook-up guru, Matthew ultimately gets Harry killed and gets permanently “blocked” from his family.[^7] Furthermore, after his successful demonstration to the police, Matthew ends up confessing his own crimes in the process of getting Joe, a murderer, to confess his crimes. Consequentially, the police charge Matthew for committing crimes of providing illegal services and failing to report a murder; his punishment is being blocked by everyone.[^8] Technology has the potential to correct these errors, such as not being able to completely read people and committing wrongdoings, Halpern says. If these errors can be fixed by technology, then the negative consequences, such as Matthew’s repercussions, can disappear, which can improve society. Although it is uncertain whether technology can truly replace human flaws, it creates an opportunity for such “perfect” technology to exist. But that does not mean human jobs will go extinct. Matthew’s careers consist of being charismatic, talking to people, and understanding them. Some jobs require human-to-human connection which technology might not ever replace. Even if technology can replace those jobs, humans are creative and can find ways to invent new jobs like Matthew; there may even be unthinkable jobs in the future that are difficult to envision today.

Both “White Christmas” and Halpern explore future job outlooks, but they also delve into how institutions can take advantage of people. In “White Christmas,” the mind is depicted as a separate entity, one that speaks on its own. The final scene reveals the police have been interrogating Joe for two days, but he has not talked, and shows Joe sitting in his prison cell.[^9] While Joe was close-mouthed, his mind spoke for itself—the “cookie” tech was used on Joe to confess his crimes. Based on the scene, the mind becomes intellectual property that is no longer private and can be extorted. The message is clear: we can be punished for thinking. This is what humanity should be wary of more than job prospects—this scene shows an invasion of privacy and, most importantly, our inner selves. Claiming that “we” should take control of technology, rather than the other way around, is optimistic. The question is, who is included in the “we”? Halpern says, “The priorities are clear: money first, people second…. Government has too many constituent parts that have their own, sometimes competing, visions of the technological future. Business, of course, is self-interested and resists regulation.”[^10] The government and corporations will direct the technology and humanity’s job prospects. So, what is left for the people? The Silicon Valley industrialists may claim that getting a grasp on technology is for our own security, but they could be the ones invading the people’s privacy and sense of self.

Technology will only keep developing—so what are we, the lone wolves, supposed to do? Instead of fearing that the government, companies, robots, and algorithms will take over human jobs, we need to believe in humanity that the people have control over their futures. We can prevent the automation apocalypse. If we don’t hold these systems and entities accountable for their actions imposed on the people, it won’t be the robots and algorithms taking over the people—it’ll be the people’s fault for letting them infiltrate our jobs and privacy. But if the lofty goal to save humanity seems like too much of a burden and effort, then perhaps Matthew’s advice to the “cookie” is one we should follow: “It’s easier if you comply.”[^11]
