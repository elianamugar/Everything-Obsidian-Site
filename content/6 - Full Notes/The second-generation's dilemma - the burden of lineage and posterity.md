# The second-generation's dilemma - the burden of lineage and posterity

The first-born, second-generation American child grows up questioning their duty and culture. If the family culture and American culture differ drastically, which one does the child pledge their duty to? If the child is an individual, who are they? The child’s personal dilemma between their collective family duties versus the American individual self introduces a difficult question: is the child the makeup of their family’s desires and needs, an individual with their own desires and needs, or both? As a Marxist, German playwright Bertolt Brecht believes in the collective rather than the individual; only the social, political, and economic conditions of living determine an individual. Conversely, Danish philosopher Søren Kierkegaard believes that an individual is “above the group,” which is only justified above ethics. Despite their contrasting definitions of the individual, both Brecht and Kierkegaard agree that individuality has its costs. To a first-born, second-generation American, the cost is great.

To show the advantages of the collective, Brecht attacks the detriments of capitalist individuality in his play, _The Good Woman of Setzuan_. Capitalism rules the setting of the play—the streets are impoverished squalors and the people are selfish and greedy. At the beginning of the play, the three gods look for a place to stay but to no avail, believing the world is immoral. Shen Te, the good-natured impoverished prostitute who attempts to remedy poverty and homelessness, willingly hosts the three gods and disproves their original notion of an immoral world. As a reward, the gods give her enough money for a small tobacco shop. Transforming from a “victim of her environment” to an active capitalist, her dilemma between remaining good-natured or a merciless capitalist shatters Shen Te. Although she generously allows the homeless to live in her shop and sells her products at marked-down affordable prices, the unsustainable business model withers in the capitalist world. To survive as a capitalist, Shen Te must be self-reliant for personal success and economic gains. How does Shen Te balance being altruistic to the poor while enduring the cutthroat capitalist world? The question is a paradox: to be altruistic means to be selfless, while being a capitalist means to be selfish. If the question must be answered, it must be answered on Brecht’s ambiguous terms—if the end goal is to be altruistic, then the means of disguising as a capitalist justifies the end. The paradoxical solution leads to the split existence of Shen Te’s alter-ego, the ruthless tobacco mogul Shui Ta who protects Shen Te’s capital interests. Shui Ta bribes, wards off the freeloaders in the shop, and commits other corrupt actions as the “supreme master of his environment.” If the means justify the ends, then Shui Ta’s existence would be temporary. Yet, as the play progresses, Shui Ta’s existence gradually cements. Instead of the means justifying the ends, the loss of Shen Te represents the loss of purpose; the means of personal gain becomes the fulfillment of life rather than pure generosity. The original plan to share the wealth by temporarily gaining wealth through greed became an everlasting scheme to selfishly keep the wealth. Brecht shares the ultimate price of the individual in a capitalist society: the individual erases the existence of goodness, and society splits between the successful and unsuccessful.

Contrary to Brecht, Kierkegaard places the individual on a pedestal compared to the insignificant collective. First, the individual exists for pleasure—the aesthetic level of existence. Regarded as the base level, if the individual does not advance to the ethical stage, then the individual is incapable of happiness. In other words, “hedonism [leads] to despair.” Most philosophers normally regard the next stage of existence—the ethical one—as the highest level, but not Kierkegaard. Here, Kierkegaard defines ethics as the collective’s morals and values, which are the constraints of an ethical person. Once an individual surpasses the ethical through the “teleological suspension of morality,” the individual reaches the rarest, most superior religious stage only attained through faith. Through an absolutely direct connection to God, the individual temporarily transcends ethics and becomes obligated to God’s commands; the individual transforms from an ethical being to a True Individual, then returns to abiding by universal ethics once the connection ends. Individuality would cease to exist if transcending ethics and morals were impossible, because everyone would follow the same moral principles. Kierkegaard’s example of Abraham as The First Individual and Father of Faith demonstrates the cost of such individuality. When Abraham was called by God to sacrifice his son Isaac, he entered the teleological suspension of morality and created a direct tether to God. Kierkegaard emphasizes his disclaimer: murder is immoral according to the Ten Commandments, the standard code of ethics. Therefore, following God’s will to murder is immoral. In this case, Abraham is the exception; within his personal experience he does not hesitate to incomprehensibly sacrifice Isaac by complete resignation to God. Abraham’s state of suspended ethics establishes his true individuality. Although Kierkegaard highly regards the individual’s journey as the most extraordinary achievement, the drastic rift between Abraham’s incomprehensible personal moment and the external ethical world is too great. In the eyes of the ethical collective, Abraham is murderous—he is the outcast. But in the eyes of God, Abraham is faithful. Through these starkly contrasting perceptions of Abraham, Kierkegaard warns of the individual’s high cost: the unhappiness which results from social alienation.

Both philosophers agree there is a cost to individuality, but how do their ideas of the individual versus the collective relate to the first-born second-generation American child? The child proves the costs. During his lifetime, Brecht lived in various places ranging from his hometown in Germany to Southern California to Scandinavia. His belief in the collective meant that his nomadic immigrant-like lifestyle did not affect his philosophy. He was not an individual; he was only part of a collective humanity. Therefore, Brecht did not have the same philosophical struggles as the child. But through Brecht, one can understand the child’s dilemma. The child’s collective is not just their family and household culture, but their family’s past, present, and future. Under this strain, the child is not an individual, but rather an extension of the collective. Whatever success the child earns on their own is, ironically, not their own success—it is the success of the family, the household, and the collective. While the child’s household values promote interdependence, America breeds the ideas of capitalism and independence. From the American environment’s influence, the child grows up learning of individual success and freedom. With these new ideas, how does the child satisfy the family’s monetary and traditional goals while striving for their own desires in America? Again, this presents a paradox which parallels Shen Te’s: how does the child balance being altruistic to the family while enduring the cutthroat capitalist world? Brecht would argue the child should commit to the family collective because that success is greater than the American one. The collective should only exist—not the individual. If the child rids the individual capitalist ideas, then the root of greed cannot exist. If the child works for the collective, then only the collective exists with no corruption in sight. Therefore, false consciousness transforms into class consciousness when the quantitative value changes into a qualitative one and a revolution occurs—the highest attainable achievement for the collective. But like _The Good Woman of Setzuan_, Brecht does not offer a clear solution to the child’s dilemma, only an idealistic goal. What exactly is the qualitative value that leads to “revolution”? Is it the child’s severing of the family's culture, or the American one? Does the revolution destroy both cultures? Does the revolution combine them into a new culture or a completely separate one? Through Brecht’s structure of thesis (Shen Te’s ethics), antithesis (Shui Ta’s economics), and synthesis (unstated), the child’s structure parallels Brecht’s: thesis (altruistic ethics for the family collective), antithesis (individual gains under the American environment), and synthesis (the child’s resolution for an undefined revolution). Brecht offers no explicit synthesis, ushering the audience to find their own solution of “a society in which people can be good _and_ yet survive, the reconciliation of economics with ethics.” Where does that leave the child? The child can either have an existential split similar to Shen Te and Shui Ta, or desert the capitalist system to eliminate the issues of evaluating the individual’s dignity and monetary value. If the child chooses the latter, then the problems of individual greed, corruption, and erasure of goodness would cease to exist. Under Brecht’s philosophy, the child should commit to familialism—the child’s collective.

On the contrary, if the child follows Kierkegaard’s philosophy, they would commit to leaving the family duty for their own path. The individual must live forward, never backward. But for the child, it is drastic. To leave a family’s culture, heritage, and history for a new path in America is a difficult burden that only American children of immigrants carry—the burden of the past, present, and future and the burden of lineage and posterity. In Kierkegaardian terms, the child’s burden equates to the difficulty of surpassing the ethical stage. Though never explicitly established, the child’s obligation to carry the “success” of the family line exists like an implicitly permanent chore. Kierkegaard highly regards the religious stage, but the child’s equivalent is freedom from _any_ duty except the duty to one’s true self. The child’s freedom from both household values _and_ American capitalistic values in search of the child’s own desires seems incomprehensible, but gratifying. Yet, this freedom leads to many uncertainties. Does the child even know what they desire? How can the child free itself from both the values of both the country they live in and the family they were born in? If the child is truly free, what values are left? If the child ever flees the values of society for their own path, the child is restrained to solitude and alienation. The family rejects the child for pursuing their own needs and desires, while the child seeks no solace in American values. No one can understand the child. The cost of alienation is also perhaps the child’s greatest reward—that is, only if the child’s will is to be their own individual.

As presented in the dilemma of the first-born second-generation American, there are always two worlds clashing: the family culture versus American culture and familialism versus individualism. Regardless of the child’s philosophical choice, it is clear that the philosophical struggle isolates the child. The family cannot understand the child’s struggle to balance the household culture with American culture and well-established Americans cannot understand the immigrant struggle to survive in a capitalist world while nurtured in familialism. Thus, the child begins to shatter. The child, left in quietude and stuck in a philosophical limbo, silently struggles and eternally contemplates both worlds. The ultimate alienation leads to an unfortunate cost: an ultra-independent child who cannot choose between their only options—embracing individuality or the collective. 

As for the general established philosophies of individuality, Brecht and Kierkegaard signify the other great costs: being an individual either leads to obliteration of the moral self or complete alienation from the external ethical world. Yet, Brecht and Kierkegaard dismiss the combined proposition at the intersection of their philosophies—the collective can support the child’s individual journey. If the collective demonstrates that compassionate individuals compose the collective’s backbone while supporting the child’s self-growth, the child will individually choose to reinvest in prosperity of the collective. Brecht counters this notion by stating that individuals are greedy and corrupt within the collective, but this only occurs when the inherent nature of the collective is based on competition. Greed only exists in the battle for superiority. Yet, when the collective plants the seeds of the child’s individual goodness, the inherent grounding of “greed and corruption” disappears because self-worth—the greatest contentment with the individual self—eliminates the concept of superiority. If the collective teaches the child to be a good individual, the child learns to equally value the self and the collective. Though Brecht and Kierkegaard offer the child one of two seemingly ideal choices, they overlook a truly ideal paradigm which exists between both philosophies—the child can trust their individual self _and_ the collective.
