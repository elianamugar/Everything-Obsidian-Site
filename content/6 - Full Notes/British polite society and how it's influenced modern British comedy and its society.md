# British polite society and how it's influenced modern British comedy and its society
In my required Semantics & Pragmatics course at BU for my Linguistics degree, one fundamental principle we learned about was [*Grice's maxims*](https://www.sas.upenn.edu/~haroldfs/dravling/grice.html) , which were a set of principles that guide cooperative communication in everyday conversations. Before I cover the four maxims, who was the man who made them? It's a British man! Specifically, [Paul Grice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Grice#:~:text=Paul%20Grice%2C%20or%20Paul%20Grice,the%20philosophical%20study%20of%20semantics.), a British philosopher of language who created the theory of the cooperative principle (Gricean maxims), which become foundational concepts in the linguistic field of [pragmatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatics) (how context contributes to meaning and how human language is utilized in social interactions). He was also important in other ways in the field of linguistics, but we'll hone in on just these relevant facts.

In 1975, Grice introduced his four conversational maxims in his work *Logic and Conversation*, which explain how effective communication happens everyday. Here are the four maxims:
>1. **The maxim of quantity** – one tries to be as informative as one possibly can, and gives as much information as is needed, and no more.
>2. **The maxim of quality** – one tries to be truthful, and does not give information that is false or that is not supported by evidence.
>3. **The maxim of relation** – one tries to be relevant, and says things that are pertinent to the discussion.
>4. **The maxim of manner** – one tries to be as clear, as brief, and as orderly as one can in what one says, and where one avoids obscurity and ambiguity.

Breaking this conversational guidelines, according to Grice, can lead to misunderstandings, misinterpretations, or even the *intentional creation of implicature* (a hidden or implied meaning). When a speaker violates a maxim, they can be deliberately uncooperative, which can hinder effective communication. However, flouting a maxim (breaking it in a way that's obvious to the listener) can be used to create **humor**/**comedy**, ambiguity, express politeness, convey hidden/additional meanings, implicature (e.g. sarcasm or irony), and other linguistic effects.

It's very interesting that a British man came up with these rules considering.... *ENTER: British polite society*. Grice created these maxims post-polite society, but polite society's effects have rippled into British society. In British etiquette, there is an emphasis on courteousness and reservedness/anti-overfamiliarity. Courteousness directly relates to the conversational maxims of quantity and manner – you don't want to run your mouth talking about random topics and bore your guest! The reservedness also directly relates to the conversational maxims of manner and relation – you don't want to talk about topics outside of the sphere you're speaking in, so British people err on the side of safety. Another thing I noticed in *Pride and Prejudice* was the upper class's ability to tell partial truths, but not whole truths, which in itself breaks the maxim of quality. In the mixture of all of the social context of British society, it's easy to observe the restraint and suppression of language, which may be in regards to English etiquette and British polite society. This forces the people of the time, including the characters in *Pride and Prejudice*, to speak in subtext, which puts the characters in an absurd situation that forces them to break ALL the maxims.

Here are some moments in *Pride and Prejudice* that demonstrate these effects:
1. The violation of the maxim of quantity – speakers should provide appropriate amount of information in utterance.
	- Vol. 1, Ch. 2: After Mr. Bennet paid the visit to Mr. Bingley, Mrs. Bennet had no knowledge of it.
		>*Observing his second daughter employed in trimming a hat, he suddenly addressed her with, "I hope Mr. Bingley will like it, Lizzy."*
		>*"We are not in a way to know what Mr. Bingley likes," said her mother resentfully, "since we are not to visit."*
		>*"Impossible, Mr. Bennet, impossible, when I am not acquainted with him myself: how can you be so teasing?"*
		>*"What can be the meaning of that emphatic exclamation?"

		Mr. Bennet thinks that Mr. Bingley will like Lizzy's hat. Due to lack of knowledge of the visit, Mrs. Bennet is confused, then deems it impossible and ridiculous. Then, Mrs. Bennet becomes bored. It wasn't until Mr. Bennet confessed the truth that all the daughters showed surprise. Intended violation of the maxim of quantity corresponds with Mr. Bennet's eccentric personality – full of contradictions and conflicts. He likes to make impromptu comic gestures and remarks and use bitter sarcasm. He's a mixture of quick parts, sarcastic humor, reserve, and caprice.

2. The violation of the maxim of quality – speakers should maintain truth and adequate evidence in their utterance.
	- Vol 1, Ch. 5: When Darcy and Elizabeth met each other for the first time, Bingley was attracted by Jane's beauty. Bingley gives a high evaluation of Jane: 
	>	*"Oh, she is the most beautiful creature I ever beheld."*

	This is a tricky one. It may seem that Mr. Bingley is telling the truth, but individuals such as he tend to violate this maxim and say groundless words by figure of speech, such as hyperbole and irony. If that doesn't convince you, I'll point out that Mr. Darcy is the epitome of violating the maxim of quality. He ironically speaks of Jane, depicting his speaking manner as grumpy, proud, and grotesque because he's concerned about his social status. When he communicates with people, he likes to take control of the topic and content of conversations, preferring to provide less information rather than lie to hearers. But we all know by the end Mr. Darcy's true good-natured character.

3. The violation of the maxim of relevance – speakers should stay relevant to their discussion.
	- Vol. 1, Ch. 15: Elizabeth and Mr. Wickham meet.
	>Elizabeth: *What time is it?*
	>Mr. Wickham: *The bell has not rung yet.*

	For the above dialogue, it seems that the answer is not connected with the question. But as a matter of fact, Elizabeth understands that what Mr. Wickham means. Both A and B knows the bell rings at a certain time of day. Elizabeth asks the question to make sure that he is in time for whatever Mr. Wickham has on his schedule. And the utterance “the bell has not rung produced by Mr. Wickham means that his activity has not begun yet. In her violation of the maxim of relevance, she cleverly and wisely changes conversational topics without being offensive.

4. The violation of the maxim of manner – speakers should remain orderly and brief whilst avoiding obscurity and ambiguity in their utterances.
	- Vol 1, Ch. 19: Mr. Collins proposes to Elizabeth.
	>*Dear Miss Elizabeth, my attentions have been too marked to be mistaken. Almost as soon as I entered the house, I singled you out as the companion of my future life. But before I am run away with my feelings, perhaps I may state my reasons for marrying. Firstly, that is the duty of a clergyman to set the example of matrimony in his parish. Secondly, I am convinced it will add greatly to my happiness. And thirdly, that it is at the urging of my esteemed patroness, Lady Catherine, that I select a wife.*

	Mr. Collins' character is quite consistent throughout the book, causing many of the girls to laugh at his over-explanations without ever getting to the root of the topic. In this case, he explains three reasons for his proposal to Elizabeth, none of which are the immediate cause of his proposal, which violates the maxim.

And though I mark the point of maxim violations in the polite society era (it's so easy, given the context of the etiquettes they follow), it has snaked its way into modern British comedies! Take, for instance, [[Fleabag (2016) by Phoebe Waller-Bridge]]. 

1. Violation of maxim of quantity
	- Informal conversation between Fleabag and Cab Driver where Fleabag copes with a traumatic experience through the filter of humor.
	>	Fleabag: *It's a kind of funny story actually.*
	>	Cab Driver: *Oh, that's good! It'll keep me going. Shoot.*
	>	Fleabag: *I opened the café with my friend Boo.*
	>	Cab Driver: *Cute name.*
	>	Fleabag: *Yeah. She's dead now. She accidentally killed herself. It wasn't her intention, but it wasn't a total accident. She didn't actually think she'd die, she just found out that her boyfriend fucked someone else and wanted to punish him by ending up in hospital and not letting him visit her for a bit. She decided to walk into a busy cycle lane, wanting to get tangled in a bike, break a finger maybe. But as it turns out bikes go fast and flip you into the road. Three people died.*
	>	Fleabag laughs.
	>	Fleabag: *She was such a dick!*

	At least she didn't break the maxim of quality!

2. Violation of maxim of quality
	- When Claire gets a new haircut and she asks Fleabag's opinion on it, Fleabag can't utter the truth that it's ugly because that would hurt Claire's feelings. Fleabag manages to call it "chic."

3. Violation of maxim of relevance
	- When Fleabag is hanging out with the Hot Priest, she constantly breaks the fourth wall while he's talking to her, choosing to to the viewer about how great his arms look instead of paying attention to what the Hot Priest is talking to her about. They end up going to what the Hot Priest deems a fun activity: sitting in on a Quaker meeting, a place for quiet reflection and if you are compelled by some higher power to say something, you can share it, then leave. Fleabag has such a moment, saying:
	>	*"I sometimes worry that I wouldn't be such a feminist if I had bigger tits."*
	Thank you, Fleabag.

4. Violation of maxim of manner
	- At the beginning of Season 2, Episode 1, Fleabag's dad is stuttering and rambling on and on around the point that he loves everyone that's at the engagement celebratory dinner. He can't bother to say it out loud, so he says at the end of his speech: *"Very much."*

In connecting *Pride and Prejudice* (1813) and *Fleabag* (2016), this demonstrates the ripple effect British polite society has had in British comedy *and* its society.
