# Egypt and Israel in 1967-1973

In early 1967, Nasser (ruler of Egypt, military dictatorship) approached Syria and Jordan for a military alliance. Because of Nasser’s idea of pan-Arabism (free the Arabs from western/European powers), Nasser decided to attack Israel first. Also in early 1967, Nasser approached Brezhnev to ask for support. Since the USSR isn’t threatened by the US due to the US’s issues in Vietnam, Brezhnev agrees to provide weapons, advisors, supplies, etc. Brezhnev’s logic was that 3 countries would be attacking Israel, a western ally. In other words, Nasser and Brezhnev share a common enemy. In Spring 1967, the Egyptian-Jordanian-Syrian alliance was publicly announced worldwide. Simultaneously, Egypt cuts off the [[Suez Canal]] from Israeli shipping and Nasser tells the UN troops who’ve been in the Sinai Peninsula since 1956 to leave. Nasser has jurisdiction to do that because they’re in his Egyptian territory. Israel perceives these as dangerous moves, assuming that a war will happen even though Nasser’s moves are ambiguous and uncertain. As a result, Israel made the decision on June 5, 1967, to begin the [[Six Day War]] by wiping out air forces and launching simultaneous attacks against Egypt, Jordan, and Syria. By June 11, 1967, the Six Day War ended after the Egyptian-Jordanian-Syrian alliance told Israel to stop. As a result, Israel took all the territory (Sinai Peninsula, Gaza Strip, West Bank, Golan Heights) and that’s as far as Israel’s willing to go.

In 1967, the UN put together a resolution called the L[[and-for-Peace]], in which Israel must give up the land it took in exchange for peace treaties which recognize Israel. The reason behind this resolution was that the Arab world didn’t recognize Israel as a valid country due to its “invasion” of Palestinian Arabs. Ultimately, the Arab world rejects the Land-for-Peace resolution.

In September 1970, Nasser dies of a heart attack and Nasser’s 2nd-in-command takes over--Anwar el-Sadat. El-Sadat saw the internal problems of Egypt: Egypt was poor and was a worthless alliance. El-Sadat was an optimistic pragmatist; he saw Egyptian economic potential and he just needed to find sources of funding and some advisors. The issue was that the Egyptian people were devastated, and there was no confidence/morale until Egypt was restored. To solve this low morale issue, el-Sadat believed he had to start another war with Israel in which Egypt can have some victories against the Israeli military. El-Sadat’s reasoning was that Egypt needed a war that would pull in the US, so that the US can become a broker/diplomat between Israel and Egypt. The US’s role would be to step in, stop the conflict, the begin negotiations between Israel and Egypt. Though El-Sadat’s logic was a sound one, he also risked breaking the Egyptian-Jordanian-Syrian alliance. 

In early 1973, el-Sadat asked Syria and Jordan about their feelings towards mutually attacking Israel. Syria agrees (seeks vengeance from Six Day War), but Jordan rejects it (accepts loss from Six Day War). Soon after, el-Sadat visits Brezhnev to ask for support in attacking Israel, but Brezhnev rejects because it was the [[détente]] period (Brezhnev doesn’t want to start a war during the peace period). Brezhnev is aware of the triangulation occurring with the US-Vietnam situation; if the US perceived that the USSR is helping start a war against Israel (a US/western ally), then the US will want to seek support from the Chinese. To counter Brezhnev, el-Sadat threatens Brezhnev with a possible Egypt-US alliance. This would look bad against Brezhnev in the communist world, so he’s ultimately trapped--Brezhnev has to give into Egypt and support the war against Israel.

On October 6, 1973, on Yom Kippur (a major holy holiday), Syria and Egypt simultaneously attacked Israeli territory ranging from the Sinai Peninsula to the Golan Heights ([[Yom Kippur War]]). Leading up to the attack, Israel was aware that Egypt and Syria were moving troops and supplies, but they didn’t assume they would attack because they had just lost the Six Day War. After 3 days, Egypt and Syria win some victories and push Israel back, repairing some Egyptian morale. Syria retakes a piece of the Golan Heights. But then 3 days later, Israel pushes back again and retook all the territory they lost. The issue was the USSR was constantly resupplying Egypt and Syria, but no one was constantly resupplying Israel. [[Kissinger]] speaks to the Israeli Prime Minister who asked for resupplies. Kissinger was hesitant because this might anger the Arab world, which holds the oil which the West relies on. But the Israeli PM pressured Kissinger by suggesting that Israel would have to resort to nuclear war against Syria and Egypt. As a result, Kissinger agreed to resupply Israel on the condition that the US controls the Israel offense.

From October 21-22, 1973, Israeli troops went to the Syrian and Egyptian capitals and took the Suez Canal. El-Sadat and Syria ask for help from Brezhnev since they’re about to be conquered by Israel, so Brezhnev publicly announces that if Israel doesn’t stop attacking, then the Soviet Red Army will have to be brought in to attack Israel. Nixon counters Brezhnev by suggesting that the US will use nuclear bombs on Moscow if Brezhnev sends troops to Israel. This shows how a regional conflict can build up to being an all-out nuclear conflict. After these heightened tensions, Brezhnev and Nixon talk things out, and so do Kissinger and the Israeli PM. On October 25, 1973, the Yom Kippur war ended. Israeli troops were still on its gained territory and must return to Israel. The UN brings up the Land-for-Peace concept again and Kissinger tries to repair the situation with Egypt and Israel through [[Shuttle Diplomacy]].

From 1973-1976, Kissinger made several trips between Israel, Egypt, Syria, and Moscow. Shuttle Diplomacy was successful because Israel agreed to withdraw its troops and accept Land-for-Peace. After Kissinger visits el-Sadat and Jordan, they accept the Land-for-Peace, but after visiting Syria, Syria and the rest of the Arab world (including Palestine) still reject the Land-for-Peace. As a result of Shuttle Diplomacy, peace between Egypt and Israel was established; el-Sadat got the victories he wanted to boost Egyptian morale, and the rest of his plan worked--the US intervened for negotiations, which established an Egyptian-US alliance. In 1977, the Carter administration took office.

During the Carter presidency, Carter didn't prioritize the Middle East situation, which worried el-Sadat about the progress between Egypt and Israel. In December 1977, el-Sadat traveled to Israel to talk with Israeli PM Menachem [[Begin]]. The talks were rocky because they couldn’t trust each other to establish a treaty, so they decided to reach out to Carter to broker a peace treaty. In 1978, Carter invited el-Sadat and Begin to Camp David, but both sides couldn't get along; Carter had to individually talk with each one of them. El-Sadat was risking a lot by signing a peace treaty for Israeli recognition in the Arab world (the ultimate Arab betrayal). In March 1979, the Camp David Accords were officially signed by el-Sadat and Begin in Washington. In the accords, Israel would give back the Sinai Peninsula and el-Sadat would recognize Israel (Land-for-Peace); as a bonus, Israel and Egypt became top receivers of US assistance, implying a US-Egyptian alliance. The leftover territory to discuss was the Gaza Strip. Both sides don’t want it: there’s a lot of hatred towards Israel in the Gaza Strip and it’s too impoverished for Egypt to deal with. The Palestinians in the Gaza Strip hate both sides because they’re a shame to the Arab world, so el-Sadat decides that Israel has to negotiate with the Palestinians to create a Palestinian state. Begin accepts these conditions, but doesn’t put a time frame on it. After the signing of the peace treaty, el-Sadat was very unpopular in the Arab world and was seen as a traitor to Egypt and the Arab world. These feelings of hatred toward el-Sadat within Egypt and the Arab world ultimately led to his assassination.
