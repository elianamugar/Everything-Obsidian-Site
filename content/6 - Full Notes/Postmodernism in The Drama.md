# Postmodernism in The Drama

How did [[postmodernism]] arise? Here's the context:
>[[Post-WWII]], it emerged prominently in the 1960s and 1970s as a reaction against the order, logic, and rationality of Modernism, particularly in art and architecture.

Straight from Wikipedia:
>*Postmodernism* is a late-20th century intellectual, artistic, and cultural movement that emerged as a skeptical reaction to [[Modernism]]'s [[utopian]] "grand narrative" ideals. It emphasizes fragmentation, irony, and the belief that reality is socially constructed rather than objectively discovered, often rejecting universal truths, stability, and absolute knowledge.

This is Brittanica's key characteristics and themes of *Postmodernism*:
>**Skepticism of meta-narratives:** Rejects universal stories – such as science, religion, or progress – that claim to explain all of human experience.
>**Relativism & Subjectivity:** Argues that truth is relative and culturally specific rather than objective.
>**Irony and Playfulness:** Uses satire and a questioning tone to challenge traditional, "serious" art and philosophy.
>**Mixing Styles & High/Low Culture:** Blurs the lines between high art and pop culture (e.g., pop art, punk aesthetics).
>**Fragmentation & Intertextuality:** Emphasizes that works of art or literature are constructed from past, existing ideas rather than absolute originality.

In *The Drama* (2026), Borgli starts the film off with a flashback of Emma and Charlie's first meeting. The audience doesn't know, until it cuts to Charlie talking to his friend, Mike, about the wedding speech that he's currently working on. Throughout the film, Borgli infuses flashbacks, both imagined and real, and changes whose POV the flashback is coming from. Some are hilarious, where Charlie is walking hand in hand with adult Emma, but sometimes he imagines himself holding hands with young Emma, the potential mass shooter Emma. 

Borgli also infuses imagined scenarios playing out in Emma and Charlie's heads. When Charlie leaves the house for a moment, Emma catastrophizes that Charlie is scheming to leave her in light of the potential mass shooting news. She imagines Mike convincing Charlie to leave her, even offering to beat her up for Charlie. That was funny. Another one is when Charlie asks Emma if she had a traumatic past, in hopes that it can rectify the reasoning for Emma *almost* committing a mass shooting. In another way, Charlie tries to catastrophize Emma's past. He imagines her witnessing this traumatically gruesome car accident which probably scarred her as a young child, but Emma's voiceover during the Charlie's imagined scene describes a very tame, untraumatic past, where she even adamantly denies it wasn't traumatic.

Borgli uses most (if not all) postmodern elements. There's fragmentation in thoughts and timelines, subjective truths displayed on screen mixed with the audience's reality, a provocative tone of dark humor which makes the audience laugh at as serious a subject matter as school mass shootings, and many more which I'm sure I've failed to mention.

I loved the editing. It was interesting how Borgli played with the narrative timeline. I was geeking out while watching it. It's very... postmodern.
