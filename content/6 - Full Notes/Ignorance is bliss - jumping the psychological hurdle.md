# Ignorance is bliss - jumping the psychological hurdle

On August 6, 1945, the US dropped the atomic bomb “Little Boy” on the city of Hiroshima.[^1] In the 2000s and 2010s, “cyber-bullying” and “cancel culture” festered on the newly invented “social media.” These terms and weapons of mass destruction had not existed before. Though nuclear warfare and social media seem drastically different, they share similarities in the novel way it psychologically affected individuals. In Amber Case’s TED Talk, “We’re All Cyborgs Now,” she shares the idea of connectivity and our second-selves within the sphere of smartphones and social media. In the film _Dr. Strangelove_, people’s lives were depicted as mere statistics. In Bill Joy’s article, “Why the Future Doesn’t Need Us,” the current advancements of genetics, nanotechnology, and robots can doom humanity if we don’t consider the consequences. These sources commonly talk about how humans and feelings detach, which can lead to unforeseen consequences. Case, _Dr. Strangelove_, and Joy describe the changing nature of humans and the devolution of human empathy, suggesting it is easier to attack others due to the removal of psychological barriers.

Case, a cyborg anthropologist, believes in the benefits of smartphones and social media, but overlooks the toxic consequences of maintaining our second-selves. Case states the advancements of smartphones and social media are not just “an extension of the physical self, but an extension of the mental self,”[^2] which allows us  “to… communicate differently.” Case sees the benefits of global connectedness, but “communicat\[ing] differently”[^3] is a vague statement. It can also mean there are different ways to fight online. Nowadays, on social media, there are Twitter feuds, Facebook threats, and other social media backlash. These fights contrast yelling at someone face-to-face. People can hide behind a screen, send a message, and bicker without facing the consequences in reality because these actions only manifest in a virtual space, in which Case explains, “Whether you like it or not, you're starting to show up online, and people are interacting with your second self when you're not there.”[^4]  Social media users create an online alias, birthing a “dummy” version of themselves to spew and absorb verbal jabs. As a result, this makes it easier to attack others on social media because dummies are merely fighting other dummies. They don’t have to face the consequences because all the fighting is done behind a hidden persona—the psychological barrier of confrontation is removed. They don’t have to look into our enemy’s eyes or hear their voices. Instead, they’re reading words on a screen or watching a video, without paying any mind to the people behind the curtain. Online users become apathetic and lose our touch with humanity as they throw their second selves on the front lines of social media warfare. 

In this new type of warfare, Case also neglects how too much connectivity can lead to over-sharing and toxic social media fighting. When Case explains that the advancement of social media and smartphones “are helping us all to communicate and interact with each other,”[^5] she dismisses the idea of over-communication, which can lead to an absence of intimacy and a lack of empathy. With this lack of empathy, online communication can end up being less full of civil conversation, but rather stubborn arguments and ignorant comments. Case says that “\[smartphones and social media] are helping us to be more human, helping us connect with each other” and that “it’s still a human connection—it’s just done in a different way.”[^6] Yet, she disregards that this is not just a human connection done in a different way. It’s also human disconnection done in a different way. We become desensitized to human empathy and start to think of ourselves (and our second selves) more than the others across the screens, which makes it easier to attack online via cyber-bullying, text threats, or hacking. Case worries that “people aren’t taking time for mental reflection anymore,”[^7] which also leads to people not thinking about their online actions and consequences. Nowadays, we can send an irrational message or react emotionally within seconds. These consequences seem small, but they can apply to a broader context, especially with regards to weapons of mass destruction. If someone spontaneously launches nuclear missiles, their irrational decision can have negative consequences.

In _Dr. Strangelove_, General Jack Ripper decides to spontaneously launch nuclear missiles in the midst of the [[Cold War]] because he was fed up with the “commies” and did not consider the possible consequences. In the war room, President Muffley and other generals were discussing how to retract the nuclear missiles. After weighing the theoretical cost of lost lives, General Buck Turgidson wonders about the possibility of committing to the nuclear attack: “Now, the truth is not always a pleasant thing, but it is necessary now to make a choice, to choose between two admittedly regrettable, but nevertheless, distinguishable post-war environments: one where you got 20 million people killed, and the other where you got a 150 million people killed.”[^8] In this case, simply seeing people as numbers removes the psychological barrier of believing they’re killing individuals, each with their own personal history and backgrounds. It’s easier to say that they will kill “20 million people” rather than “20 million men, women, and children.” These high death tolls are consequences that must be considered in advancements of genetics, nanotechnology, and robots.

The [[Cold War]] race to develop nuclear, biological, and chemical weapons parallels the modern race of genetics, nanotechnology, and robots which will have “new classes of accidents and abuses.”[^9] Technologist and futurist Bill Joy says, “Knowledge alone will enable the use of \[genetics, nanotechnology, and robots].”[^10] If knowledge is the key to destruction, then wars can be fought from afar. Acts of war will become more logical, rather than emotional, and there will be a greater distance between enemies. The intimacy between enemies is becoming less, and along with that is the loss of empathy. After all, launching weapons from afar is much easier than shooting an enemy at close range. Pulling the trigger will no longer require a lot of emotional willpower, instead, someone can press a button halfway across the world and kill masses without ever seeing death. In terms of impacts, Joy advises: “Failing to understand the consequences of our inventions… seems to be a common fault…”[^11] Actions have consequences, and with inventions like modern drone technology, we have the power to kill targets from a distance. This is similar to when [[Hitler]] switched from ordering his soldiers to shoot Jews in mass numbers to killing Jews in gas chambers.[^12] In both instances, the higher-ups realize that by removing the human-to-human, or enemy-to-enemy connection, it makes it easier for human soldiers to kill “enemies.” Without thinking about the influence of systems, or questioning the chain of command, we can blindly commit gruesome acts.

People are capable of attacking with the knowledge of genetics, nanotechnology, and robots. In nuclear warfare, people can attack with missiles and atomic bombs. With social media, people can attack with words, videos, and other virtual means. All of these warfares eliminate the psychological barriers to attacking the “enemy” and alienate the lives of individuals. The “enemy” is not just an enemy—they’re also humans. But if the system embeds the idea that the enemy is simply evil, a statistic, or a stereotype, people can fall into the plot of _Ender’s Game_, a 1985 science-fiction novel written by Orson Scott Card. Ender Wiggin is a child commanded to kill an alien species and the adults tell him that the alien species is the enemy. Only after wiping out their species, does Wiggin realize the aliens are like the human race—they’re just trying to survive: “In the moment when I truly understand my enemy, understand him well enough to defeat him, then in that very moment I also love him.”[^13] How can we learn to love our enemy if we have become so psychologically distant from them? If we don’t understand our enemies, it’s easier on our conscience to verbally and physically attack them. What you don’t know can’t hurt you.
