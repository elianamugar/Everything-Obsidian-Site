# Soviet war in Afghanistan

In 1978, the communist party took over in Afghanistan, which was a surprise to Brezhnev and the USSR. Afghanistan’s major religion is Islam, but the communist party in Afghanistan made radical changes against Islam. As a result, there was pushback by Islamic groups. In 1979, the Mujahideen, an Islamic group (Holy Warriors), declared a Jihad (Holy War) against communism. This meant that it was a war of all Muslims versus communism. The Mujahideen gets support from Iran/Khomeini, who is a very conservative Islamic leader. Brezhnev begins to involve the USSR in Afghanistan, even though military advisors suggest to brezhnev that it’s not a great idea due to the guerrilla war-like tactics that are used in Afghanistan. The USSR wasn’t prepared to fight guerrilla wars compared to the conventional ones with Europe and NATO. There were many issues that the advisors brought up: the Mujahideen would hide in local populations, use hit-and-run tactics, use the mountains to their advantage. Most importantly, the advisors were aware of the US failures in Vietnam with going into a country, winning small battles, but never being able to defeat the overwhelming power of the Mujahideen. Regardless of the advice, in December 1979, Brezhnev sent the Red Army to Afghanistan. The Mujahideen declares Jihad against the USSR and the communist party in Afghanistan, asking assistance from the Muslim world. Carter viewed this as an aggressive Soviet act, especially with concerns of the Persian Gulf region (the West’s oil). Carter says that [[détente]] is over--Carter withdraws the Salt II treaty, cuts agricultural trade with the USSR, and ultimately worsens USSR-US trade relations. Into the 1980s, the USSR wasn't doing well because of Afghanistan--they can’t defeat the Mujahideen. The USSR soldiers don’t want to be there and there was an opium addiction growing, spreading into the USSR. This blew up other issues--the economy was doing terrible, the black market was poisoning the economy, and opium trade was poisoning Soviet lives. In May 1988, Gorbachev, the new USSR leader, announced that they would leave Afghanistan and that they’ve lost. Gorbachev wouldn’t support the communist party in Afghanistan. It was devastating--the USSR spent too much money and drug problems have risen in the USSR. In February 1989, the last USSR troops leave Afghanistan, signaling a possible ending of the Cold War.
