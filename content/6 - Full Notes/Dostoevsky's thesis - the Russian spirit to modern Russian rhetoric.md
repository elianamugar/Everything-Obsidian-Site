# Dostoevsky's Thesis - the Russian Spirit to Modern Russian Rhetoric

Ippolit Kirillovitch, the prosecutor against Dmitri, makes a striking quote: "While \[Ivan and Alyosha] seem to stand for 'Europeanism' and 'the principles of the people \[respectively],' \[Dmitri] seems to represent Russia *as she is*. ([[The Brothers Karamazov (1880) by Fyodor Dostoevsky#632]])"

In short, the thesis of Dostoevsky's book comes at the end. Alyosha represents the principles of the people, Ivan represents European ideals, and Dmitri represents Russian ideals. In this book, you can see the rooted sentiments in which modern Russia (ruled by Vladimir Putin) have drawn from. I've heard that these same sentiments used to justify Russia's rhetoric appear in Tolstoy's *War and Peace*. Dostoevsky makes a point in Kirillovitch's speech of Russian superiority over their European counterpart and admits to Russia's downfalls at the moment. At that point in time, when Dostoevsky wrote the book, Russia was still developing, but he truly believed Russia had a chance to trump the rest of Europe, and possibly even the world. With the banishment of serfdom being fairly recent at the time of writing the book, the roots of [[Communism]] and the ideal Russia were planted in this book (which makes sense, considering *The Communist Manifesto* was published in 1848).

In The Brothers Karamazov, we can see sprinkles of modern Russian rhetoric in Russian superiority.
