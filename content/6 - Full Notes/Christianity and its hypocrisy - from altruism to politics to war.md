# Christianity and its hypocrisy - from altruism to politics to war

The Christian Bible highlights the need for compassion and submissiveness, teaching the “Golden Rule” of treating others how one would like to be treated. In the New Testament, 22 Matthew 39 advises: “You shall love your neighbor as yourself”[^1] and 6 Luke 29 advises: “To one who strikes you on the cheek, offer the other also….”[^2] Though the roots of Christianity were moral and pious, some exploited its vulnerabilities and molded the religion into a tool for influence and control. People were dependent on Christianity as a way of life, and it was taken advantage of. As a result, Christianity’s passive nature allowed some to manipulate followers of the faith by turning the religion’s rhetoric into a form of persuasion. Christianity began as a religion with an altruistic mission to improve upon the world for the common good, but it changed, becoming a problematic tool for politics and war—it disrupted livelihoods, pitted identities against one another, and affected nations’ goals towards resolve and unity.

In its purest form, Christianity was a faith that focused on salvation, selflessness, and goodwill. During the Apolistic Period of 30-60AD, the idea of Christianity slowly spread and was seen as a threat to the Roman Empire. One aspect that people converted was the secrecy and extent it was considered treacherous.[^3] If it was considered dangerous to worship Christianity, then perhaps people realized that there was some truth in the faith that made the empire fearful. Because it was an exclusive faith based on missionary work, Christianity triumphed. Eventually, it drew in enough converts, growing considerable influence over the people, and in 313 AD, Emperor [[Constantine]] legalized Christianity. This set up the Church as a religious power that also played a role in the otherwise secular, non-religious, politics. A driving force in those politics was money. As a religious power, the Church emphasized what [[E.K. Hunt]], an economics professor, wrote in “The Christian Paternalist Ethic”: “The wealthy were given the responsibility of administering their wealth, on God’s behalf, to alleviate the suffering and promote the general welfare of their brothers.”[^4] The root of the faith showed its initial altruism, advertising salvation because of the idea of “general welfare,” an idea that strove for the benefit of people. People serviced God by uplifting others and gave up possessions for everyone to live a decent mortal life because of their common goal to live in eternal life. 

After the fall of Rome in 410 AD, St. [[Augustine]], a theologian of the early Christian church, argued in _The City of God_ that either humanity should live on earth, securing damnation, or live for eternal life in heaven. Saint Augustine pleaded to live their lives morally and to work diligently for God in a mission to “save” as many people from mortality’s doom.[^5] The collapse of Rome displayed a newfound, unfathomable chaos for those living during its time and Saint Augustine’s rhetoric addressed the vulnerable. His logic pertained to the idea that if “earthly cities” fall, then living in an eternal one is the safe haven—his most powerful ideological argument. As Saint Augustine explored the lack of belief in Christianity to be the reason as to why Rome fell, the connection between faith and reason became an investigative topic, which led into the realm of using Christianity as a way to justify political moves.

In 1215, King [[John I]] signed the [[Magna Carta]], which was originally written for the elite and powerful, institutionalizing the feudal society and its hierarchy. The document claims itself as a God-granted one that places emphasis on the protection of nobility’s places in society and introduces the concept of “natural rights.”[^6] In the closing of the document, it says, “... men… hold all the aforesaid liberties, rights and concessions,... free and quietly, fully and wholly, for themselves and their heirs.”[^7] Although the term “natural rights” had not yet come to fruition, the concept began to form in these words. The problem that guarding “natural rights” by God introduces is the unchecked and unquestioned power of nobility, especially since this concept was reserved and outlined for them in the Magna Carta, a God-granted document. Despite the Magna Carta stating that “these conditions… shall be kept in good faith and without evil intent,”[^8] This idea of “good” and “evil” within laws has no definition and becomes a subject explored by scholars.

During the Medieval period, when Christian education provided scholars texts that served as subject matters for philosophical reflection, Saint [[Thomas Aquinas]], a Dominican friar and Catholic priest, explored the reason for the existence of laws.[^9] Aquinas wrote, “A law must be some function of reason… followed in the attainment of happiness…. Since the name of _law_ denotes something bearing upon the general good.”[^10] Aquinas declared laws to be for “good,” but does not define the vague term of the “general good,” which is an idea that future rulers bend for their own affairs. Yet, in regards to the power of man-made laws, Aquinas mentioned, “...the framing of a law either belongs to the whole people, or belongs to a public personage who has care of the whole people.”[^11] By this logic, Aquinas allowed rulers to determine the system of rules society should follow, as long as it is in “good faith” and under God’s rule. This solidifies the unchecked power of rulers because no one can question the proxy placed by God and introduces the problem of the combination of Church and state.

Because there was no separation of Church and state, politics and religion intertwined, skewing the motivations for war and violence. One of the earliest events that introduced the influence of papal power in royalty was King [[Clovis I]]’s conversion to Christianity, motivated by  striking an alliance with the Pope and bishops of Gaul in 498.[^12] Also, during the Investiture Controversy, Holy Roman Emperor [[Henry IV]] defied the papacy, leading to his excommunication by Pope Gregory VII. After Henry IV’s penance, the pope temporarily allowed him back into the Church, only to be excommunicated again.[^13] These events demonstrated the strong influence of the Church because kings relied on the papacy to validate their leadership, displaying partial submissiveness towards the Church.

But the Christian influence took a darker turn when [[Charlemagne]], king of the Franks, considered his reason to conquer was to convert as many people as possible. During his military campaigns, he condemned tribal leaders and beheaded them because of their false god worshipping. His brutal policy went so far as to execute anyone who refused to be baptized, but it was successful for his campaigns. As an establishment of his own protection, Pope Leo III coronated Charlemagne as Emperor.[^14] Although Charlemagne's religiosity drove him to be ruthless, he was an effective leader. The pope asked for his protection, upholding Charlemagne's right to rule and establishing the close ties between kingship and papacy, which became an advantage for warfare. 

During the [[Crusades]], leaders used the tether between themselves and the Church to stimulate morale and noble reasonings for fighting. Emperor [[Alexius I]] asked Pope [[Urban II]] for aid to fight the Turks and, in the pope’s speech, he rallied the crowd to fight on behalf of God. The primary point was if a soldier of Christ perished, then they were guaranteed their sins’ removal.[^15] With this rhetoric, the Byzantine Empire was able to gather fighters of any social status and reputation because of the desirable consequence of the remission of their sins; if their sins are dismissed, then they would live in eternal salvation—the goal Christians strive for. Yet, the argument for the Crusades was twisted because, in the beginning formation of Christanity, the message of the faith was to be kind and compassionate towards people. But in the Crusades, there was a rift between people, separating identities and creating two sides of the battle: the righteous Christians fighting for their homeland and the Seljuk Turks stealing the Christian homeland. Though the Bible argued for Christians to be pious and passive, the fighting and killing contradicts the argument, showing the change of Christianity over time. 

The persecution of people practicing other religions displayed the hypocrisy of Christianity’s values and ideals. After Constantine legalized Christianity, all other religions were deemed inferior, with historian and professor [[Paula Fredriksen]] remarking, “The proviso was this: these pagans could not continue in their native religions.”[^16] In consequence, because Christianity was established as the superior faith, anyone who was not Christian was seen as a threat. During the Black Death, Jews were blamed for the curse of the plague and subject to torture.[^17] The deep roots of anti-Semitism stemmed from the idea that the Jews were responsible for the death of Jesus, providing the religious basis for the hatred of Jews. This hatred was expressed during the attacking and Christian conversions of Jews in the First Crusade, with one account saying, “From this cruel slaughter of the Jews a few escaped; and a few because of fear, rather than because of love of the Christian faith, were baptized.”[^18] In baptizing the Jews, in a sense this was a form of religious imperialism and conquest, imposing the Christian ideals rather than their Jewish ones. 

Unfortunately, spearheading the motivation for soldiers to fight for Christianity and carrying out revenge and glory for God was supported by Aquinas’ logic. He wrote that war must be waged by an institution and a proper authority that represents God; war cannot be for self gain and must have a higher cause; and war must be fought in advancement of peace.[^19] The ultimate purpose for Christians fighting the Crusades was to be inherently “good” in the face of “evil,” but the definition of “good” and “evil” depended on the perspective. If one was Christian, one was good, but if one was non-Christian, one was evil. Yet, the rhetoric applies to non-Christians. Muslims believed that Christianity was absurd, thinking they were “uncivilized barbarians.”[^20] Although this demonstrated the conflict between believers of different faith, denominations within Christianity also fought against each other.

During the French Wars of Religion, the Catholics and the Calvinist Protestants clashed, claiming about 3 million lives.[^21] While these wars were being fought, the bloody [[Saint Bartholomew Day’s Massacre]] occurred and led to the death of about many [[Huguenots]].[^22] This demonstrated that even different branches of Christianity disputed amongst themselves. Events escalated when these religious wars transitioned into the [[Thirty Years War]]. The [[Austria]]n [[Habsburg]] dynasty, associated with Catholicism, wanted “to assert greater authority over their hereditary lands” and many saw that as a “struggle against historical progress.” In contrast, the Hohenzollern dynasty, associated with Protestantism, were favored because they were believed to be the future.[^23] Implicitly, the war was ceaseless because of the “religious animosities” and the “fusion of church and state.”[^24] Defining the problems of religion and politics, the 30 Years’ War demonstrated that there were always different believers mastheading their own causes, attempting to impose their beliefs over others, and the dispute of how political action should be legitimized.

The involvement of Christianity over time has changed from a faith with a just cause, spreading the word of God to provide comfort for people in the mortal world to live an eternal life. In stark contrast to the past, the development of Christianity’s status changed from a faith to a tool for power and influence over people. The paradox of using Christianity for war and politics is that it creates a rift between God and the people—the Bible advised that followers should be empathetic and considerate, but leaders believed that this advice was reserved for Christians. If Christianity persists in politics, it could lead to more hypocrisy of the faith, which is unfair to Christians who believe in the faith because of its moral and compassionate ideals. Cyclically, if this hypocrisy persists, then the danger of creating a dominant Western faith could cause further conflict amongst those who do not fit under the “majority.” Ultimately, the common goal for stability and the maintenance of peace might not ever be achieved.
