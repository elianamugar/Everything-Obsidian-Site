# A 30-year difference - Blade Runner vs. Blade Runner 2049

In _Blade Runner_ (1982), Ridley Scott and Jeff Cronenweth, ASC, set out to create a big science fiction set. The vision for the film was inspired by _Citizen Kane_ (1941), which although was a black and white film, aided the neo-noir look in _Blade Runner_ by taking many noir aspects from the film. That being said, Cronenweth used lots of contrast, backlighting, neons for the signage, and Xenon spotlighting used mainly for sporting events and advertisements. Within the film, lots of smoke was used as a way of diffusion, which was harder to control but gave way to the beautiful shafts of light that Cronenweth and Scott wanted for the film.The camera work in the film is driven by slow pans and basic jibs, alongside many classic statics for close-ups, medium close-ups, and especially wide shots. The cinematography as a whole focused on lighting the vast sets and the characters that lived in the world of _Blade Runner_, which was highlighted by the choice of more conservative camera movements. Utilizing backlight, smoke, and other visual tools to enhance the futuristic atmosphere had story-driven reasons. For example, the smoke made sense for a polluted world, neon practicals made sense to use as umbrella handles, and the Xenon spotlights made sense to represent bright headlights for the constant patrolling presence of the police.

In _Blade Runner 2049_ (2017), Roger Deakins, ASC, BSC, decided to work with director Denis Villeneuve and create their own vision separate from the cinematography on _Blade Runner_. Deakins wanted to focus most of the cinematography on the characters, so the lighting and set designs would highlight the actors. For example, towards the beginning of the film, K (Ryan Gosling’s character) as well as his counterpart were to remain in silhouette, which was accomplished through the architecture of the set as well as the lighting through soft, but blown-out windows. Throughout the film, lots of bright soft lights scatter the windows and overheads, which is a little different to the darker noir look from its original counterpart which emphasized shafts of sunrays coming through windows. Beyond the silhouetting and brightly lit settings, Deakins creatively textured the sets with patterns, most notably the water reflection caustic pattern. The camera work shares similar conservative movements as the original such as slow pans, with the main difference being the inclusion of extreme close-ups, the use of zooms, and a few dolly movements. Otherwise, the static shots work for the film, especially on the wides, because the concentration is on the composition (with the aid of lighting) to frame the characters.

Though Deakins did not take direct inspiration from _Blade Runner_, he still shared Cronenweth’s vision in establishing neon-lighting since futuristic Los Angeles was not lit by any natural light any longer due to the pollution. Both _Blade Runner_ and _Blade Runner 2049_ also share the story-driven use of elemental forces such as rain, snow, and smoke pollution to aid the diffusion of light. Deakins may not have intended for the shared vision of “shafts of light” that Cronenweth envisioned since the beginning, but they were still present in _Blade Runner 2049_ since the use of spotlighting mixed with the diffusion remained from Cronenweth’s techniques in _Blade Runner_. The cinematography of both films can be compared and contrasted since they share some of the same techniques to achieve the look of their films, but to say one is an improvement on the other is not as clear-cut. _Blade Runner 2049_ had access to newer technology and a bigger budget to build bigger sets that would be more time and space efficient for lighting and camerawork. _Blade Runner_ had its own set of technical challenges to deal with since it was filmed with film rather than digital, but regardless, Cronenweth and Scott achieved the vision they were going for. They are both films with their own styles and the only difference is the improvement and advancement of technology within the timespan of producing both films.
