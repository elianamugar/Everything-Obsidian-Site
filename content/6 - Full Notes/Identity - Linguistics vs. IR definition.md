# Identity - Linguistics vs. IR definition

In my linguistics class on language, race, and gender class, the *very* first thing we learned was defining *identity*. This was important to the class as it would determine how we examine race and gender with regard to linguistics, and that all centered around the main question: what is identity?

First, we started with [[David Hume]]'s framework "Of Personal Identity" from his famous *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739) in which he proposes his central claim: identity is not static, nor a stable structure; it's dynamic based on emerging behaviors.

> For my part, when I enter most intimately into what I call myself, I always stumble on some particular perception or other, of heat, cold, light or shade, love or hatred, pain or pleasure. I can never catch myself at any time without a perception, and can never observe anything but the perception.

Hume says we “are nothing but a bundle of different perceptions, which succeed each other with an inconceivable rapidity, and are in a perpetual flux and movement.... The identity we ascribe to the mind of man is only a fictional one.” In other words, the "self" is an illusion. There is no real sense of the <u>continuity of self</u> – it's an illusion. Other traditions have also settled that identity is dynamic and the self is an illusion. In fact, it's a key pillar in [[Buddhism]].

Hume's proposal on identity became the framework for the *Analysis in Identity in Linguistic Interaction*, proposed by Mary [[Bucholtz]] and Kira [[Hall]], both prominent sociolinguists in the field. They defined 5 key principles in analyzing identity:
1. **Emergence**: identity is the product or behavior, not a cause.
	- e.g. Because they have a twang, they are Southern. As opposed to: Because they are Southern, they have a twang.
2. **Positionality**: behavior informs identity, as opposed to vice versa. The things we decide to do <u>produce</u> our identity.
	- e.g. Speaker v. listener, commander v. follower, other temporary interactional roles, nationality, ethnicity, large locale ethnographic level
3. **Indexicality**: speech gives way to social meaning and has the potential to activate a whole set of social meanings.
	- e.g. using *-ing* may show you may be formal, female, educated, northern, high class, uptight/rule-follower, grouch; while using *-in* may show you're rushed, male, lower class, southern.
4. **Relationality**: the emergence of a person's identity is relative to the identities of those they interact with.
	- e.g. if you're home by yourself, you don't have an identity – there are no identities in opposition to redefining it.
5. **Partialness**: no one is 100% of their identity at all times. There's a limit to which one can construct or control their identity.
	- identity construction can be partly deliberate/intentional and partly unconscious/shaped by larger social structures

In my notes regarding **relationality** and **partialness** I have written: "All identities are intersubjective." This brings me to international relations, where it is crucial in this field to define "identity". IR focuses on different levels of analyses of which to construct their theories (individual human agents, ). Within [[constructivism]] this idea of "identity" and "intersubjectivity" is important to their main principle that global politics are shaped by shared ideas, norms, and values rather than strictly material power and that state behavior is driven by *identity* and *social interactions*. Within constructivism, [[social constructivism]] emerges. Their main princple is that knowledge and reality are actively constructed through *social interaction*, culture, and *language*. It's clear now how [[Linguistics]] and [[IR]] are intertwined.

In IR, identity is considered "relatively stable, role-specific understandings and expectations about one's self." This is where [[Realism]] and [[Liberalism]] draw most of their analyses from. Yet, [[constructivism]] believes that identities can *change*, or be *dynamic*, much like linguistics's definition of identity. Through *intersubjectivity* (shared by other people the ideational component of IR not simply being the total of the beliefs of individuals), humans build knowledge and make sense of the world, not in isolation, but through shared social experiences, communication, and mutual interaction. Through this, identities emerge, which are acquired by interacting with or defining the self in relation to a structure composed of social relationships, shared meanings, rules, norms, practices. This sounds a lot like linguistic's **relationality**, in which the emergence of a person's identity is relative to the identities of those they interact with and very much related to linguistic's **partialness**, in which identity construction can be partly deliberate/intentional and partly unconscious/shaped by larger social structures.

Constructivism's school of thought considers one question regarding identity: how can one recognize the existence of a particular identity? One response they have is to monitor the discourse or combination of **language** and techniques employed to maintain these practices. For example, there may be **diplomatic language** emphasizing constructive engagement, balance of power, use of force, deterrence, and other IR-related ideas. At the helm of identity, there will always be language and philosophy. It's through these two fields, IR constructs theories about the global order (or lack thereof).
