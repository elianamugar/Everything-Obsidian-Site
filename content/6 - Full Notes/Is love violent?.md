# Is love violent?
I don't know why I decided to explore such a difficult topic, as there will probably not be a straightforward answer, but there was a pattern I noticed in the literature I was consuming. Before I begin to dive into the topic, with a quick Google search, here are the definitions of **love**:
>1. an intense feeling of deep affection.
>2. a great interest and pleasure in something.

And the definition of **violence**:
>1. behavior involving physical force intended to hurt, damage, or kill someone or something.

Towards the beginning of *Brief Encounter*, as Laura Jesson begins to tell her husband, Fred, about the emotional affair she was having (told as a "dream"), she says this particularly striking quote:
>Oh Fred, I've been so foolish. I've fallen in love. I'm an ordinary woman. I didn't think such **violent** things could happen to ordinary people.
>	*Brief Encounter* dir. David Lean, 1945

I was taken aback by "falling in love" being described as "violent." It was a concept I might've heard before on TikTok by Julian de Medeiros who gives philosophical lectures on love from different philosopher's standpoints. De Medeiros mentions Žižek quite often, a Slovenian Marxist philosopher and cultural theorist, and taught a lecture on Žižek's concept on "Love Without the Fall." In this concept, Žižek claims that one cannot love without the fall. "The fall" is the radical transformation, or the discombobulating experience, that occurs when you meet someone and it shatters your understanding of self. You realize that the self you thought you were before meeting the stranger (a person whole and content with life) is now the self with a missing piece – the missing piece being the stranger that has become your most intimate partner in life. And yes, if you think of "falling in love" in this way, then yes, love is violent! Falling in love is so transformative, potentially painful, and completely unpredictable. Although the behavior is not a physical one, "the fall" is a behavior intended to hurt, damage, or kill with the target being "the self."

I also think the main reason behind describing "love" as "violent" has to do with **passion**. According to a quick Google search on the meaning of "passion" it means:
>1. strong and barely controllable emotion.
>2. the suffering and death of Jesus.

Now, one definition is more pertinent to this discussion than the other, but both definitions have a negative connotation to them. This was interesting to notice, considering passion used to be a highly romanticized concept (think of all the 19th and 20th century literature about love and you can imagine a phrase of "fervent passion" appearing). When I think back to reading [[Crime and Punishment (1866) by Fyodor Dostoevsky]] (written in 1866), I remember Dostoevsky describing Raskolnikov (the main character and criminal) as passionate for his becoming an extraordinary man of action. Ultimately, Raskolnikov ends up killing a man – the consequence of his passion. And I think this is where I see the connection between love and violence – passion. It reminds me of watching [[Far From the Madding Crowd (2015) dir. Thomas Vinterberg]] (sorry I haven't read the 1874 Thomas Hardy novel) when William Boldwood kills Sgt. Troy to save Bathsheba Everdene from her marriage obligations with Sgt. Troy. Gabriel Oak, a farmer that works on Bathsheba's farm, tells Bathsheba that Boldwood will be spared his life as it was a **crime of passion**. Although the story is set in Victorian England (1860s), this phrase is still used today as legal terminology to refer to a **violent crime** (e.g. homicide) "in which the perpetrator commits the act against someone because of sudden strong impulse such as anger or jealousy rather than a premeditated crime ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime_of_passion#:~:text=A%20crime%20of%20passion%20(French%3A%20crime%20passionnel)%2C%20in%20popular%20usage%2C%20refers%20to%20a%20violent%20crime%2C%20especially%20homicide%2C%20in%20which%20the%20perpetrator%20commits%20the%20act%20against%20someone%20because%20of%20sudden%20strong%20impulse%20such%20as%20anger%20or%20jealousy%20rather%20than%20as%20a%20premeditated%20crime.%5B1%5D))." The consistent theme of "strong impulse" and being so overcome with emotion that your action is uncontrollable connects the themes of love, violence, and passion.

For funsies, here are some other quotes from Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice written in 1813 involving "love" and "violence":
> The happiness which this reply produced was such as he had never felt before; and he expressed himself on the occasion as sensibly and as warmly as a man **violently in love** can be supposed to do. ([[Pride and Prejudice (1813) by Jane Austen#354]])

>Had it been your uncle's doing, I must and would have paid him; but **these violent young lovers** carry everything their own way. ([[Pride and Prejudice (1813) by Jane Austen#364-5]])

But to tie it all together, I think the Hot Priest's sermon from *Fleabag* (2016) really nails the entire theme of love, passion, and violence:
>*Love is awful. It’s awful. It’s painful. It’s frightening. It makes you doubt yourself, judge yourself, distance yourself from the other people in your life. It makes you selfish. It makes you creepy, makes you obsessed with your hair, makes you cruel, makes you say and do things you never thought you would do. It’s all any of us want, and it’s hell when we get there. So no wonder it’s something we don’t want to do on our own. I was taught if we’re born with love then life is about choosing the right place to put it. People talk about that a lot, feeling right, when it feels right it’s easy. But I’m not sure that’s true. It takes strength to know what’s right. And love isn’t something that weak people do. Being a romantic takes a hell of a lot of hope. I think what they mean is, when you find somebody that you love, it feels like hope.*

Is love violent? I don't think so – at least, I think that love's end state, or rather its final stage, is not violent. I'm on the side of the people that believe love should be an overwhelming sense of calm. But from the media I've consumed, I understand how love can be violent. Maybe what they're describing isn't necessarily love at all, it might be some other thing like lust or limerence or some in-between state before love (maybe the fall?), and they're mistaking that for love. What I do believe, however, is that on the other side of "the fall," must be that overwhelming sense of calm. In other words, love is the thing that is certain *after* the violence, *after* the fall. Perhaps the saying should be *the calm **after** the storm*, but for certain, the saying is never *the calm **during** the storm*. Love is realizing the truth of the person is not *through* the violence/fall, but what happens *after* the violence/fall.
