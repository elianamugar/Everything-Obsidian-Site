# Mortality and Loneliness in Lonesome Dove

In reading *Lonesome Dove*, there is this idea that pops into my mind: *the futility of mortality*. It's a concept that refers to the undeniable, biological reality that all human attempts to defer death indefinitely are ultimately doomed to fail. There are many deaths in the book, some silly and stupid, others grave and painful, but everyone in the book knows that this is the consequence of, not just being a cowboy or living in the "Wild West", but of life.

I guess a better way to describe this is the term *[mortality salience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mortality_salience)*, which is equivalent to *the futility of mortality*. It derives from *terror management theory* in which mortality salience causes existential anxiety which may be buffered by one's cultural worldview and/or a sense of self-esteem. With the abundance of characters in the book, some are desensitized to death, many still fear death, and others are indifferent to death. This idea of mortality salience causing existential anxiety leads to a big theme of the book: [[Loneliness]]. Beyond the title of the book being *Lonesome Dove*, it is also the name of the start of their journey, the south border of Texas. The name also fits the symbolism of many characters' emotional states with lots of these cowboys being emotionally estranged from one another. But some want to belong to a community, most notably Newt, the youngest member of the crew with a dead mother and unknown father (who we later learn is Captain Call).

There's an interesting discussion I'd like to dissect between Captain Woodrow Call and Captain Gus McCrae:

> "I think we ought to have listened to our cook," Augustus said. "It's looking droughty to me."
  "If we can make Powder River I guess we'll be all right," Call said.
  "What if Jake lied to us?" Augustus said. "What if Montana ain't the paradise he said it was? We'll have come a hell of a way for nothing."
  "I want to see it," Call said. "We'll be the first to graze cattle on it. Don't that interest you?"
  "Not much," Augustus said. "I've watched these goddamn cattle graze all I want to."

At first glance, it seems like Gus is just complaining about the land of Montana. But Gus line investigates whether there's a deeper meaning to Montana: **What if Montana ain't the paradise he said it was?** Montana being considered as this "paradise" or this "escape" from where they were can also be a metaphor for heaven, which is where Gus ends up dying. Call, taking Gus's question at face-value, responds: **I want to see it. Don't that interest you?** Call gets to see Montana, or paradise/heaven, but at the cost of Gus's life, which is funny because Gus responds to Call saying, **Not much. I've watched these goddamn cattle graze all I want to.** And so Gus dies. He's seen it and then he dies because there's not much for him there. That's where his journey ends. Call, on the other hand, deals with staying alive and having to fulfill his promise to Gus that he'll deliver his coffin to the start of their journey, to Lonesome Dove. And so Call goes through an almost Sisyphean journey, suffering along the way, kind of in his own hell of people pestering about his journey (and, on a deeper level, his purpose for living). Call gets fed up with it, which reminds me of the famous line from [[Sartre]]'s *No Exit*: "Hell is other people." I think this quote gets frequently misunderstood, so to add a clarification, Sarah Bakewell explains the quote very well in her book *At the Existentialist Cafe*:

>Episodes of competitive gazing recur throughout Sartre's fiction and biographies, as well as in his philosophy. In his journalism, he recalled the unpleasantness after 1940 of feeling oneself looked at as a member of a defeated people. In 1944, he wrote a whole play about it: Huis clos, translated as No Exit. It depicts three people trapped together in a room: a military deserter accused of cowardice, a cruel lesbian, and a flirtatious gold-digger. Each looks judgementally at at least one of the others, and each longs to escape their companions' pitiless eyes. But they cannot do so, for they are dead and in hell. As the play's much-quoted and frequently misunderstood final line has it: "Hell is other people." **Sartre later explained that he did not mean to say that other people were hellish in general. He meant that after death we become frozen in their view, unable any longer to fend off their interpretation. In life, we can still do something to manage the impression we make; in death, this freedom goes and we are left entombed in other people's memories and perceptions**.'

This beautiful metaphor of heaven and paradise being Montana and living in the harshness of the frontier being hell is a masterclass in symbolism.
