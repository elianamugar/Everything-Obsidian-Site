# Tremendous Trajan

In 103 CE, an unknown artist sculpted Emperor Trajan, one of the Five Good Emperors. During Trajan’s reign, he became well known for his “Trajan Column,” an art piece that memorialized his military feats in his Dacian Wars. The particular military sculpture of Trajan that I examined at the Harvard Art Museum was made of Pentelic marble with a contrapposto stance. The whole piece is idealized, from head to toe, with no apparently strong emotion because it is meant to establish Trajan’s superiority in both strength and military preparedness. In order to display the traits of Trajan, the artist laboriously included specific muscle details, most apparent in the legs. Ideally, seeing the whole figural sculpture with its arms and full facial structure would add to the pieces of the puzzle, but from what is left, a great amount of detail was sculpted into the marble. The prominent details of the chest piece and cloth around his shoulders give clues as to who he was in the context of the army. Based on those pieces alone, observers can properly assume that he had high authority, indeed the highest authority there was available. As for the posture of the piece, it can be observed that Trajan is looking up, never looking down, demonstrating his pride and confidence. 

The most similar sculpture to the one of Emperor Trajan is the “Portrait of Augustus Caesar” by an unknown Roman sculptor in 20 BC. Both sculptures are in military dresses and in the contrapposto pose, but the difference is that the sculpture of Augustus Caesar has an obviously raised arm. It could be speculated that the sculpture of Emperor Trajan may have a raised arm, but from what was presented, it’s difficult to tell. Perhaps the preservation for the “Portrait of Augustus Caesar” may have been better, therefore revealing more details than the sculpture of Emperor Trajan. But, both sculptures are similar to each other, which is surprising considering that they were sculpted about 100 years apart from each other.

In observing the sculpture, I couldn’t help but be amazed by the feat of creating a figural sculpture. I noticed the intricate details embedded in the armor and to think that artists in the 100s could create human-like sculptures is awe-inspiring. I knew that the Romans’ culture heavily revolved around their mythology, so the inscription of the battling griffins immediately caught my attention. Reflecting on my visit, the most impressive detail to me are the folds found on the cloth and military dress. The paludamentum, or the cloak, has very realistic folds imitating how it would look in real life, and sculpting that out of marble still amazes me. I wished that the head were preserved over time because I would have liked to see how much detail they put into the face. Overall, I genuinely appreciate the sculpture considering how early in history it was created because it is difficult to imagine how revolutionary and creative the process of sculpting an idealistic human-like figure is. But when I begin to, it simply leaves me speechless.
