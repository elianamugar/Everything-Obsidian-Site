# Mill and Marx on human nature

Mill believes there is a human nature, and that all humans are inherently born selfish according to individual psychological [[hedonism]]. Because of this inherent selfishness, Mill proposes that internal sanctions are the best way to motivate individuals. If we change from within, then we can change the social, political, and economic conditions. Also, this selfishness for pleasure leads to the idea that quality matters as much as quantity, since some pleasures are “higher” than others. Marx, on the other hand, does not believe in human nature. He believes that the social, political, and economic conditions (ultimately, the conditions of living) determine an individual. As a result, Marx wants to change the social, political, and economic conditions in order to change people’s existence.

This difference is more notable in Marx’s retort to capitalism. Since he doesn’t believe in human nature, but instead the “conditions of living,” he proposes the removal of capitalism (for the most part) altogether. If you remove capitalism, then you remove greed and corruptness. As a result, people will contribute to general welfare, since there is no possibility of the existence of greed and corruptness. There is no existence of selfishness. Since Mill believes in the idea of internal sanctions, we would have to motivate ourselves in order to affect the outside world with good intentions. Then, we can be motivated to contribute to the general social welfare instead of private welfare. This idea works for Mill because he _does_ believe in human nature’s inherent selfishness. This selfishness equates to the individual’s search for pleasure. If we change our pleasure to contributing to social welfare, then we will be satisfied with contributing to social welfare which will also affect private welfare.
