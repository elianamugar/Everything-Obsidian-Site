# Notes
- Looks at EU’s GDPR as the gold standard of cyber-laws
- Question: “whose rights are curbed, to what degree, and how does a society decide which side to favor?”
- Focus: “treatment of data modification techniques **(anonymization and pseudonymization)** under the law and their ability to intensify surveillance risks.”
	- “...these techniques represent a new form of surveillance that has less to do with a centralized state-led panopticon targeting individuals as private citizens and more concerned with promoting various data marketplaces engaged in the trade of both behavioral data and its associated predictive algorithms and products.”

- Big data
	- “The binding of advanced predictive tools with large data sets on human activity, with the aim of tracking, monitoring, analyzing, and disseminating this information.”
		- Main sources of big data
			- Organizational records: economic transactions, contracts, registrations w/companies or gov’t institutions such as births, deaths, marriages
			- Accumulation through website: e-commerce activity, exchanges on social media platforms, browser searches usage
			- Physical movement of people collected through private and public surveillance cams, smartphones, satellites, sensors
- Big data risks
	- Production and consumption of Big Data produces new legal and ethical risks
	- “Privacy work primarily deals with the impact of Big Data practices on individual rights, including the extraction of data, the use of disclosures, and consent, access, and privileges of users.”
	- **Privacy risk:** focuses on the adverse effects of Big Data practices on rights of individuals as private citizens
	- **Surveillance risk:** relates to how Big Data is used by the state and companies to monitor and control individuals as participants in politics and markets
- Privacy risk
	- **Privacy:** “a claim, entitlement, or right of an individual to determine what information about themself may be communicated to others.”
	- “...prioritized a concern for individual rights in relation to threats from ‘outside,’ often distant, powerful interest groups such as the state or companies.”
		- “Ontological preference for individual rights… a particular blind spot… considering the right to organize and the rights of nations to self-determination.”
	- Social contract theory → proper understanding of privacy requires acknowledgements on rights **and** **_norms_**
	- **Norms:** important contextual factors, outside legal environments, broader political and social discourses
	- “Martin (2016a, p. 51, b) argues that the current legal emphasis on disclosures of terms and conditions associated with data use has paradoxically freed firms ‘to implement questionable privacy practices as long as the practices are accurately reported.’”
	- “disclosure statements are often unidirectional, giving users little option to change or influence the actual practices of companies”
	- Solutions?
		- “recent research has explored the efficacy and utilization of techniques like anonymization to alleviate privacy risk.”
		- **Anonymization and pseudonymization:** “These techniques work by disconnecting or ’de-identifying’ a data subject from a data set” (**these are the GDPR’s techniques)**
- Surveillance risk
	- **Surveillance capitalism:** “is not a centralized state-controlled program that observes individual subjects but rather a market place, wherein behavioral data and ‘prediction products that anticipate what you will do now, soon, and later’ are exchanged” - Zuboff _Age of Surveillance Capitalism_
	- **Zuboff’s ethical issue is mainly with “behavior modifications” these commodities promise, as opposed to privacy**
	- Businesses can have better access to and influence over their consumers
	- Governmental surveillance can be justified for national security or public safety, positing a “nothing to hide” argument, but evidence shows these need to be targeted to all citizens, not just some
- Positives
	- “There is little doubt that the use of Big Data can have positive outcomes in social policy targeting criminal behavior, in optimizing medical and other health care delivery, and in addressing inequality and poverty.”
	- “Whelan argues that platforms like Uber work, not because of their ability to foster trust between strangers…, but because of an underlying knowledge that their actions are observed.”
- Negatives
	- 91% of US adults believe they’ve lost control over their personal data
		- “...under US law, companies do not need consent to collect personal data nor do they have to share it with these users on request (The Electronic Communications Privacy Act, 18 U.S.C. §2510).”
- Data protection trade-off: privacy risk vs. surveillance risks
	- **Privacy:** rights of individuals to have control over visibility of personal information, **but also rights to our physical bodies, our emotions, personal relationships, our political views, and our choices**
	- Surveillance risks emerge b/c algos and analytics can be mobilized by large companies and gov’ts to place individuals + groups in time and space in order to understand the past and predict future behaviors
- GDPR background
	- Contrast to US: EU “more successful in instituting restrictions on data collection and analysis”
	- Purpose: “to give citizens and residents of the EU/EEA control over their personal data, and to simplify the regulatory environment for int’l business by fully harmonizing the nat’l data laws of its member states
	- Purpose: “\[to create] a consistent, comprehensive approach to both privacy violations and mass surveillance for EU citizens”
	- Goal: “to restrict the ‘availability of large datasets and sophisticated analytics tools used to examine these datasets’ by larger corporations who have a ‘dominant position’ in Big Data market.”
	- Motive: “to address the rising power of these dominant Big Data players and address the ‘economic imbalance between \[these companies] on one hand and consumers on the other’.”
	- Criticism: GDPR is “a stifling force for innovation and technological development due to its extensive requirements in the processing of personal information by _data controllers_ and _data processors_.”
	- The GDPR “represents a significant intensification of the legal environment in that it is binding for all member states, and includes the rights to erasure and access; requirements for affirmative consent; and the ability to levy heavy fines.”
- Legal compliance options - identified data sets (when the user is identified)
	- Data controllers (who by default wish to collect + process personal/identified data sets) must follow 4 principles
		- 2 for data collection (data minimization and special categories)
		- 2 for data processing (purpose specification and automated decisions)
	- Data collection - data minimization
		- GDPR requirement: “collection of data sets is ‘limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which \[it is] processed’.”
		- “...prevents data controllers from stockpiling additional ‘just-in-case’ information, extraneous to a specific purpose, in the anticipation that this information may be of value in the future.”
		- “...significantly reduces the value of personal (identifiable) datasets for data brokers as they are less transferable to businesses that have other data processing purposes.”
		- “...limit\[s] the scope of use and free exchange of all data collected by controllers.”
	- Data collection - special categories
		- **Special categories:** more sensitive data
		- GDPR requirement: “‘prohibits’ the processing of what it deems to be sensitive data which ‘reveals racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, trade-union membership, and the processing ofdata concerning health or sex life’ except for in a narrow range of highly controlled circumstances that involve explicity consent, reasons of substantial public interest or public safet, or for special medical treatment.”
	- Data processing - purpose specification
		- Goal: “provide subjects with more control over the movement of their personal information”
		- Definition: “personal data can only be collected for a ‘specific, explicit and legitimate’ purpose and not further ‘processed’ in ways that are not ‘compatible’ with the original purpose
		- Goal: “ensure a temporal limitation to the use of data, so that ‘the period for which the personal data is stored, is limited to a strict minimum’ and that, ‘time limits should be established by the controller for erasure or for a periodic review’
		- **Special purpose principle:** “requires firms to ‘inform their data subjects of the future forms of processing they will engage in (which must still be legitimate by nature) and closely monitor their practices to assure they do not exceed the permitted realm of analyses.’”
		- “...prevents data controllers from expanding their operations and selling the information for any incompatible use.”
		- “...the potential lifespan and proliferation of the data for subsequent surveillance by companies and government agencies are somewhat limited.”
		- “Any variation to the stated purpose, which may include the intention to sell data, **requires additional consent processes that must include an opportunity to opt-out.**”
		- Goal: “to curtail scope drift or ‘inhibit “mission creep”’, which could otherwise give rise to the usage of the available personal data beyond the purposes for which they were initially collected.’”
		- “...limit\[s] the scope of use and free exchange of all data collected by controllers.”
	- Data processing - automated decisions
		- GDPR requirement: “cautions against any unacknowledged use of _automated decisions_ in the processing of data” by “requir\[ing] companies \[to] obtain explicit consent from individuals if they wish to use these techniques.”
		- GDPR requirement: “organizations must provide ‘meaningful information about the logic involved, as well as the significance and the envisaged consequences of such processing for the data subject’ if requested.”
	- **“the principles of** **data minimization** **and** **special categories** **enforce a new standard in data collection, which alleviates the data subject of the individual burden of having to understand, manage, and monitor his/her own data.”**
	- “GDPR further prevents short-term trading of personal data for benefits and access to services through new data rights, such as the right to be forgotten and the right to be informed.”
	- Privacy risk still present: “...allows companies to store personal data with a significant level of detail as long as the purpose is specified and the individual is informed.”
- Legal compliance options - de-identified data sets (when the user is anonymous/under a pseudonym)
	- “anonymization and pseudonymization techniques create different trade-offs in relation to the data protection they offer.”
	- **Anonymized data:** “‘data [is] rendered anonymous in such a way that the data subject is not or no longer identifiable’ by ‘all the means reasonable’ and that this modification must be irreversible (Directive 95/46/EC).”
		- EU doesn’t provide a standard of successful anonymization, advising “using a combination of randomization and generalization techniques in order to create stronger privacy guarantees.”
		- Law becomes redundant in anonymizing
		- GDPR: “regulation does not… concern the processing… of anonymous information”
			- “Data controllers and processors do not have to abide by the above four principles of data protection.”
		- To comply, “anonymization requires ‘careful engineering’ and ‘constant monitoring’ in order to ensure the data remain de-identified”
	- GDPR “suggests clear preference for de-identification through ‘pseudonymization.’”
	- **Pseudonymization:** “the processing of personal data in such a way that the data can no longer be attributed to a specific data subject without the use of additional information.” (Art. 4.5)
		- GDPR: pseudonymization meets “the principles of data protection by design and… default” (Rec. 78)
	- Difference between anonymization and pseudonymization is “whether the data can be re-identified”
		- “...if data are pseudonymized, the additional personal information is not destroyed, but is held separately and securely, through the use of ‘technical and organizational measures’ to ‘ensure that the personal data is not attributed to an identified or identifiable natural person’ (Art 4.5).”
	- Risk: “data can become identifiable if both the ‘personal’ and ‘additional’ information elements are brought together
	- GDPR’s response: “both the personal information and de-identified information fall within the law; however, the use of pseudonymization allows for a relaxation of the **purpose specification principle**.
		- Article 6(4): “permits the processing of pseudonymized data for uses _beyond the purpose for which the data were originally collected_.”
		- “Freedom to sell de-identified for similarly general (unrestricted) use… to ‘create incentives’ for controllers and processors ‘to apply pseudonymization.’”
		- GDPR requirements: “gather permissions and consent from data subjects, as well as the need to provide guarantees and disclosures of data policies” to pseudonymization
	- Relaxation of **purpose specification principle** (under pseudonymization) means that **“pseudonymization is an attractive option for controllers, allowing for regulatory compliance with the GDPR, while permitting an expansion of data collection and the subsequent uses of this data.”**
		- “Pseudonymization might prove to be the best option for those data controllers who, after assessing both their interests and those of their data subjects, need greater regulatory flexibility while still controlling and storing personal information.”
- “...the law lacks a vital understanding of contemporary big data practices and that improvements in terms of privacy also bring underlying and systematic expansion of surveillance risk.”
- De-identification techniques in the age of surveillance capitalism
	- Zuboff: data capitalism is “driven to acquire ever more predictive sources of behavioral data on our choices, activities, behaviors, personalities, and emotions.” (Big Data Gold Rush)
	- Zuboff: “the system of ‘surveillance capitalism’ does not operate as a centralized panopticon that identifies and targets individuals, but as a decentralized marketplace that exchanges both behavioral data (as a raw commodity), and its associated predictive models to analyze this data.”
	- “...behavioral data is treated as a free commodity--a _res nullius_-- largely ungoverned by legal frameworks.”; “...behavioral data is an object with no owner.”
		- “Here data subjects, anonymized or otherwise, are afforded no ownership rights of their own behavioral data.”
		- Zuboff’s analogy of elephants in the Ivory Trade: mammals weren’t the product, but the abandoned carcass; the product on the other hand has been ripped from \[our] lives.
		- “This decoupling of behavioral data from its subjects is a new technological form of alienation.”
	- Zuboff’s concern: those that seek “to enhance social control” and to “automate us” through “complex and comprehensive means of behavior modification”
	- “The findings of Kramer et al. (2014) no doubt reiterate concerns surrounding this powerful combination of technologies to influence the preferences and emotions of consumers and voters (Lyon 2003, p. 14, 2014). The potential for reconstructing the relationship between individuals, companies, and states at a fundamental level is massive here, and is what Lyon (2014, 2015) refers to as “anticipatory governance,” where Big Data is used to both measure and influence popular thinking around matters of public concern.”
- Recommendations
	- “Proper legal consideration of the property rights surrounding the ownership of **behavioral data**; what we have referred to as an object with no owner (_res nullius_)
		- “...consideration may challenge the assumption of the GDPR that techniques such as anonymization and pseudonymization separate data from individuals.”
		- “In order to future proof citizens from the wider implications of data collection and trade, we recommend the regulators widen their net.”
			- Issue: “...the growing market for pseudonymized behavioral data remains outside the purview of the current law.”
			- Issue: “...broader context in which behavioral data operates, complete with its capabilities to make accurate predictions and inferences about people.”
			- Requirement: “...a greater philosophical engagement with not only what behavioral data _is_, but also the deeper question as to whether an individual’s behaviors, actions, and emotions can be considered their own property, and if so, whether the recording, measuring, or trading of such phenomena is an infringement of individual rights.”
		- “Our recommendation is not necessarily wedded to the establishment of new property rights over _res nullius_ data but may encompass alternative approaches, such as a stakeholder perspective that would ask the question: ‘if Big Data can predict and modify the activities of people with great accuracy then should it not stand to reason these same individuals have a stake in its function?’”
	- Specific: “For the GDPR to be robust, data that are collected with the intention to be anonymized should still be subject to the consent and agreement of the individual.”
		- Pseudonymization requires consent process, while the current law doesn’t require anonymized data under consent process
		- “...it is important to ensure all individuals know if their data can be traded, even if the data are completely anonymized, and to grant them the right not to make their anonymous data available for this purpose.”
- Conclusion
	- “...this emphasis placed on **individual privacy** does not provide sufficient protections to citizens against the surveillance risks associated with the collection, analysis, and the trade of their **behavioral data**.”
	- “...the way the law has been constructed has created space for a behavioral futures market to emerge with very few protections for individuals.”
	- “...the law must also protect increasingly visible behavioral aspects of people as their political, social, and economic choices constitute the future.”
	- The reach of the GDPR “will be limited if lawmakers fail to understand the broader behavioral data ecosystems they seek to regulate.”
	- “...at present, the GDPR’s effort to codify data ethics, particularly in relation to behavioral data, is limited because its derogations create a passage through which companies are able to escape the law’s restrictions.”
	- “...the law creates the space for a behavioral data market in which commercial self-interest is likely to flourish.”
	- “...the GDPR will make it possible for the behavioral data market to function unencumbered.”
	- Concern: “both the nature of the data market and the trading of \[behavioral data] are open to new strategies that are being deployed to modify behavior” (Zuboff 2019)
	- Currently, “...the GDPR is likely to make data controllers more aware of their responsibilities to data subjects, but it provides little protections against the misuse of data within these markets.”
	- **“If regulators are to \[keep pace with the needs of the community and the commercial imperatives of an increasingly lucrative market] well, the surveillance implications of big data will need to be taken just as seriously as our well-founded privacy concerns.”**
