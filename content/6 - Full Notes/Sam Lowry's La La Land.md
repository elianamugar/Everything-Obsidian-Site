# Sam Lowry's La La Land

In Sam Lowry’s dream sequences, he’s depicted as a Braveheart-esque hero, wearing shining armor. Yet, in Lowry’s reality, he’s simply a bureaucratic worker. In the beginning of the movie, Lowry claims he’s content with his job, saying he doesn’t want to move up the ladder when his mother offers him a higher position. After some hallucinations and dream sequences throughout the movie, Lowry changes his mind, telling Mr. Kurtzman, “I don’t know what I want” after Mr. Kurtzman admits that he signed the documents to keep Lowry in the same position. For Lowry, the dream sequences take him out of his boring and repetitive reality of being a bureaucratic automaton. In the dream sequences, industrialization, its buildings and soulless people with baby masks are Lowry’s enemies. In a way, Lowry runs his own path in life by killing industrialization through the slaying of these enemies, deeming himself the hero of his own dreams. But in reality, he’s a worker that no one cares about.

Then, throughout the movie, Lowry starts to lose touch with reality. He loses the ability to distinguish reality and fiction. He suffers the dread of robotic work at the Ministry of Information but finds excitement through his little spurts of hallucinations appearing in his reality. His motivation to keep going in life is chasing the woman that he keeps seeing in his dreams. By going off to find the woman in his dreams, he tries to make himself not just a hero in his dreams, but the hero in his real life to find the “damsel in distress.” He’s trying to control his own destiny and take hold of it to prove to himself that the bureaucracy and his job don’t control him--but it’s all a farce. At the end, when it’s revealed that Lowry was in a dream all along, it establishes director Terry Gilliam’s point about society--that no one can escape the mechanical way of life.

In a way, the dreams do serve as a problem for Sam Lowry, but it depends from which perspective the audience perceives the “problem.” For workers, the bureaucracy wouldn’t want them to have dreams because that would show that there’s something beyond the bureaucratic lifestyle and possibly prevent them from performing with their best abilities at their jobs. Therefore, the dreams would be deemed a “problem” in the eyes of the bureaucracy. But in the eyes of the worker, it may serve as their escape from their machine-like reality and something to strive for. It may not seem like a problem to a culture that promotes dreamers, but in this dystopian society, it’s implied that those things are discouraged, especially when Mr. Kurtzmann wants to keep Lowry as his worker. Even when Lowry’s mom offers Lowry a higher up position, that’s a thing given through patronage, an already existing system that they’re locked into--it’s just moving within a rigid, mechanical system. Ultimately, although Sam Lowry was trapped in the bureaucratic system, he flees to his dreams, which was revealed at the end. He completely loses touch with reality in his “punishment” (or Lowry’s reward, it depends on the perspective) and begins his new life in his imagination within his delusional state.
