# When the smog clears

The infamous Great Smog of London occurred when industrial pollution became so severe that London was covered in a smog for days, causing thousands of deaths. As a whole, we as humans have not been good stewards of the Earth’s resources. For the sake of technological progress and efficiency, especially around the time of the Industrial Revolution, we sacrificed Earth’s resources. Though it is difficult to control a whole species, it is much easier to control consumption and environmental awareness on an individual basis. Each individual has the most responsibility and accountability for their own actions, and it is up to our own conscious minds to decide whether our actions have positive or negative consequences. Based on my consumption journal, I have generally been a good steward of helping the environment, but my efforts are not consistent. For example, I recycle, turn off the lights and water, and walk as a mode of transportation. Yet, other times I take long showers, dispose of excess food, and use plastic bags instead of reusable ones. At least, as an individual, I can control these actions and work to better the environment since I am aware of my harmful actions towards the environment. 

The [[tragedy of the commons]] is an economic problem in which every individual has benefits from consuming a resource, but at the expense of other individuals—with no limits on the number of people consuming. Essentially, if everyone acts in their own best interest, it results in harmful over-consumption. From this perspective, Hardin believes that all humans are inherently selfish and would consume the most resources at the expense of others. Though in some aspects Hardin’s point is valid, it does not necessarily make sense considering that one of the most popular doctrines that people unknowingly follow is utilitarianism. By utilitarianism, people’s actions are to ensure the greatest happiness for the greatest number of people, which directly contradicts Hardin’s argument. In short, people have a tendency to drift towards altruistic values rather than selfish ones as presented in Hardin’s argument. This makes more sense than Hardin’s tragedy of the commons, considering that a healthy and sustainable community is one which manages all its members’ current needs while ensuring adequate resources are available for future generations. Since we all want to thrive in happiness, which in ethical terms means “absence or avoidance of pain,” creating a sustainable community in which we share equal resources without harming the environment would bring us the most avoidance of pain. In other words, if we do not pollute the Earth, then our future generations will not face the displeasurable repercussions of global warming, which ultimately ensures the greatest happiness for all of humanity’s posterity. A growing population of humans can result in a growing population of environmentally friendly humans who can adapt and learn to create a sustainable Earth with our current ecosystems and resources. In turn, these environmentally conscious humans will create healthy ecosystems and resources that will be reusable in the future. Unfortunately, as presented in Peggy Shepard’s “Environmental Justice” TEDxHarlem talk, the ethical implications of “sacrifice zones” directly contradict the altruistic nature of utilitarianism especially within the context of being environmentally friendly. Certain communities, usually aimed toward those on the lower end of the economic spectrum, are the main targets for sacrifice zones. Those living in sacrifice zones do not benefit from environmental efforts. Rather, they are being undermined and cannot economically support themselves to have an environmentally healthy community. Since sacrifice zones exist in developed and developing countries, the question is whether the application of utilitarianism to environmental conservation efforts can be extended to the worldwide scale.

Because of the utilitarian notion within environmental ethics, developed countries have a duty to help developing countries avoid the same kinds of pollution problems. In the bigger scope, it is not countries helping countries—it’s humans helping other humans for the betterment of our whole species. Developed countries should determine our future goals to improve our habitat so that it can be sustainable for future generations around the world—not just on a country by country basis. It is important for politicians to environmentally regulate businesses, since they are the controlling interest groups that contribute to our high carbon pollution levels. Some climate policies would help drive those carbon pollution levels down. In an idealistic scenario, politicians could convince green technology companies to sell their products at a more affordable price, but that would not be an economically sound strategy for businesses. This is an unfortunate reminder that interest groups control a lot of our individual capabilities to be eco-friendly.

For more sustainable living, some preferable actions would be to switch to clean technology, otherwise known as green technology. Nowadays, there are electric cars and stoves which would be a better replacement than consuming gas. One easier action would be to recycle and be conscious of the products we consume on a daily basis. If enough individuals commit to be environmentally conscious and friendly, then the goal of sustainability is within reach. Because individuals make up a collective, I do believe that individual actions can make a difference. The issue lies with the interest groups that limit our accessibility to being more environmentally friendly. It’s much more difficult to buy new green technology if it is expensive and out of pocket. There are some things that are out of our individual control—we don’t have the agency to steer our economic ship from the passenger deck. Fortunately, interest groups are also a collective of individuals as—we just need to have the right individuals within these interest groups and the right politicians to guide us to be more environmentally healthy.
