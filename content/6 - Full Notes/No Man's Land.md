# No Man's Land

During the Great War, a new type of warfare emerged: trench warfare. With the [[modernization]] of technology came the rise of new weapons, including machine guns. As a result, to escape the hellfire of these rapid-fire weapons, soldiers resorted to digging trenches to escape the rain of bullets. Two notable trench warfare battles occurred—the Battle of the Somme and the Battle of Verdun. In these battles, many soldiers died with little territory gained for either side. Though, in the large scale context, these battles occurred between the British, French, Germans, and other forces, the make-up of those powers were human soldiers. From the experiences of these soldiers, the devastation revealed the psychology of the soldiers and their submission into dehumanizing thought. They lost a sense of individuality and could only see their enemies as an entity, no longer as other human beings.

Paddy Griffith, a British military historian, truthfully described trench warfare to be a symbol of “futile industrial slaughter.” In witnessing the stalemates and loss of lives with little territorial rewards, soldiers became hopeless because the deaths were not amounting to anything significant, which was the pure definition of futility. Jonathan George Symons, a British rifleman deployed in France, described this hopelessness in a letter to his friend in November 1915: “How can you expect men to live in this… only now and again do we get a shelling, but one gets used to it.” Symons remarked on the absurdity of the war and depicted the new familiarity of death, questioning how men were situated in such destruction. Symons’ experience with shelling in trench warfare sounded shockingly normal and told of the dehumanizing effects of the Great War. 

Some other soldiers remarked on this common feeling, such as Richard James, a British Corporal deployed in France, who said in August 1915, “It’s a funny sensation being under fire for the first time but it soon wears off.” Similar to Symons, James described the initial shock of soldiers when suddenly being engaged in battle and the shift to indifference. This was a common pattern among soldiers and, for many, work was a way to resume to some sort of normalcy. To fulfill a soldier’s duty, this work gave them a purpose, no matter how mechanical and dulling it may be. In hindsight, it looked like this made them feel less human, but in the chaos of trench warfare, British Private R.C.S. Frost, who was deployed in France, described it best, saying that it was simply “how soldiers can adapt themselves to circumstances” and work was a show of strength rather than weakness. As if to distract themselves from the chaotic situation at hand, soldiers focused on their work as a coping mechanism, but still felt some sense of pointlessness.

As a result of this hopelessness, the soldiers started experiencing a numbness to the deaths and killings; the effects of trench warfare became normalized. This added to the lack of empathy for the enemy, as soldiers must do during wartime in order to cope with killing human beings. This led to soldiers treating the Germans as shooting targets, removing humanism from German soldiers. British Private Gilbert Williams, in 1915, recounted his anger when he could not kill them because they were out of range and stated, “You can’t realise the power one seems to possess when handling a Maxim \[machine gun]. Personally I feel as if I could lay out the whole German army.” There was a sense of glory in being able to kill the Germans for both nationalistic and selfish reasons. On one hand, soldiers could prove themselves worthy of their country’s cause if they were able to contribute to the war by killing as many enemies as possible. On the other hand, war was like a game for some soldiers where the higher the kill count, the more medals and accolades a soldier received. The Great War and its trench warfare led to the desensitization to life and death and used the soldiers’ lives as pawns and numbers rather than individuals. A year later, Private Williams detailed the rash outlook on death through the hastened burials in the trench sides: “...now that the trenches have crumpled one is constantly seeing the bones of men’s legs or their boots, or skulls sticking out from the sides of the trenches, pleasant, eh?” By the sarcastic tone of his rhetorical question, Private Williams’ indifference to death and killings did not change within the span of a year—he was one of many soldiers desensitized to death.

Due to the desensitization of soldiers, many became apathetic to the enemy, which accustomed soldiers to killing. Some soldiers called the enemy “beastly Germans” or “Huns”which dehumanized the enemy and reestablished the “us versus them” narrative, a condemned narrative that seemed old-fashioned. Many thinkers and politicians believed that a war of this scale could not happen again because it seemed barbaric, but as Austrian physician Sigmund Freud pointed out during the outbreak of the Great War, in order for the world to be civilized “...unlike in the days of classical antiquity, the words ‘foreign’ and ‘hostile’ should no longer be synonyms.” In calling the Germans “beasts” and the more barbaric term “Huns” in reference to nomadic warriors, the ideas of “foreign” and “hostile” remained synonymous, which opposed Freud’s ideas put forth to progress to the civilized world politicians believed they were living in.During these times, it felt like the world was regressing or stagnating, rather than improving, which made people feel lost. Especially for the soldiers, their deaths were oddly casual and unreasoned.

​As the trench warfare went on, soldiers soon realized that the horror of war was not merely that people were dying in mass numbers, but that they were arbitrarily dying. Colour Sergeant Richard Gilson described this utter devastation: “It was horrible suspense, as I seemed to be the only man untouched, all around me, and being personally acquainted with each man made matters worse, in fact, it’s all wrong to call them men, as they were mostly mere boys.”This displayed the ultimate helplessness of the individual soldier by explaining how alone one can feel when they were alive while comrades were dying. The experience worsened when a soldier knew his comrades on a personal level, which could hinder a soldier’s ability to kill once they realized that everyone was human. To explain this sentiment, French psychologist Gustave le Bon stated that intellectually, the collective man appeared inferior to the individual. In relation to war, soldiers were disconnected from reality and uniform in thought, but individual thought was superior because it kept them grounded in reality. Yet, individual thought could also be a deterrent during times of war because countries needed armies and soldiers to fight, not think; governments did not want soldiers to realize they were committing gruesome acts. 

The repercussions of trench warfare during the Great War led to soldiers believing the world was enigmatic, in which Freud remarked, “Thus the civilized world-citizen of whom I spoke before may find himself helpless in a world that has grown strange to him when he sees his great fatherland disintegrated, the possessions common to mankind destroyed, and his fellow citizens divided and debased.” Indeed, the soldiers who witnessed the destruction of land could see the horror in the capability of humanity to destroy on a larger scale began to lose faith in the goodness of humanity. After all, what they were witnessing did not seem to be for the betterment of society, but rather the end of society.

From these thoughts and sentiments, the emergence of the philosophies from WWI made sense. After the war, there was a loss of faith in humanity and so there was an appealing connection to German philosopher Frederick Nietzsche’s nihilism, the belief that life was meaningless. Realism emerged to uncover the veil behind the puppet masters of the war. Those dissatisfied with the picture of the world because it left out what lies underneath the surface led to modernism. The lack of an overarching foundation for instilling meaning in life and the distrust in systems to create meaning led to postmodernism. People were vulnerable to different doctrines, ideologies, and beliefs because, after the Great War, many were scarred. People were looking for a sense of purpose and a will to keep living in such a broken world. There was trauma amongst Europe, and it would take time and patience to rebuild. But as history played out, some leaders thought war was the immediate way to give people purpose—another world war would devastate the globe.
