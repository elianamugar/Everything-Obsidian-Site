# Removing the pacifier - South Vietnam's reliance on the American teat

During the French-Indochinese War, Vietnamese leader Ho Chi Minh led the Viet Minh political organization and controlled the guerilla Vietnamese People’s Army against the French. The French lost in April 1954 and the Geneva Conference soon took place. Minh negotiated for an independent and unified Vietnam, even asking for Soviet and Chinese support, but the communist powers refused—Vietnam would be divided at the 17th parallel and left to fend for itself. Chaos ensued soon after the [[Geneva Accords]] were signed. Many Vietnamese fled between the northern and southern halves of Vietnam because of uncertainties surrounding the creation of a communist northern Vietnam. When Minh eventually established an independent communist North Vietnam by enacting a totalitarian government and communist economic system, President Dwight E. Eisenhower developed his rationale for the Domino Theory: if southern Vietnam falls to communism, then communism will flood the rest of Southeast Asia. To prevent this, the U.S. must ensure a self-governing South Vietnam with a South Vietnamese army to protect its government. George Herring, an expert foreign analyst on the Vietnam War, asserts in his article “People’s Quite Apart: Americans, South Vietnamese, and the War in Vietnam” that the U.S.’s lack of cultural awareness led to the failure in South Vietnam.

In October 1954, Eisenhower’s installation of the Ngo Dinh Diem in South Vietnam was the first American step towards developing an independent South Vietnamese government. Although Diem “met the American criterion for leadership,” he was too detached from his own people (Herring, 1-2). In other words, Diem fit the American standards to rule, but those standards did not apply well to a South Vietnamese government working towards its own independence. Rather than working for South Vietnam’s interest, Diem did not represent his people. Ironically, American-backed Diem refused to follow American instructions because he did not want to be alienated by his own people as an unfavorable American puppet. Even though he was wary of the people’s perception of his leadership, Diem disregarded his people and used his power ruthlessly—he relied on military power to protect his dictatorship and placed anti-Buddhist policies in a Buddhist majority country. The disconnect between Diem and his people, in turn, made Diem unpopular in South Vietnam. South Vietnam’s independence was failing. President John F. Kennedy and his administration attempted to remedy the situation by “\[attaching] conditions to its offer of additional economic and military assistance,” but Diem did not accept, forcing the Americans to realize there was no better alternative to Diem (Herring, 2). The paradoxical Kennedy solution was one of the first intrusions of American aid. Through American incentivization, the U.S. tried to force South Vietnam to follow American instructions. South Vietnamese independence could not be built on American reliance—this was the ultimate contradiction. As the Diem issue grew, the U.S. “resolved” it by ordering a coup on the Diem administration, which resulted in the deaths of Diem and his brother. South Vietnam’s power vacuum left the government with revolving-door leaderships which were discomforting to the U.S. (Herring, 2).

From 1965 to 1967, under the Lyndon B. Johnson presidency, the U.S. sent in more economic aid and military assistance to build up South Vietnam and repair the power vacuum problems the U.S. created. U.S. General William Westmoreland suggested using “combined command” between the American and South Vietnamese armies, which would emulate the same tactic used during the Korean War, but the South Vietnamese rejected the idea, “perceiving it as a form of neocolonialism” (Herring, 6). The U.S. was unaware of its colonial tendencies—they were not conscious about South Vietnam’s perception of the U.S. takeover. Because of the South Vietnamese army’s refusal to cooperate with a unified U.S. command, the U.S. took matters into their own hands and used American air and ground forces against North Vietnam (Herring, 6). The U.S. took over the fighting in Vietnam and undermined the South Vietnamese army. The U.S. was stubborn—they perceived South Vietnam as unintelligent and a hindrance to aggressive American strategies. The U.S. wanted quick results and forced the Vietnamese to keep up, regardless of Vietnamese input. Herring emphasized this “takeover effect” was the “Americanization” effect on the war, leaving the Vietnamese to conclude “they were fighting for the United States in a war that was America’s, not theirs” (Herring, 7). As a result of Americanization, the South Vietnamese government, army, and economy were modeled and chiseled the “American way.” Americanization did not ensure South Vietnam's capabilities to be an independent and self-sufficient nation; the U.S. was losing its grasp on its original tasks. The more the U.S. “imposed its own ways on its ally,” the more the U.S. was forming an American core within the shell of South Vietnam (Herring, 7). Subsequently, the South Vietnamese army began relying on American support, resources, and firepower to fight their wars. The goal of an independent South Vietnam seemed distant because the American-raised South Vietnam clung onto American assistance.

The bombardment of American aid disrupted the South Vietnamese economy, so the U.S. had another American “solution” for another American fault: flood South Vietnam with American consumer goods. Like previous American solutions, it created a bigger mess. South Vietnam’s economy worsened because the South Vietnamese used American aid and consumer goods to their advantage, increasing crime and corruption (Herring, 9). It was the same pattern: the U.S. failed again. The Americanized attempts to fix the economy via capitalism destroyed South Vietnam’s domestic businesses and industries. The weak country became weaker, which left South Vietnam without a sustainable economic system or army to support itself. As a result, South Vietnam’s government and army was left with one choice—rely on the U.S. economic and military aid to survive. The U.S. failed its original tasks to assist the establishment of an independent South Vietnamese government and army.

After the U.S.’s successive failures, there was one success: the Tet Offensive. This was the first display of successful cooperation—American and South Vietnamese forces banded together to defeat the offensive Vietcong and North Vietnamese Army. Although the Tet’s result was fruitful for the U.S.-South Vietnamese alliance, there was an underlying issue: America could not “discriminate between friend or foe,” which only increased friction with the South Vietnamese (Herring, 15). This lack of true allyship was not new. If anything, the Tet Offensive highlighted the pre-existing cultural insensitivity between the “allies” which underlie their previous failures. To worsen relations, the American public saw the South Vietnamese “allies” as brutal and inhumane after the street execution of a Vietcong captive by a Saigon police chief was publicized which led to the U.S. public’s disapproval of the Vietnam War (Herring, 15). With American morale at an all-time low, the American policy changed to reel back on Americanization. The Johnson administration decided to stop the bombing campaigns, but too many issues remained unresolved.

Because of the Tet’s military success, the Johnson administration was convinced South Vietnam could handle itself. In a move towards “Vietnamization,” the U.S. decided to withdraw U.S. troops while sending assistance to South Vietnam to relieve the U.S. of its heavy fighting duty. In 1968, the unpopular Vietnam War became the focal point of Nixon’s candidacy; after his election, Nixon enacted the Vietnamization policy. With the announcement of Vietnamization, the eager American public thought the U.S. began reeling troops out of Vietnam, but the U.S. “was still doing most of the fighting and paying the cost” (Herring, 16). To worsen the matter, the South Vietnamese leaders were receptive to it. This the South Vietnamese reliance on the U.S. was an effect opposite of the U.S.’s original duty to support South Vietnam’s independence. The South Vietnamese people were angered by their dependence on the U.S. and, after the Tet, tensions increased between the allies. During the peace initiatives, the South Vietnamese were worried about their susceptibility to the National Liberation Front and North Vietnam, but the U.S. disregarded the South Vietnamese concerns (Herring, 16). The U.S.’s agenda to end the war in Vietnam took precedence over South Vietnam’s faltering hope for independence.

The Nixon administration moved to quicken Vietnamization. Immediately, the U.S. was withdrawing their troops, but from the Vietnamese perspective, this was “too late and imposed too abruptly” (Herring, 17). The U.S.’s sudden abandonment led to South Vietnamese outcry—they were too dependent on the U.S. and the desertion would be too detrimental. American money, troops, military advisors, firepower, air support, equipment, and the U.S. command system became so deeply ingrained into the South Vietnamese government and warfare that the sudden cut-off from the U.S. left South Vietnam weak and defenseless. South Vietnam could not stand on its own. This failed cooperation worsened by the 1973 Paris agreements. During the agreements, the U.S.’s ultimate duty to help South Vietnam’s independence was neglected. National Security Advisor Henry Kissinger did not consult with South Vietnamese leader Nguyen Van Thieu during negotiations and the Nixon administration allowed North Vietnamese troops to remain in South Vietnam, as well as other North Vietnamese concessions. Despite Thieu’s opposition to the Nixon administration’s decisions, Nixon’s indirect threat of assassination through the subtle reminder of Diem’s death forced Thieu’s hand to sign the agreements. This was the U.S.’s ultimate betrayal towards South Vietnam—the U.S. cut deals with the enemy (Herring, 19). The U.S. completely abandoned its duty of ensuring South Vietnamese independence and left them at the mercy of North Vietnam. In the end, Saigon fell to North Vietnamese troops and both halves unified under the communist North Vietnam.

There was one glaring reason why the U.S. operation in South Vietnam failed so miserably: the cultural differences between the U.S. and South Vietnam were overlooked. The oppressive American presence and South Vietnam’s toxic reliance on the U.S. was certainly a factor in South Vietnam’s downfall, but their ignorance of cultural disparities was the underlying cause of the U.S.-South Vietnamese imbalance. The U.S. was impatient, progressive, and wasteful while South Vietnam was patient, traditional, and economical (Herring, 2 & 11-13). These differences were apparent but unaddressed throughout the Vietnam War. The U.S.’s “solutions” to South Vietnam’s problems failed because South Vietnam’s problems needed South Vietnamese solutions. Unfortunately, an unexpected tether resulted between the U.S. and South Vietnam. The U.S. cut the umbilical cord without remorse and easily disposed of its duties in South Vietnam, leaving an American void that could not be filled. From the U.S.’s failures in Vietnam, Herring concluded four lessons: do not establish a dependency system for the weaker nation, understand the relation between military and political impacts, know our allies, and be sensitive to the effects of American intervention (Herring, 22-23). The U.S. must acknowledge its powerful global influence, but also recognize they are not invincible. The U.S. cannot fight every war to stop communism—some wars are not worth fighting.
